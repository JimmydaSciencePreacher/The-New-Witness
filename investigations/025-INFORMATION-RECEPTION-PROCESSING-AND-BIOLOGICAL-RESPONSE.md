## 3. Information is not one undifferentiated scientific object

The word *information* is used in several related but distinct ways.

### 3.1 Information in communication theory

Claude Shannon's mathematical theory of communication addresses the quantitative problem of representing and transmitting messages through communication channels, including channels affected by noise.

In this setting, information is quantified through probability distributions and uncertainty. The theory does not require that the message's semantic meaning be understood by the engineer measuring transmission.

A simplified communication model distinguishes:
- a source that produces a message or signal;
- an encoder or transmitter that prepares it for transmission;
- a channel through which it passes;
- noise or interference that may alter it;
- a receiver that obtains the transmitted signal;
- a decoder or destination that reconstructs the message.

This is a model of communication, not a universal account of every kind of information or every biological process.

Reference: [Shannon information theory and its foundations in communication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5226236/).

### 3.2 Shannon entropy

For a discrete random variable (X) with possible outcomes (x_i) and probabilities (p(x_i)), Shannon entropy is:

\[
H(X)=-\sum_i p(x_i)\log_2 p(x_i)
\]

When base-2 logarithms are used, the unit is the bit.

Entropy measures uncertainty associated with a specified probability distribution. It does not, by itself, measure truth, purpose, semantic meaning, usefulness, intelligence, or biological function.

A fair coin has one bit of Shannon entropy per toss because either outcome is equally likely. A heavily biased coin has lower entropy because one outcome is more predictable.

The result depends on the random variable, the set of possible outcomes, and the probability distribution being analyzed. It is not a free-standing amount of meaning contained in an object.

Reference: [The application of information theory to biochemical signaling systems](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3820280/).

### 3.3 Mutual information

Mutual information measures how much observing one variable reduces uncertainty about another, under a specified joint probability distribution.

\[
I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)
\]

It can be used to quantify statistical dependence between a signal and a response. It does not by itself prove that the signal causes the response, that the receiver understands the signal, or that the relationship has a particular purpose.

Causation requires additional experimental design, mechanistic evidence, or justified causal analysis. Correlation and information-theoretic dependence must not be promoted automatically to causation.

### 3.4 Channel capacity and noise

A communication channel has limits on how much information can be transmitted reliably under specified assumptions. Noise can interfere with the signal. Coding and redundancy can help a receiver detect or correct errors.

In biological systems, signaling pathways operate amid molecular fluctuations and variable environmental conditions. Information theory can quantify some of the input-output relationships in such systems, but the model must be tied to actual measurements and a defensible definition of the variables.

Reference: [Quantifying information transmission in intracellular signaling](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9507437/).

## 4. Information, signal, energy, and matter: necessary distinctions

These terms often appear together, but they do not mean the same thing.

| Term | Bounded meaning in this investigation | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| **Matter** | Physical material that can be investigated by composition, structure, and properties | That every material structure is itself a message |
| **Energy** | A measurable physical quantity associated with physical states and transfers | That energy is identical to information or meaning |
| **Signal** | A physical or abstract variable that carries a detectable pattern or variation in a specified system | That every signal is understood or intentionally sent |
| **Information** | A domain-dependent concept; in Shannon theory, a quantitative measure based on probability and uncertainty | That every use includes semantic meaning, purpose, or intelligence |
| **Meaning** | Semantic or interpreted content within a language, organism, or interpretive framework | That Shannon entropy alone measures meaning |
| **Response** | A change in a system associated with an input or condition | That response proves conscious understanding |
| **Witness** | In the GUA-W governing relationship, the derivative observer or recorder of what is given | That the Witness is the Author or creates the reality observed |

A signal is often physically embodied in a changing state of matter or energy. A radio signal, for example, is an electromagnetic wave with measurable physical properties. A nerve impulse involves electrical and chemical processes in living tissue. A DNA sequence is a material sequence of nucleotides.

The information-theoretic analysis of these cases depends on which variables are measured and how possible states are defined. The physical carrier and the information measure are related, but they are not identical descriptions.

## 5. DNA: sequence, expression, and biological function

DNA is a molecule composed of nucleotide units. In the familiar DNA alphabet, the bases are adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), and thymine (T). The order of bases in a DNA sequence can carry hereditary information used by cells.

### 5.1 Sequence and genetic information

The sequence of nucleotides is not interchangeable with the whole organism. It is one material component of a larger biological system.

For protein-coding genes, sequences in DNA are transcribed into RNA, and the coding information in messenger RNA is translated into an amino-acid sequence. Proteins then fold and interact within cellular environments. Other genes produce functional RNA molecules rather than proteins.

Reference: [Molecular Biology of the Cell — How Cells Read the Genome: From DNA to Protein](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK21050/).

### 5.2 Expression is regulated

A cell does not simply read every gene continuously in the same way. Gene expression is regulated by multiple mechanisms, including transcription factors, chromatin state, RNA processing, RNA stability, translation, and signaling pathways.

Different cell types can contain essentially the same genome while expressing different subsets of genes. Their distinct structures and functions depend on regulatory context, developmental history, and interactions with their environments.

Therefore:
- DNA sequence matters.
- Regulatory mechanisms matter.
- Cellular context matters.
- The products of gene expression matter.
- Environmental conditions can influence expression.

A DNA sequence alone is not a complete description of a living organism's development or behavior.

### 5.3 Coding regions and noncoding regions

Some DNA sequences specify protein sequences. Other DNA regions participate in regulation, chromosome organization, RNA production, or other functions. Some sequences have no currently established function, and function can be difficult to establish.

It is incorrect to assume that every nucleotide has a known, direct, one-to-one role in determining a visible trait. It is also incorrect to assume that a sequence's statistical complexity automatically establishes a particular biological function.

### 5.4 What sequence information does and does not establish

Sequence analysis can identify patterns, compare genomes, detect variants, and help predict genes or regulatory features. Functional claims require additional evidence, such as expression data, biochemical assays, perturbation experiments, and organism-level observations.

The distinction is important: a statistical property of a sequence is not the same thing as a demonstrated causal role in a living system.

References:
- [NCBI Bookshelf — Expression of Genetic Information](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9842/)
- [NCBI Bookshelf — Understanding a Genome Sequence](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK21136/)
- [Information Theory in Computational Biology: Where We Stand Today](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7517167/).

## 6. Cellular signaling: reception and response

Cells receive and respond to chemical and physical conditions in their surroundings. A signaling pathway may involve a ligand, a receptor, intracellular transduction, and a downstream response.

A simplified pathway can be represented:

**Input or ligand → receptor → intracellular signaling → regulated target → cellular response**

This is a simplified explanatory model. Real pathways can branch, interact, amplify, inhibit, adapt, and feed back into earlier steps.

### 6.1 Reception

A receptor or sensor interacts with a particular input under specified conditions. Receptor specificity is not absolute in every case; different ligands may bind with different affinities, and receptor responses can depend on concentration, cellular context, and other molecules.

### 6.2 Transduction

The initial interaction can alter intracellular conditions. This may involve phosphorylation, ion movement, second messengers, protein interactions, or changes in gene expression.

### 6.3 Processing and response

The cell's response depends on the pathway's architecture and state. Possible responses include changes in metabolism, movement, secretion, growth, gene expression, or cell division.

A response is not proof that a cell possesses human-like comprehension. The term *processing* in a biological model describes mechanisms and relationships; it does not automatically imply conscious interpretation.

### 6.4 Noise, reliability, and feedback

Biochemical signaling is affected by molecular fluctuations, variable concentrations, and environmental conditions. Cells can use feedback, redundancy, thresholds, and pathway interactions to regulate responses.

Information theory provides tools to quantify how much a measured input predicts a measured output, even when the pathway is noisy. But the analysis depends on the experiment and on which variables are chosen.

References:
- [The application of information theory to biochemical signaling systems](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3820280/)
- [Quantifying information of intracellular signaling](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9507437/)
- [Cellular noise and information transmission](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5561739/).

## 7. Nervous systems: signals, neural coding, and interpretation

Neurons communicate through electrical and chemical processes. Action potentials involve changes in membrane voltage associated with ion movement through channels. At synapses, chemical neurotransmitters or electrical coupling transmit influence to other cells.

Neuroscience investigates how neural activity relates to sensory input, motor output, memory, attention, and other functions.

Information theory can be used to examine how much a neural response predicts a stimulus or behavioral variable. It can quantify relationships between recorded activity and selected experimental conditions.

But three distinctions remain essential:

1. **Neural activity is not identical to a conscious experience.** A neural recording is evidence about activity in a nervous system; it does not by itself exhaust the question of subjective experience.
2. **A neural code is a scientific model.** The choice of what counts as a stimulus, response, or relevant variable shapes the information calculation.
3. **Statistical dependence is not a complete causal explanation.** A neural response may correlate with a stimulus without establishing the full mechanism by which a perception or action occurs.

A useful introductory review describes the application of Shannon's framework to neural communication: [Information theory in the brain](https://www.hms.harvard.edu/bss/neuro/bornlab/nb204/statistics/reinagel-infotheory-currbiol2000.pdf).

This investigation does not attempt to settle consciousness. It records that the relation among neural signaling, information measures, interpretation, and conscious experience remains a distinct research problem.

## 8. Measurement: the Witness receives a representation

Information claims depend on observations and measurements. A scientific instrument does not simply deliver reality without mediation. It interacts with a system, produces a response, and requires calibration and interpretation.

A simplified measurement chain is:

**Physical system → interaction with sensor → instrument response → recorded data → analysis → interpretation**

Each link can introduce limitations:
- sensor sensitivity and selectivity;
- calibration error;
- noise and interference;
- sampling frequency and resolution;
- data processing and filtering;
- model assumptions;
- uncertainty in interpretation.

A detector may register a signal without identifying its source. A measured pattern may be consistent with several possible explanations. Independent measurements, calibration standards, controls, and reproducible procedures help distinguish alternatives.

This is especially relevant to the Witness. Reception is not automatically complete understanding. A record is not the whole reality recorded. Interpretation can be accurate, incomplete, or mistaken.

The scientific investigator must therefore ask:
- What did the instrument actually measure?
- What variable was inferred rather than directly measured?
- What calibration and controls were used?
- What uncertainty remains?
- What alternative explanations fit the data?
- What would falsify or correct the proposed interpretation?

## 9. Information and entropy: a necessary distinction from thermodynamics

Investigation 024 examined thermodynamic entropy. Investigation 025 now examines Shannon entropy. The same word appears in both fields, but the quantities are not identical.

### 9.1 Shannon entropy

Shannon entropy quantifies uncertainty in a specified probability distribution and is commonly expressed in bits when base-2 logarithms are used.

### 9.2 Thermodynamic entropy

Thermodynamic entropy is a physical state quantity in thermodynamics and statistical mechanics. It is related to the number and probability of microscopic configurations compatible with a macroscopic description, with physical units and constants appropriate to the formulation.

### 9.3 Their relationship and limits

There are mathematical and conceptual connections between information theory and statistical physics. However, Shannon entropy and thermodynamic entropy should not be treated as interchangeable without specifying the model, units, physical interpretation, and assumptions.

A mathematical analogy between uncertainty and physical entropy does not establish that information is a substance, that a message is energy, or that biological information automatically has semantic meaning.

Reference: [Reintegrating Biology Through the Nexus of Energy, Information, and Matter](https://academic.oup.com/icb/article/61/6/2082/6347348).

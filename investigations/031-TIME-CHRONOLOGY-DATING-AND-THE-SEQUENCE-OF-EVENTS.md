## 3. Four terms that must remain distinct

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sequence** | The order in which events or features are related: before, after, or contemporaneous within the resolution available |
| **Relative age** | An ordering that establishes which feature is older or younger without necessarily assigning a numerical age |
| **Numerical age** | An estimated elapsed time or calendar placement, derived from a defined measurement and model |
| **Chronology** | An ordered framework assigning dates or age relationships to a series of records or events |

A chronology may combine relative relationships, numerical measurements, reference markers, and models. It is not identical to any one measurement.

Another distinction is essential:

- **Precision** concerns the fineness or repeatability of a measurement.
- **Accuracy** concerns closeness to the quantity being estimated.
- **Uncertainty** characterizes the dispersion or limits of values reasonably attributable to the measurand, under a stated model.
- **Resolution** concerns the smallest temporal separation the record or method can distinguish.

A result reported to many decimal places is not necessarily accurate. A date with a narrow analytical uncertainty can still have a wider contextual uncertainty if the sample's relationship to the event is poorly established.

## 4. Relative chronology: establishing order before assigning years

Geology often begins by establishing relationships among layers, structures, and events. These relationships can establish sequence without directly measuring elapsed years.

### 4.1 Superposition

In an undisturbed sequence of sedimentary layers, lower beds are generally older than the beds above them. The principle depends on the sequence remaining sufficiently intact.

The National Park Service describes superposition as a basic principle of relative dating and explains that disturbances such as folding and faulting can alter the original arrangement. [NPS — Superposition and Original Horizontality](https://home.nps.gov/articles/geologic-principles-superposition-and-original-horizontality.htm)

The witness must therefore inspect the actual structure. An overturned or faulted sequence cannot be read as though it were an undisturbed stack.

### 4.2 Original horizontality

Sedimentary layers are generally deposited in horizontal or nearly horizontal positions. If they are now tilted or folded, that deformation occurred after deposition.

This establishes a relationship between deposition and later deformation. It does not by itself give either event a numerical date.

### 4.3 Cross-cutting relationships

A fault, intrusion, vein, or other feature that cuts an existing rock unit is younger than the material it cuts, provided the relationship is correctly identified.

The National Park Service describes cross-cutting relationships as a method for determining relative ages of rocks and geological structures. [NPS — Cross-Cutting Relationships](https://home.nps.gov/articles/geologic-principles-cross-cutting-relationships.htm)

### 4.4 Inclusions and unconformities

Fragments included within a rock are generally older than the rock that contains them. An unconformity represents a surface or interval where deposition stopped, erosion occurred, or a portion of the record is absent.

An unconformity is especially important because the visible rock sequence may not preserve every event or every interval of elapsed time.

**What relative dating establishes:** order and relationships among recorded features.

**What it does not automatically establish:** the number of years between them.

Relative chronology is not an inferior substitute for numerical dating. It answers a different question and can constrain the interpretation of numerical dates.

## 5. Fossils and faunal succession: biological sequence as chronological evidence

Fossils occur in geological contexts. Their distribution through successive rock units can help identify and correlate strata.

The National Park Service describes faunal succession as the recognizable succession of fossil assemblages through geological time. Fossils can therefore help establish relative ages and correlate rock units across different locations. [NPS — Faunal Succession](https://www.nps.gov/articles/geologic-principles-faunal-succession.htm)

Several distinctions are necessary.

### 5.1 Fossil age and enclosing rock

A fossil may be preserved in sediment deposited after the organism died. The age of the fossil's biological material, the time of burial, and the age of the enclosing rock are related but not automatically identical.

A fossil can also be reworked: eroded from an older deposit and redeposited in a younger one. In that case, the fossil is older than the deposit in which it is now found.

### 5.2 Index fossils and correlation

Some fossil groups have a useful combination of broad geographic distribution and relatively limited stratigraphic range. These fossils can help correlate layers between locations.

Correlation does not mean the layers are physically continuous or that every location experienced identical depositional conditions.

### 5.3 Fossils and numerical dating

Fossils are often dated through their stratigraphic relationship to other materials, including volcanic ash layers or minerals that can be dated by radiometric methods. The numerical age may therefore belong directly to a volcanic mineral or other dated material, while the fossil's age is constrained by its geological relationship to that material.

**What is observed:** fossil remains and their positions within geological units.

**What is inferred:** biological succession, relative sequence, correlation, and—in combination with other evidence—numerical age constraints.

**What must not be conflated:** the organism's death, fossilization, burial, reworking, and the deposition of the current enclosing layer.

## 6. Dendrochronology: assigning calendar years to tree rings

Tree-ring dating offers an unusually direct example of a chronology built by matching repeating natural records.

In suitable tree species and environments, annual growth produces recognizable ring patterns. Ring width and other properties vary with environmental conditions.

Dendrochronologists compare patterns among living trees, dead trees, and historical timbers. Matching sequences can assign calendar years to individual rings.

The National Park Service explains that crossdating allows ring patterns to be matched and can help determine when a tree was felled or died, depending on which outer rings and bark remain. [NPS — Dendrochronology](https://home.nps.gov/articles/archeology-dendrochronology.htm)

The University of Arizona's Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research describes crossdating as the fundamental technique for assigning the correct calendar year to individual rings by matching patterns among samples. [University of Arizona — Crossdating](https://www.ltrr.arizona.edu/lorim/basic.html)

### 6.1 Crossdating is not simply counting rings

Counting rings from the center outward can be wrong if a tree has a missing ring, a locally absent ring, or a false ring. Crossdating checks the pattern against other samples from the same region.

A sequence of unusually narrow and wide rings can function like a pattern signature. Matching overlapping sequences extends the chronology backward through dead wood and preserved timber.

### 6.2 What date is actually obtained?

The date assigned to a ring is the year in which that ring grew.

That does not automatically establish:
- the year the tree died;
- the year it was cut;
- the year timber was transported;
- the year a building was constructed;
- the year the timber was reused;
- the year an archaeological deposit formed.

If bark and the final growth ring are present, the outermost ring can closely constrain the felling year. If outer rings are missing, the felling date may be later than the last preserved ring.

Historic England's dendrochronology guidance explicitly cautions that the date of the rings is not necessarily the date of felling or use, and that the presence of bark and outer rings affects the precision of the felling estimate. [Historic England — Dendrochronology Guidelines](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/dendrochronology-guidelines/dendrochronology-pdf/)

### 6.3 Chronology and climate are related but distinct

A ring sequence can assign calendar years. The width or density of those rings may also be used as a climate proxy, as examined in Investigation 030.

The chronological question is: **When did this ring form?**

The proxy question is: **What environmental conditions influenced its growth?**

The same sample can witness to both, but the inferences are not identical.

## 7. Radiometric dating: radioactive decay as a physical clock

Radioactive isotopes decay at characteristic statistical rates. A parent isotope transforms into a daughter product. The half-life is the time required for half of the parent isotope in a large population to decay.

The U.S. Geological Survey explains the parent-daughter relationship, half-life, and use of radioactive isotopes to determine the ages of rocks and minerals. [USGS — Geologic Age: Using Radioactive Decay](https://www.usgs.gov/educational-resources/geologic-age-using-radioactive-decay-determine-geologic-age)

A simplified decay relationship is:

\[
N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}
\]

where:
- \(N_0\) is the initial parent-isotope quantity;
- \(N(t)\) is the parent quantity remaining;
- \(\lambda\) is the decay constant;
- \(t\) is elapsed time under the model.

The half-life is related to the decay constant:

\[
t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}
\]

The physical decay law is not itself the whole dating procedure. The investigator must establish the initial conditions, measure the relevant isotopes, identify the mineral or material being dated, and evaluate whether the system remained sufficiently closed.

### 7.1 What is dated?

Radiometric methods date a material or geological event represented by that material, according to the isotope system and geological history.

Depending on the mineral and method, the measured age may relate to:
- crystallization;
- cooling below a closure temperature;
- metamorphic resetting;
- alteration;
- or another event that changed or preserved the isotope system.

A mineral age is not automatically the age of every rock layer or fossil nearby.

### 7.2 Closure and disturbance

A closed system retains the relevant parent and daughter isotopes sufficiently for the age calculation to be meaningful. Heating, fluids, weathering, alteration, or other geological processes can add or remove isotopes.

If the system has been disturbed, the measured isotope relationship may record a later event or a mixture of events.

The investigator must examine the mineral's history and not merely report the laboratory output.

### 7.3 Multiple isotope systems

Different isotope systems have different half-lives, parent-daughter pairs, mineral hosts, and useful age ranges. Comparing more than one suitable method can help test whether the geological interpretation is coherent.

The USGS notes that multiple methods may be applied where feasible and that the behavior of isotopes in Earth's crust remains a subject of continued study and refinement. [USGS — Geologic Age](https://www.usgs.gov/educational-resources/geologic-age-using-radioactive-decay-determine-geologic-age)

**What is measured:** isotope abundances and ratios in a defined material.

**What is inferred:** elapsed time since a specified isotope-system event, under the relevant assumptions.

**What must not be conflated:** the age of a mineral, the age of a rock body, the age of a sedimentary layer, and the age of an event inferred from their relationships.

## 8. Radiocarbon dating: the death of an organism and the calibration of its carbon

Radiocarbon dating uses carbon-14, a radioactive isotope incorporated into living organisms through carbon exchange with their environment.

After an organism dies, it no longer replenishes its carbon through normal biological exchange. Carbon-14 then decays. Measurement of the remaining radiocarbon, interpreted with an appropriate calibration model, can estimate the time since the organism stopped exchanging carbon with its environment.

The crucial distinction is that a radiocarbon measurement is not automatically a calendar date.

### 8.1 Radiocarbon age and calendar age

Atmospheric radiocarbon concentrations have varied over time. A measured radiocarbon age must therefore be calibrated against independently dated reference records to estimate calendar-age ranges.

The IntCal20 research publication explains that radiocarbon ages must be converted to calendar-age equivalents using calibration curves that account for fluctuations in atmospheric carbon-14. [Reimer et al. — IntCal20 Calibration Curve](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/intcal20-northern-hemisphereradiocarbon-age-calibration-curve-055-cal-kbp/83257B63DC3AF9CFA6243F59D7503EFF)

Different reservoirs require appropriate calibration. Marine organisms, for example, may reflect carbon that has a different radiocarbon history from the atmosphere. Freshwater reservoir effects can also matter.

Historic England's guidance explains the need to use a calibration curve appropriate to the sample's carbon reservoir and to report the method and uncertainty. [Historic England — Radiocarbon Dating and Chronological Modelling](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/radiocarbon-dating-chronological-modelling/radiocarbon-dating/)

### 8.2 Contamination and sample selection

A sample can contain younger or older carbon introduced through burial, conservation, handling, or environmental processes. Pretreatment aims to remove contaminants, but sample selection and preservation remain important.

### 8.3 What event is dated?

Radiocarbon generally estimates when the sampled organism or organic material stopped exchanging carbon with its environment. It does not automatically date the deposition of the surrounding sediment, the construction of a building, or the event that caused the organism's death.

A piece of old wood can be burned or reused long after the tree stopped growing. The radiocarbon age of the wood is not automatically the date of the fire or building in which it is found.

**What is measured:** the radiocarbon content of a defined sample.

**What is inferred:** the time since the sample's carbon exchange ceased, converted through calibration into a calendar-age estimate.

**What must not be conflated:** organism death, wood growth, burning, burial, reuse, and the formation of the archaeological context.

## 9. Ice-core chronology: annual layers, markers, and age-depth models

Ice cores preserve layers of snowfall and chemical or physical signals. Some records show annual cycles that can be counted, while volcanic deposits and other recognizable horizons provide tie points for comparison among cores.

Investigation 030 examined ice cores as environmental proxies. Here the focus is different: how the ice itself is assigned an age at a given depth.

### 9.1 Annual layer counting

Seasonal variations in chemical species, dust, and other properties can identify annual layers. Counting these layers builds a chronology from a known or constrained starting point.

But layers become thinner with depth as ice is compressed. Some signals may be missing or ambiguous, and counting errors can accumulate.

### 9.2 Volcanic tie points

Volcanic sulfate or other identifiable signals can provide horizons that are correlated with known or independently dated events. These markers help anchor or synchronize chronologies.

The SP19 South Pole ice-core chronology combines annual layer counting with volcanic matching. Its authors report age uncertainties that vary with depth and the spacing of tie points; they also identify limitations where records and markers are less secure. [Fudge et al. — SP19 Chronology, Part 1](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/15/1793/2019/cp-15-1793-2019.html)

### 9.3 Ice age and gas age are different

Air bubbles become enclosed as snow is compacted into ice. The gas trapped in bubbles is generally younger than the surrounding ice at the same depth because the air remains connected to the atmosphere for a period before final enclosure.

Thus:
- the age of the ice matrix;
- the age of the trapped gas;
- and the age of a chemical signal

may require distinct interpretations and corrections.

**What is measured:** depth, annual or seasonal signals, chemical markers, and trapped gases.

**What is inferred:** an age-depth relationship for ice and, separately, an age relationship for enclosed atmospheric gases.

**What must not be conflated:** ice age, gas age, deposition year, and the date of the environmental event represented by a marker.

## 10. Stratigraphic correlation and age models

A stratigraphic sequence is a record of deposition, interruption, erosion, deformation, and other geological events.

An age model connects measured or correlated chronological constraints to depths or positions within that sequence.

The model may combine:
- relative order;
- radiometric dates;
- fossil assemblages;
- magnetic reversals;
- volcanic ash horizons;
- annual layers;
- sedimentation rates;
- and other independent or partially independent markers.

### 10.1 Interpolation is not direct observation

If two horizons have age constraints, a model may estimate the ages of material between them. The result depends on the assumptions used about accumulation, sedimentation, hiatuses, and changes in rate.

A simple constant accumulation rate may be inappropriate where deposition varies or stops.

### 10.2 Missing time

Erosion or non-deposition can remove part of the record. A physical contact between two layers can therefore represent a substantial interval that is not preserved.

A sequence of adjacent layers does not prove that no time elapsed between their formation.

### 10.3 Correlation is not identity

Two sections may be correlated through a distinctive marker, fossil assemblage, or other signal. Correlation supports a relationship in time; it does not prove the entire histories of both locations were identical.

The witness must state whether a date is measured directly, constrained by correlation, or interpolated by a model.

## 11. Astronomy: looking into the past through traveling light

Astronomy introduces a different relationship between observation and time.

Light travels at a finite speed. When a telescope receives light from a distant object, it observes light that left that object earlier.

NASA explains that the Moon is seen as it was about 1.3 seconds earlier, and that light from distant galaxies can take billions of years to reach telescopes. [NASA — How Does Webb See Back in Time?](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/science-overview/science-explainers/how-does-webb-see-back-in-time/)

### 11.1 Lookback time

Lookback time is the elapsed time between the emission of the observed light and its reception, as described within the relevant astronomical model.

The light's arrival is a present observation of a past emission event.

This is not the same as directly observing the object's present state. The object may have changed substantially during the light-travel interval.

### 11.2 Cosmological redshift

As space expands, the wavelength of light traveling through it is stretched. This cosmological redshift is related to the expansion history of the universe.

NASA describes cosmological redshift as the stretching of light waves as space expands and explains its use in studying distant galaxies and the past. [NASA — What Is Cosmological Redshift?](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/webb/what-is-cosmological-redshift/)

Redshift is an observable spectral relationship. Converting redshift into distance or lookback time requires a cosmological model and its parameters. It is not a universal stopwatch that can be read without assumptions.

### 11.3 Distance, emission time, and present cosmic distance

In an expanding universe, several distance concepts are used, including luminosity distance, angular-diameter distance, comoving distance, and proper distance at a specified cosmic time.

These quantities answer different questions. They should not be substituted for one another without stating the definition and model.

**What is observed:** light received now, its spectrum, and measured redshift.

**What is inferred:** properties at emission, lookback time, and distance measures within a cosmological model.

**What must not be conflated:** the date of observation, the time of emission, the object's present state, redshift, and the several cosmological distance definitions.

## 12. A common chronology audit across disciplines

The methods differ, but the Witness can apply a common set of questions without pretending that the methods are interchangeable.

| Discipline or method | Directly measured or observed | Chronological inference | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dendrochronology | Ring patterns and anatomical features | Calendar year of ring formation | Missing outer rings, false or absent rings, context of timber use |
| Radiometric dating | Parent and daughter isotope abundances | Time since a specified isotope-system event | Open-system behavior, inheritance, resetting, sample context |
| Radiocarbon | Carbon-14 relative to stable carbon | Time since carbon exchange ceased, calibrated to calendar age | Reservoir effects, contamination, calibration uncertainty |
| Stratigraphy | Layer order and geological relationships | Relative sequence and correlation | Deformation, erosion, hiatuses, reworking |
| Fossil succession | Fossil assemblages in strata | Relative age and correlation | Reworking, ecological range, incomplete preservation |
| Ice-core dating | Depth, annual signals, chemical markers | Age-depth chronology; separate gas chronology | Layer ambiguity, thinning, missing signals, tie-point spacing |
| Astronomy | Received light and spectral features | Emission history and lookback time | Finite light speed, expansion, cosmological model dependence |

This comparison does not create a single universal dating method. It identifies the distinct evidence each method uses and the chronological claim it can support.

## 13. The event-dating chain

A date assigned to an event often depends on a chain of relationships:

**Event → material affected or produced → sample selection → physical or chemical measurement → calibration or age model → contextual relationship → event-date estimate**

At every step, the investigator should ask:

1. What event is the actual target?
2. What material or signal is being dated?
3. What physical process connects that material to the event?
4. Could the material predate the event?
5. Could it have been altered or reset afterward?
6. Could it have been transported, reused, reworked, or contaminated?
7. Does the method date formation, death, deposition, cooling, burial, or another event?
8. What assumptions connect the measured age to the event?
9. Is the result a direct date, a relative constraint, a correlation, or a model-based estimate?
10. What is the uncertainty of the measurement and of the contextual connection?

A date is only as relevant to the event as the evidence connecting the dated object to that event.

## 14. Chronological uncertainty and false precision

Uncertainty can enter at multiple levels:

- instrument precision;
- sample heterogeneity;
- contamination;
- calibration-curve uncertainty;
- initial-condition assumptions;
- decay constants;
- system closure;
- age-depth interpolation;
- layer identification;
- stratigraphic correlation;
- sample-to-event association;
- model selection.

These uncertainties should not be collapsed into one number if they arise from different sources or describe different parts of the inference.

A laboratory may report a narrow analytical uncertainty while the historical event remains less precisely dated because the sample's context is uncertain.

Likewise, multiple dates that agree may share the same calibration, reference chronology, or model assumption. Agreement is stronger when the evidence is genuinely independent.

**Precision in the measurement does not guarantee precision in the historical claim.**

## 15. What this investigation reveals across disciplines

Chronology is not one instrument or one formula. It is a family of methods for establishing temporal relationships.

Several common distinctions emerge:

1. **Sequence precedes some numerical dating.** Relative relationships can constrain what happened before or after another event.
2. **A dated sample is not automatically the event.** The relationship between sample and target must be established.
3. **Different methods date different physical processes.** Ring formation, organism death, mineral crystallization, cooling, deposition, and light emission are not interchangeable.
4. **Calibration matters.** A measured physical quantity may require a reference record or model before it becomes a calendar date.
5. **Chronologies combine evidence.** A chronology can integrate direct measurements, relative order, markers, and interpolation.
6. **Records can be incomplete.** Erosion, missing layers, reworking, disturbance, and preservation affect what survives.
7. **Models connect observations.** An age model can be useful without being identical to the observations from which it is constructed.
8. **Uncertainty follows the entire inference.** The final event-date claim must account for more than instrument precision.
9. **Independent methods can cross-check.** Their agreement is meaningful when shared assumptions and errors are recognized.
10. **The target event must remain explicit.** The witness must say what the date actually refers to.

## 19. What this investigation establishes—and what remains unresolved

### Bounded findings

1. Sequence, relative age, numerical age, and chronology are related but distinct.
2. Stratigraphic superposition, original horizontality, cross-cutting relationships, inclusions, and unconformities establish relative relationships, subject to the actual condition of the geological record.
3. Fossil succession can support relative dating and correlation, but a fossil may be reworked or preserved after the organism's death.
4. Dendrochronology assigns calendar years to tree rings through crossdating; ring dates do not automatically establish the date of timber use or construction.
5. Radiometric dating uses parent-daughter isotope relationships and half-lives to estimate time since a specified isotope-system event.
6. Mineral and rock ages depend on geological history, including crystallization, cooling, resetting, alteration, and system closure.
7. Radiocarbon dates require calibration to calendar-age estimates and must account for reservoir effects, contamination, and sample context.
8. Ice-core chronologies combine annual layer counting, volcanic tie points, and age-depth relationships; ice age and enclosed gas age are distinct.
9. Stratigraphic age models connect measurements and correlations to depth or sequence, and may include interpolation and missing intervals.
10. Astronomical observations receive light emitted in the past; lookback time and distance interpretations depend on physical definitions and cosmological models.
11. A sample's numerical age is not automatically the date of the event associated with it.
12. Analytical precision does not eliminate contextual uncertainty.
13. Multiple dating methods can cross-check a chronology when their shared assumptions and errors are examined.
14. A faithful chronological claim identifies what is dated, how it is dated, and how it relates to the target event.
15. The completed GUA-W remains unchanged.

### What this investigation does not establish

- It does not calculate the age of the Earth or the universe.
- It does not settle a theological chronology debate by selecting a scientific method.
- It does not claim every geological sequence is undisturbed.
- It does not claim every fossil dates the layer in which it is found.
- It does not claim a tree-ring date automatically dates the construction of a building.
- It does not claim radiometric dating always dates crystallization; the relevant isotope-system event depends on the mineral and geological history.
- It does not claim a radiocarbon age is already a calendar date before calibration.
- It does not claim ice age and gas age are identical.
- It does not claim redshift alone gives a model-independent lookback time.
- It does not claim all chronological methods are interchangeable.
- It does not claim that a narrow laboratory uncertainty guarantees a narrow event-date uncertainty.
- It does not add a time, clock, chronology, or dating component to GUA-W.

## 20. Unresolved questions carried forward

1. How should a chronology be evaluated when its independent dating methods share reference records or calibration assumptions?
2. How can a witness distinguish an actual gap in a historical sequence from a gap caused by missing preservation or incomplete sampling?
3. What evidence is sufficient to establish that a mineral's isotope system remained closed after the event being dated?
4. How should age-depth models represent variable accumulation, erosion, and hiatuses without concealing uncertainty?
5. How can the age of a fossil be constrained when its enclosing material cannot be dated directly?
6. How can AI distinguish a direct measurement from an age inferred through correlation or interpolation when summarizing a scientific paper?
7. What is the relationship between the different astronomical distance measures and lookback time, and how do cosmological parameters affect each?
8. How should chronological claims be compared when one method dates formation and another dates death, cooling, burial, or deposition?
9. What does the comparison of natural clocks reveal about recurring physical processes, and where do their differences prevent a direct comparison?
10. How should a Witness report a chronology when the order is secure but the numerical interval remains uncertain?

These questions remain open. They do not indicate that GUA-W is incomplete.

## 21. Next direction

The investigation has followed how witnesses establish sequence and assign dates. A further question now arises from the relationship between chronological records and the events they are said to represent:

**How do scientists distinguish a continuous process from a sequence of preserved snapshots—and what evidence establishes that a record is complete, interrupted, mixed, or missing?**

The next investigation should emerge by examining:
- continuous and discontinuous records;
- sedimentation and depositional hiatuses;
- erosion and unconformities;
- annual and seasonal cycles;
- time-series sampling and temporal resolution;
- event layers and abrupt transitions;
- preservation bias and missing records;
- interpolation between observations;
- the difference between an event's duration and the resolution of its record.

The question is not whether every process leaves a complete record. It is how the Witness can tell what is present, what is absent, and what cannot be reconstructed from the surviving evidence.

**A record is not necessarily the whole event. A gap in the record is not automatically a gap in what occurred.**

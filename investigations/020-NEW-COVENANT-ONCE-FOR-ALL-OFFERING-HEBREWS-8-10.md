## 1. The question carried forward

Investigation 019 examined Christ's everlasting priesthood, the Father's oath, the order of Melchisedec, the once-for-all offering, and the continuing intercession of the Son.

Hebrews 7:25 states that Christ is able to save to the uttermost those who come unto God by Him, seeing He ever lives to make intercession. Hebrews 7:27 states that He offered Himself once.

Investigation 020 follows the argument into Hebrews 8–10.

**Central question: What has Christ accomplished as Mediator of the New Covenant, and how does Hebrews preserve both the complete sufficiency of His offering and the seriousness of its warnings?**

The examination asks:

1. What does Hebrews mean by the better covenant and its better promises?
2. How does Jeremiah 31:31–34 function in Hebrews 8?
3. What is the relation between the earthly sanctuary and the heavenly things?
4. Why does Hebrews connect covenant inauguration with death and blood?
5. What does Christ's entrance into the heavenly sanctuary accomplish?
6. What does the cleansing of conscience mean in Hebrews 9:9–14?
7. How do Christ's once-for-all offering, His appearance before God, and His future appearing relate?
8. What does Hebrews 10 mean when it says there remains no more sacrifice for sins?
9. How must the warning in Hebrews 10:26–31 be read alongside the assurance and invitation in 10:19–25?
10. How does the passage relate to the Father's purpose and the Son's keeping without erasing the hearer's responsibility to continue in faith?

The investigation must not reduce the New Covenant to a slogan, equate every covenant in Scripture, or weaken the warning because the promises are strong. Nor may it weaken the sufficiency of Christ's offering by treating it as incomplete.

## 4. Hebrews 8:1–6 — the summary and the better ministry

Hebrews 8 begins by identifying the sum of the argument:

> Now of the things which we have spoken this is the sum: We have such an high priest, who is set on the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens; (Hebrews 8:1, KJV)

The High Priest examined in Hebrews 7 is not serving in an earthly sanctuary as a successor to Aaron. He is set at the right hand of the Majesty in the heavens.

Verse 2 calls Him a minister of the sanctuary and of the true tabernacle, which the Lord pitched, and not man.

Verse 3 says every high priest is ordained to offer gifts and sacrifices; therefore it is necessary that this High Priest also have something to offer. The following chapters explain the character and finality of that offering.

### 4.1 Earthly copy and heavenly things

Hebrews 8:4–5 says that if Christ were on earth, He should not be a priest, because there are priests who offer gifts according to the law. These serve unto the example and shadow of heavenly things.

The writer quotes the instruction to Moses concerning the pattern shown in the mount (Exodus 25:40).

The text distinguishes the earthly service from the heavenly things of which it is an example and shadow. The earthly tabernacle is not presented as the ultimate reality that defines God's heavenly sanctuary. Its pattern is given by God.

### 4.2 A more excellent ministry

Hebrews 8:6 states:

> But now hath he obtained a more excellent ministry, by how much also he is the mediator of a better covenant, which was established upon better promises. (KJV)

The argument identifies three connected claims:

1. Christ has obtained a more excellent ministry.
2. He is Mediator of a better covenant.
3. The covenant is established upon better promises.

The comparison is not based on a human improvement project. The better covenant is associated with the ministry of Christ.

**Direct finding:** Hebrews identifies Christ as the heavenly High Priest and Mediator of a better covenant established upon better promises.

**Boundary:** The details of the promises must be read from the Scripture Hebrews itself quotes, rather than supplied by a later theological summary.

## 5. Hebrews 8:7–13 — Jeremiah's New Covenant promise

Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes Jeremiah 31:31–34. The quotation is central to the argument and must be read as a whole.

God says He will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, not according to the covenant made with their fathers when He took them by the hand to lead them out of Egypt.

The text identifies the former covenant's failure in relation to the people: they continued not in God's covenant, and He regarded them not.

The New Covenant promise then gives several specific declarations.

### 5.1 “I will put my laws into their mind”

God says:

> I will put my laws into their mind, and write them in their hearts: (Hebrews 8:10, KJV)

The subject of the action is God. The promise is not first described as the people's independent act of writing God's law into themselves. God says He will put and write.

The promise concerns inward relation to God's law, not merely possession of an external document.

### 5.2 “I will be to them a God”

The same verse continues:

> and I will be to them a God, and they shall be to me a people. (KJV)

The covenant promise includes belonging and relationship. The text does not reduce the covenant to an inward psychological experience.

### 5.3 Knowledge of the Lord

Hebrews 8:11 says they shall not teach every man his neighbour and every man his brother, saying, Know the Lord: for all shall know Him, from the least to the greatest.

This is a covenant promise concerning knowledge of the Lord among the people of the covenant. Its full relation to teaching, evangelism, discipleship, and the Church must be examined with other passages rather than assumed from this verse alone.

### 5.4 Mercy and sins remembered no more

Hebrews 8:12 says:

> For I will be merciful to their unrighteousness, and their sins and their iniquities will I remember no more. (KJV)

The promise includes mercy toward unrighteousness and the non-remembrance of sins and iniquities.

This becomes especially important in Hebrews 9–10, where the writer explains the relation between Christ's offering and the forgiveness of sins.

### 5.5 The first covenant and the new

Hebrews 8:13 says that in saying “A new covenant,” God has made the first old; and that which decayeth and waxeth old is ready to vanish away.

The verse describes the old covenant as obsolete in light of the promised New Covenant. It does not say God's earlier revelation was false. Hebrews uses the earlier revelation as the very Scripture that announces the New Covenant.

**Direct finding:** The New Covenant promise is God's promise. It includes inward inscription of His laws, covenant belonging, knowledge of the Lord, mercy, and the non-remembrance of sins.

**Supported inference:** The New Covenant's promised inward work is not presented as originating in autonomous human initiative. God is the speaker and actor in the promise.

**Boundary:** Hebrews quotes Jeremiah's promise to explain Christ's ministry. The exact scope and historical fulfillment of every phrase must be examined with Jeremiah, the Gospels, Acts, and the rest of the New Testament. Do not silently replace “house of Israel” and “house of Judah” with a different wording; investigate the canonical relation.

## 6. Hebrews 9:1–10 — the first covenant's sanctuary and limits

Hebrews 9 describes the first covenant's ordinances of divine service and its worldly sanctuary.

The writer names the tabernacle's divisions and furnishings:

- the first tabernacle, called the sanctuary;
- the candlestick;
- the table and shewbread;
- the second veil;
- the tabernacle called the Holiest of all;
- the golden censer;
- the ark of the covenant;
- the golden pot that had manna;
- Aaron's rod that budded;
- the tables of the covenant;
- the cherubims of glory shadowing the mercyseat.

The writer then describes the priests' regular service and the high priest's entrance alone into the second tabernacle once every year, not without blood, which he offered for himself and for the errors of the people (9:6–7).

### 6.1 The Holy Ghost signifying

Hebrews 9:8 says:

> The Holy Ghost this signifying, that the way into the holiest of all was not yet made manifest, while as the first tabernacle was yet standing: (KJV)

The writer interprets the arrangement as signifying a limitation in access under the first tabernacle's order.

Verse 9 calls it a figure for the time then present, in which gifts and sacrifices were offered that could not make the one performing the service perfect, as pertaining to the conscience.

Verse 10 describes meats, drinks, divers washings, and carnal ordinances imposed until the time of reformation.

The point is not that God made a mistake by giving the earlier ordinances. Hebrews presents them as an appointed arrangement whose limits pointed beyond themselves.

**Direct finding:** The first tabernacle's service was restricted, repeated, and unable to make the worshipper perfect as pertaining to conscience.

**Boundary:** Do not confuse the earthly sanctuary's limitations with an assertion that the earlier ordinances were unauthorized. Hebrews says they were imposed until the time of reformation.

## 7. Hebrews 9:11–14 — Christ enters the greater and more perfect tabernacle

Hebrews 9:11 turns to Christ:

> But Christ being come an high priest of good things to come, by a greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands, that is to say, not of this building; (KJV)

Verse 12 says He entered once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us, neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by His own blood.

The contrast is direct:

- the former high priest entered the earthly sanctuary with the blood of another;
- Christ entered the holy place once, by His own blood;
- the result is described as eternal redemption.

### 7.1 The blood of Christ and the conscience

Hebrews 9:13 compares the sanctifying of the flesh through the blood of bulls and goats and the ashes of an heifer with the blood of Christ.

Verse 14 asks:

> How much more shall the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without spot to God, purge your conscience from dead works to serve the living God? (KJV)

The text gives the action and purpose:

1. Christ offered Himself without spot to God.
2. The offering is through the eternal Spirit.
3. His blood purges the conscience from dead works.
4. The purpose is service to the living God.

### 7.2 What is a cleansed conscience?

The text does not say that the conscience becomes an independent source of truth or that every subjective feeling thereafter is infallible.

It says Christ's blood purges the conscience from dead works to serve the living God. The cleansing is connected to Christ's offering and to service.

The phrase “dead works” should be read in the argument of Hebrews. It must not be reduced to a general insult against every human action, nor should the investigator invent an exhaustive list beyond the text.

**Direct finding:** Christ's self-offering is superior to the former sacrificial service and obtains eternal redemption. His blood purges the conscience from dead works to serve the living God.

**Supported inference:** The New Covenant's inward promise in Hebrews 8 is connected to Christ's sacrificial work in Hebrews 9. Forgiveness and inward cleansing are not separate saving projects.

## 8. Hebrews 9:15–22 — Christ the Mediator and the necessity of death

Hebrews 9:15 identifies Christ as the Mediator of the New Testament:

> And for this cause he is the mediator of the new testament, that by means of death, for the redemption of the transgressions that were under the first testament, they which are called might receive the promise of eternal inheritance. (KJV)

This verse directly connects:

- Christ as Mediator;
- His death;
- redemption of transgressions under the first testament;
- those who are called;
- reception of the promise of eternal inheritance.

### 8.1 The testament and death

Hebrews 9:16–17 speaks of the necessity of the death of the testator in relation to a testament. The passage then returns to the inauguration of the first covenant with blood.

The argument in 9:18–22 recalls Moses' use of blood in the covenant inauguration (Exodus 24:1–11). Hebrews says that almost all things are by the law purged with blood, and without shedding of blood is no remission (9:22).

The passage is not giving a detached maxim about physical blood. It is explaining covenant inauguration, sacrifice, and remission in the Scriptural argument.

### 8.2 “They which are called”

Hebrews 9:15 does not say that the called originate the covenant or its redemption. Christ is the Mediator, and His death is the means identified for redemption. Those who are called receive the promise of eternal inheritance.

This should be compared with the prior investigations of the Father's giving and calling, but the terms must remain anchored in each passage's own context.

**Direct finding:** Christ's death is central to His mediation of the New Covenant and to redemption of transgressions, so that the called receive the promise of eternal inheritance.

**Boundary:** Hebrews 9:16–17 has generated interpretive discussion concerning the force of the Greek term translated “testament” and its relation to covenant and death. The immediate argument must be respected; a claim that one English gloss settles every question is unwarranted.

## 9. Hebrews 9:23–28 — the heavenly sanctuary, the one offering, and the appearing

Hebrews 9:23 says it was necessary that the patterns of things in the heavens should be purified with these; but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these.

Verse 24 clarifies that Christ has not entered holy places made with hands, which are figures of the true, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God for us.

This continues the argument from Hebrews 7:25: Christ's heavenly presence and intercession belong to His enduring priestly ministry.

### 9.1 Not repeated offering

Hebrews 9:25–26 contrasts Christ with the high priest who enters the holy place every year with blood of others.

Christ did not enter to offer Himself often. If so, He would have needed to suffer often since the foundation of the world. Instead:

> but now once in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself. (Hebrews 9:26, KJV)

The text is explicit: Christ's self-offering is not repeated.

### 9.2 Once to die, and after this the judgment

Hebrews 9:27 states the appointed pattern for human beings: once to die, and after this the judgment.

Verse 28 then compares Christ:

> So Christ was once offered to bear the sins of many; and unto them that look for him shall he appear the second time without sin unto salvation. (KJV)

The passage holds together:

1. Christ was once offered to bear the sins of many.
2. He will appear the second time.
3. His second appearing is unto salvation for those who look for Him.

The first appearing and offering are not repeated; the future appearing is not another atoning sacrifice. The text distinguishes His completed sacrifice from His future appearing.

**Direct finding:** Christ entered heaven itself, appears in God's presence for us, offered Himself once to put away sin, and shall appear again unto salvation.

**Boundary:** Hebrews 9:24 says He appears in God's presence for us; Hebrews 7:25 says He ever lives to make intercession. The passages are closely related, but the investigator should not invent a detailed heavenly liturgy that Scripture does not disclose.

## 10. Hebrews 10:1–10 — the shadow, the body, and the will of God

Hebrews 10 opens by describing the law as having a shadow of good things to come, and not the very image of the things. The repeated sacrifices could never make those who came perfect.

If the worshippers had been purged once, the writer reasons, they would have had no more conscience of sins; but in those sacrifices there is a remembrance again made of sins every year (10:2–3).

The text says it is not possible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins (10:4).

### 10.1 The quotation from Psalm 40

Hebrews 10:5–7 quotes Psalm 40:6–8:

> Sacrifice and offering thou wouldest not, but a body hast thou prepared me: (Hebrews 10:5, KJV)

The Son comes to do the will of God. The passage contrasts the repeated sacrifices with the obedience and offering of Christ.

Verse 10 gives the conclusion:

> By the which will we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all. (KJV)

The will of God and the offering of Christ are joined. The sanctification described here is through the offering of Christ's body once for all.

### 10.2 “Once for all”

The expression must be read with the contrast throughout Hebrews 9–10. The former sacrifices were repeated and could not take away sins; Christ's offering is once for all.

The text does not present the believer as repeating Christ's sacrifice through personal effort, religious performance, or ritual. Nor does it say that Christ's one offering makes the commands and warnings of the rest of Hebrews unnecessary.

**Direct finding:** The sanctification in Hebrews 10:10 is through the offering of Jesus Christ's body once for all, according to the will of God.

## 11. Hebrews 10:11–18 — one sacrifice, perfected for ever, and sins remembered no more

Hebrews 10:11 contrasts the daily standing priest who offers oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins, with Christ.

Verse 12 says:

> But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God; (KJV)

Verse 14 continues:

> For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified. (KJV)

The passage connects the one offering with the perfection of those being sanctified.

### 11.1 The Spirit's witness

Hebrews 10:15 says the Holy Ghost also is a witness to the hearers. The writer then quotes the New Covenant promise from Jeremiah 31:33–34:

- God's laws in their hearts and minds;
- sins and iniquities remembered no more.

Verse 18 concludes:

> Now where remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin. (KJV)

The argument is decisive in its own terms: where sins are remitted through the promised New Covenant work, no further offering for sin is required.

### 11.2 “Perfected for ever”

The phrase in 10:14 must be held alongside the continuing language of sanctification. Hebrews does not say that every believer has already reached sinless moral completion in daily conduct. The letter continues to command endurance, holiness, mutual exhortation, and faithfulness.

The perfection here is grounded in the one offering and the standing it secures for those sanctified. The investigator must not collapse sacrificial access and cleansing into a claim that the believer has no further need to grow, obey, repent, or endure.

**Direct finding:** Christ's one offering is sufficient; He has sat down at God's right hand; by one offering He has perfected for ever them that are sanctified; the New Covenant promises sins remembered no more; where remission is, there is no more offering for sin.

## 12. Hebrews 10:19–25 — bold access and the commands to continue

The writer turns from the doctrinal argument to its practical consequence:

> Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, (Hebrews 10:19, KJV)

The access is by the blood of Jesus. The way is described as new and living, which He consecrated for us through the veil, that is to say, His flesh (10:20).

The passage then identifies:

- a great priest over the house of God (10:21);
- drawing near with a true heart in full assurance of faith (10:22);
- hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience;
- bodies washed with pure water;
- holding fast the profession of faith without wavering, for He is faithful that promised (10:23);
- considering one another to provoke unto love and good works (10:24);
- not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, but exhorting one another (10:25).

The text places assurance and exhortation together.

### 12.1 The structure of the response

The commands are not the price paid to make Christ's offering sufficient. They are the stated response of those addressed after the writer has explained the access opened by Christ.

- Draw near.
- Hold fast.
- Consider one another.
- Provoke unto love and good works.
- Assemble.
- Exhort one another.

The reason for holding fast is not the reliability of human strength alone: “for he is faithful that promised” (10:23).

**Finding:** The completed offering establishes bold access, and the hearers are commanded to draw near, hold fast, and encourage one another.

**Boundary:** Do not make the commands optional because the promise is sure. Do not make the promise uncertain because the commands are real.

## 13. Hebrews 10:26–31 — the warning against willful sin

This warning must be read carefully and without evasion.

Hebrews 10:26 says:

> For if we sin wilfully after that we have received the knowledge of the truth, there remaineth no more sacrifice for sins, (KJV)

The following verses describe a fearful expectation of judgment and fiery indignation, the punishment of one who despised Moses' law, and the greater seriousness of trampling under foot the Son of God, counting the blood of the covenant an unholy thing, and doing despite unto the Spirit of grace (10:27–29).

Verse 30 recalls God's declaration that vengeance belongs to Him and that He will recompense, and that the Lord shall judge His people. Verse 31 concludes:

> It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God. (KJV)

### 13.1 What is the warning about?

The immediate context matters:

- the hearers have received the knowledge of the truth (10:26);
- the warning concerns the rejection or despising of the Son and His covenant blood (10:29);
- the writer describes judgment, not a minor loss of reward (10:27–31);
- the warning follows the invitation to draw near, hold fast, and assemble (10:19–25).

The phrase “sin wilfully” cannot be detached from the explanatory description in verse 29. The passage concerns a grave repudiation of the Son and His covenant sacrifice. The text does not give license to treat deliberate sin as harmless.

### 13.2 What does “there remaineth no more sacrifice for sins” mean?

Hebrews has already established that Christ's offering is once for all and that there is no more offering for sin (10:10–18). The warning now says that if the truth is knowingly repudiated in the described manner, there is no alternative sacrifice waiting outside Christ.

The statement does not mean Christ's sacrifice has become insufficient. It means there is no other sacrifice for sin after the one offering of the Son is rejected.

### 13.3 Do not narrow or broaden beyond the text

Two opposite errors must be avoided:

1. **Narrowing the warning until it says nothing:** claiming that the passage has no real force for its hearers, or that the described repudiation is merely hypothetical, without textual warrant.
2. **Broadening the warning until it contradicts the letter:** claiming that every instance of deliberate wrongdoing is automatically the exact apostasy described here, without examining the passage's full description of trampling the Son, profaning covenant blood, and rejecting the Spirit of grace.

The text must be allowed to speak with its full severity. The precise relation between this warning, the identity of the addressed hearers, and the final state of every individual case requires the broader argument of Hebrews and the full canonical testimony.

**Direct finding:** Hebrews warns of severe judgment for repudiating the Son and treating the covenant blood as unholy. There is no other sacrifice for sins beyond Christ's once-for-all offering.

**Unresolved boundary:** Hebrews 10:26–31 must be compared with Hebrews 6:4–12, Hebrews 3:12–14, and Hebrews 10:32–39 before drawing a complete conclusion about the relation between the warning, perseverance, and the final state of those addressed. The warning is real; a comprehensive account must not be asserted from 10:26 in isolation.

## 14. Hebrews 10:32–39 — remembrance, endurance, and faith

The writer tells the hearers to remember the former days, when after they were illuminated they endured a great fight of afflictions (10:32).

He recalls their suffering, public reproach, compassion for those in bonds, and the taking joyfully of the spoiling of their goods, knowing they had in heaven a better and enduring substance (10:33–34).

Then comes the command:

> Cast not away therefore your confidence, which hath great recompence of reward. (Hebrews 10:35, KJV)

The writer says they have need of patience, that after they have done the will of God they might receive the promise (10:36).

He quotes Habakkuk 2:3–4 concerning the coming One and the just living by faith (10:37–38). The section concludes:

> But we are not of them who draw back unto perdition; but of them that believe to the saving of the soul. (Hebrews 10:39, KJV)

The writer does not resolve the warning by removing the call to endurance. He addresses the hearers with confidence and urges them not to draw back.

**Direct finding:** The hearers are commanded to remember, endure, retain confidence, do the will of God, and live by faith. The writer expresses confidence that “we” are not of those who draw back unto perdition, but of those who believe to the saving of the soul.

**Boundary:** The text must not be reduced either to salvation by human merit or to a warning with no real consequence. The relation between God's keeping and the believer's endurance is addressed across the whole letter and the wider canon.

## 15. Canonical comparison — the covenant, Christ's blood, and the Father's purpose

### 15.1 The Last Supper — Christ identifies the covenant in His blood

In Matthew 26:26–29, Mark 14:22–25, and Luke 22:14–20, Jesus identifies the cup with His blood of the New Testament/covenant, shed for many for the remission of sins.

These Gospel accounts connect the covenant promise with Christ's approaching death. Hebrews explains the priestly and sacrificial significance of that death.

The texts should be read together without forcing every difference among the Gospel accounts into identical wording.

### 15.2 John 6:51–58 — His flesh given for the life of the world

John 6 records Jesus speaking of the bread He will give as His flesh, which He will give for the life of the world. The passage speaks of eating His flesh and drinking His blood and of having life through Him.

This is a relevant comparison with Hebrews' emphasis on the offering of Christ's body and blood. However, the interpretation of the eating and drinking language must be examined within John 6 and the wider Gospel; Hebrews 10 should not be used to erase the language of John 6, nor John 6 used to bypass Hebrews' argument about the once-for-all offering.

### 15.3 Romans 3:21–26 — redemption through Christ's blood

Romans 3 speaks of righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ, justification by grace, redemption in Christ Jesus, and God setting Him forth as a propitiation through faith in His blood.

This agrees with Hebrews in locating the saving ground in God's action through Christ, not in an alternative human sacrifice or an independent human source of redemption.

### 15.4 Romans 8:1–4, 31–39 — no condemnation and no separation

Romans 8:1 declares no condemnation to those in Christ Jesus. Verses 2–4 speak of the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus and God's sending His Son concerning sin.

Romans 8:31–39 then grounds assurance in God's action, Christ's death and resurrection, His place at God's right hand, His intercession, and the impossibility of separation from the love of God in Christ.

This comparison is especially important after Hebrews 10's warning. The warning must not be made to cancel Romans 8, and Romans 8 must not be used to silence Hebrews 10. Each passage must be read in its own argument and then compared canonically.

### 15.5 2 Corinthians 3 — the new covenant and the Spirit

2 Corinthians 3 contrasts the letter and the Spirit, and speaks of the ministry of the New Testament and the Spirit's work. It is a direct canonical comparison for the New Covenant's inward work.

The text should be examined in its own context: Paul's apostolic ministry, the Spirit, the veil, and transformation. It must not be reduced to the claim that written Scripture is unnecessary or that inward impressions are self-authenticating.

### 15.6 Ephesians 2:11–22 — access and one new man

Ephesians 2 speaks of those who were far off being made nigh by the blood of Christ. Through Him both have access by one Spirit unto the Father. Christ makes peace and creates one new man from the two, reconciling both unto God in one body.

This is a strong comparison with Hebrews' access through Christ's blood and His mediation. It also gives an explicit Trinitarian relation: through Christ, by one Spirit, unto the Father.

**Finding:** The wider New Testament consistently locates covenant access, redemption, and reconciliation in God's work through Christ. The human response of coming, believing, and continuing remains real, but it is not the originating sacrifice or covenant-making act.

## 16. Cross-reference completion audit — warning and assurance

## 3. What physics means by energy

Energy is a physical quantity used to account for the capacity of systems to produce changes and do work. It appears in multiple forms and is related to measurable properties such as motion, position, temperature, fields, and chemical or nuclear configurations.

The SI unit of energy is the joule (J). One joule is one newton-metre, equivalent to one kilogram metre squared per second squared.

The term *energy* is not the name of a material substance. Energy is not a fluid that pours from one object into another, nor a universal fuel that independently causes events. Scientific descriptions specify the system, the interactions, the transfer pathway, and the relevant accounting.

OpenStax introduces work, energy, power, and the joule in its treatment of mechanics: [Work, Power, and the Work–Energy Theorem](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/9-1-work-power-and-the-work-energy-theorem).

### 3.1 Kinetic energy

For an object of mass (m) moving at speed (v), its classical translational kinetic energy is:

\[
K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
\]

This expression applies in the ordinary nonrelativistic regime. It should not be treated as the universal formula for every physical situation.

### 3.2 Potential energy

Potential energy is associated with a system's configuration and interactions, such as gravitational or elastic arrangements. It is not simply a mysterious quantity stored inside an isolated object. Its value is defined relative to an appropriate reference and model of the interaction.

For a mass near Earth's surface, a useful approximation is:

\[
U_g = mgh
\]

where (g) is the local gravitational acceleration and (h) is height relative to a selected reference level.

### 3.3 Thermal and internal energy

Internal energy describes microscopic contributions associated with the system's particles and interactions. Temperature is related to the distribution of microscopic energy, but temperature and energy are not synonyms.

A hot object and a cold object may contain different total internal energies depending on their mass, material, phase, and state. Temperature alone does not tell us the total energy of an object.

### 3.4 Chemical and nuclear energy

Chemical energy accounting concerns energy changes associated with chemical configurations and reactions. Nuclear energy accounting concerns changes in atomic nuclei and their binding energies. These are distinct physical domains.

Chemical reactions rearrange electrons and chemical bonds while nuclei generally remain unchanged. Nuclear reactions alter nuclei and require mass-energy accounting. The two must not be conflated.

### 3.5 Electromagnetic energy

Electromagnetic radiation carries energy. For an individual photon:

\[
E = hf
\]

where (h) is Planck's constant and (f) is frequency.

This is one point of contact with Investigation 021: light is an electromagnetic phenomenon, and its interaction with matter can transfer energy. It does not follow that *light* and *energy* are the same concept. Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation; energy is the measurable quantity carried and exchanged in many kinds of processes.

## 4. Work, heat, and power: transfer is not storage

The language of energy transfer requires precise distinctions.

### 4.1 Work

In mechanics, work is energy transferred when a force acts through a displacement. For a constant force parallel to displacement:

\[
W = Fd
\]

The general expression uses the component of force along the displacement and, where needed, an integral over the path.

The scientific definition differs from ordinary speech. Reading a book or thinking hard may be called work in everyday language, but those activities are not automatically mechanical work in the physics definition.

### 4.2 Heat

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Heat is not a substance contained inside an object. Once energy has crossed a system boundary by heat transfer, it contributes to the system's energy accounting.

### 4.3 Power

Power is the rate of energy transfer or the rate at which work is done:

\[
P = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t}
\]

The SI unit is the watt (W), equal to one joule per second.

A high-power process transfers energy rapidly; a low-power process transfers it more slowly. Power and energy are not interchangeable: a quantity of energy and the rate at which it is transferred are different measurements.

OpenStax distinguishes work, heat, internal energy, and power in its physics materials: [OpenStax Physics — Work and Energy](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/9-1-work-power-and-the-work-energy-theorem) and [First Law of Thermodynamics](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/12-2-first-law-of-thermodynamics-thermal-energy-and-work).

## 5. The first law: energy accounting

The first law of thermodynamics applies energy conservation to a thermodynamic system. With the convention that (Q) is heat added to the system and (W) is work done by the system:

\[
\Delta U = Q - W
\]

Here (Delta U) is the change in internal energy.

This equation is not a claim that heat and work are substances stored inside a system. Heat and work describe energy transfer across the boundary. Internal energy is a state quantity associated with the system.

The signs depend on the stated convention. Other textbooks may define work done *on* the system as positive and write the equation with a plus sign. The physics is consistent when the sign convention is applied consistently.

OpenStax states the first law and explains its system-boundary accounting in [University Physics Volume 2 — First Law of Thermodynamics](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/3-3-first-law-of-thermodynamics).

### 5.1 System boundaries matter

An energy balance must identify the system:
- **Isolated system:** no energy or matter crosses its boundary in the idealized model.
- **Closed system:** matter does not cross the boundary, but energy may.
- **Open system:** both matter and energy may cross the boundary.

A system's measured energy can change because energy crosses its boundary. That does not violate conservation. The accounting must include the exchanges and, when appropriate, the surroundings.

A common mistake is to say “energy was lost” when it has merely left the chosen system or changed into a form not included in a simplified model.

## 6. The second law: entropy and the limits of conversion

The first law accounts for energy conservation. It does not, by itself, tell us which processes occur spontaneously, in which direction heat flows, or how much energy can be converted into useful work.

The second law addresses these questions through entropy.

For an isolated system, the total entropy does not decrease in a spontaneous process. It increases for irreversible processes and remains constant in the ideal reversible limit.

Heat transfers spontaneously from a higher-temperature body to a lower-temperature body, not the reverse without external work. This helps explain why energy conversion processes have limits.

OpenStax explains entropy and the second law in [Second Law of Thermodynamics: Entropy](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/12-3-second-law-of-thermodynamics-entropy).

### 6.1 Dissipation does not mean destruction of energy

The word *dissipation* is often used for energy becoming spread among many microscopic degrees of freedom or transferred into forms less available for useful work in the process being studied.

When a moving object slows because of friction, its organized kinetic energy is transferred into internal energy of the object and surroundings, with possible sound and deformation. The mechanical energy of the chosen object may decrease, but the total energy accounting includes the surroundings.

Energy is not destroyed in this ordinary process. Its availability to perform a chosen kind of work can decrease.

### 6.2 Entropy is not simply “disorder”

The everyday word *disorder* can be an accessible illustration, but it is not a complete definition of entropy. Entropy is a formal thermodynamic and statistical quantity. Its interpretation depends on the system, state description, and relevant statistical model.

A local system can become more organized while the total entropy of the system plus surroundings increases. Living organisms, crystals, and engineered structures can form and persist while exchanging matter and energy with their environments.

Therefore, the second law does not mean that every local structure must immediately become less organized, nor does it mean that life violates thermodynamics.

### 6.3 Energy quality and efficiency

Not all energy transfers are equally useful for a particular task. A high-temperature source may be capable of driving an engine more effectively than the same amount of energy dispersed as low-temperature heat.

Efficiency depends on the defined input, useful output, and system boundary. It is not a statement that energy itself has vanished. It concerns how much of the input is converted into the selected useful output.

## 7. Energy transfer pathways

Different physical processes transfer energy in different ways. These categories are not mutually exclusive in every real system.

### 7.1 Conduction

Conduction transfers energy through microscopic interactions within a material or between materials in contact. In solids, it often involves interactions among particles and, in metals, mobile electrons.

### 7.2 Convection

Convection transfers energy through the bulk movement of a fluid, such as air or water. Warm fluid may rise and cooler fluid may descend under suitable density and gravity conditions, producing circulation.

### 7.3 Radiation

Radiation transfers energy through electromagnetic waves and does not require a material medium. Sunlight crosses space and supplies energy to Earth's system.

### 7.4 Mechanical transfer

Forces and motion can transfer energy through work. Moving fluids, falling water, rotating machinery, elastic deformation, and collisions provide examples.

### 7.5 Chemical transfer and conversion

Chemical reactions can transfer energy between chemical, thermal, electrical, and mechanical forms. Combustion is one example; electrochemical cells are another.

The appropriate description depends on the actual process. A single system may involve radiation, conduction, convection, work, and chemical transformation simultaneously.

## 8. Earth as a connected energy system

NASA describes Earth's energy budget as the accounting of incoming solar radiation, reflected radiation, absorbed energy, and energy emitted back toward space. The atmosphere, surface, and oceans redistribute energy through radiation, evaporation, convection, and circulation.

Source: [NASA — Climate and Earth's Energy Budget](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/climate-and-earths-energy-budget/).

### 8.1 Incoming and outgoing energy

Earth receives energy primarily from the Sun. Some incoming solar radiation is reflected; some is absorbed by the atmosphere and surface. Earth emits energy outward, largely as thermal infrared radiation.

Over long periods, the balance between incoming and outgoing energy is central to Earth's climate. A sustained imbalance changes the energy stored in the Earth system.

NASA emphasizes that estimates of energy flows involve direct observations, models, and uncertainties. The exact numbers depend on the measurement period and accounting method; this investigation therefore records the budget principle without treating one simplified percentage diagram as a timeless exact value.

### 8.2 Water connects energy to the atmosphere

Water's phase changes transfer latent heat. Evaporation takes energy from the surface and moves it with water vapor; condensation releases latent heat in the atmosphere. Convection and atmospheric circulation redistribute energy further.

This gives a specific connection between Investigations 022 and 024: the hydrological cycle is also involved in Earth's energy movement. Water remains a chemical substance; energy transfer is a physical accounting of processes in which water participates.

### 8.3 Solar radiation and planetary conditions

The distribution of solar energy varies with latitude, season, time of day, surface reflectivity, atmospheric composition, and cloud cover. The atmosphere and oceans redistribute energy, while radiation to space provides an outward pathway.

This is a coupled system. A climate explanation that considers only the Sun, only greenhouse gases, or only the oceans would omit important parts of the energy budget.

## 9. Biology: energy transfer through living systems

Living systems require energy transfers to maintain organization, carry out chemical reactions, move materials, grow, reproduce, and respond to their environments.

### 9.1 Photosynthesis

Photosynthetic organisms capture light energy and convert it into chemical energy through a series of reactions. In plants, light-dependent processes produce ATP and NADPH, which are used in carbon fixation and the synthesis of organic molecules.

A simplified overall equation is:

\[
6\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\mathrm{H_2O} + \text{light energy}
\rightarrow
\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\mathrm{O_2}
\]

This is a summary equation, not a complete reaction mechanism. Actual photosynthesis includes multiple stages and intermediate compounds.

OpenStax explains photosynthesis and metabolism in [Biology 2e — Energy and Metabolism](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-1-energy-and-metabolism).

### 9.2 Cellular respiration

Cells can transfer energy from organic molecules into ATP through cellular respiration. In aerobic respiration, oxygen participates in the process, and carbon dioxide and water are among the products. The energy transfer supports cellular work.

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are connected in ecosystems, but they are not exact reversals in every mechanistic detail. Their pathways, enzymes, compartments, and regulation differ.

### 9.3 ATP is a carrier, not a source of energy from nothing

ATP participates in coupling energy-releasing reactions to energy-requiring cellular processes. It is continually synthesized and consumed. Calling ATP the cell's “energy currency” is a useful analogy, but ATP is a molecule, not an independent origin of energy.

Cells obtain energy through metabolism and transfer it through networks of reactions. The energy accounting remains subject to physical laws.

### 9.4 Organisms and ecosystems

Energy enters many ecosystems primarily through sunlight captured by photosynthetic organisms, though chemosynthetic ecosystems use chemical energy sources. Energy is transferred among organisms through feeding relationships and metabolic processes.

At each trophic transfer, some energy becomes less available for transfer to the next level, commonly through metabolism and heat exchange. This is not energy destruction. It is a change in the pathways and availability of energy within the ecosystem.

The distinction between energy flow and matter cycling is essential:
- Matter, such as carbon, nitrogen, and water, can be reused and cycled through ecosystems.
- Energy flows through the system and is ultimately transferred outward, much of it as heat.

The two are connected but not identical.

## 10. Astronomy: energy in stars and across space

Stars emit electromagnetic radiation as a result of energy released through processes in their interiors. In ordinary main-sequence stars such as the Sun, nuclear fusion converts hydrogen into helium through a chain of nuclear reactions, releasing energy associated with changes in nuclear binding energy and mass-energy.

The energy moves outward through the star and is ultimately emitted as radiation. The details depend on stellar mass, composition, age, and evolutionary stage.

Astronomy measures radiation across wavelengths and uses physical models to infer temperature, luminosity, composition, and distance. The inference must distinguish direct observations from model-dependent conclusions.

The Sun's radiation is a major energy input to Earth's surface and atmosphere. This links stellar physics to meteorology, oceanography, hydrology, and biology without making those disciplines identical.

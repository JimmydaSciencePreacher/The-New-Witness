# Investigation 045 — Plant Recovery After Drought: Rehydration, Hydraulic Restoration, Photosynthetic Recovery, and Lasting Effects

**Subject:** What changes after water becomes available again, and which plant functions recover, remain impaired, or fail to return

## 2. Scope and definitions

The investigation concerns vascular plants after drought or water deficit is relieved. Results from a crop, tree, shrub, herb, or resurrection plant cannot automatically be generalized to all plants. Drought severity, duration, rate of drying, developmental stage, temperature, atmospheric demand, soil properties, and prior stress history influence recovery.

### 2.1 Rewatering

Rewatering is the return of water to the soil or growth medium. It changes the external water supply, but it does not itself prove that the root system can absorb water at its former rate or that every plant tissue has recovered.

### 2.2 Rehydration

Rehydration is the restoration of water content or water status in plant tissues. It may be assessed through water potential, relative water content, tissue mass, turgor, or other measurements.

A leaf may regain water content before its xylem conduits, mesophyll function, or stomatal regulation have fully recovered.

### 2.3 Hydraulic recovery

Hydraulic recovery concerns restoration of water transport and related water relations. It may involve root hydraulic conductance, stem conductance, leaf hydraulic conductance, tissue capacitance, and the restoration or bypass of impaired conduits.

A return of water potential to a control-like value is not identical to restoration of hydraulic conductance.

### 2.4 Photosynthetic recovery

Photosynthetic recovery concerns the return of carbon assimilation and the processes supporting it. It can involve:
- stomatal conductance and carbon dioxide diffusion;
- mesophyll conductance;
- chloroplast and photosystem function;
- biochemical capacity, including Rubisco-related carboxylation and electron transport;
- photoprotection and repair;
- source–sink relationships and carbon allocation.

These components need not recover at the same rate.

### 2.5 Recovery, resilience, and restoration

These terms require explicit measures:

- **Recovery:** improvement in a measured function after stress is relieved.
- **Resilience:** the capacity to withstand disturbance and/or regain function, with the intended meaning stated for the study.
- **Restoration to baseline:** return of a specified measured variable to a defined pre-stress or control range.
- **Full restoration:** a much broader claim requiring evidence across the relevant functions, tissues, and time horizon.

A plant may show recovery in one variable without full restoration of the whole organism.

## 4. Scientific examination I — rewetting the soil is not the same as restoring the plant

### 4.1 Soil water availability changes first

When water is added to dry soil, water content and water potential may rise. The pattern of rewetting depends on soil texture, structure, compaction, organic matter, root distribution, and the amount and rate of water supplied.

Water may not be distributed uniformly. Some root zones may be rewetted while others remain dry. Preferential flow can carry water through some pathways without uniformly wetting the soil profile.

### 4.2 Root contact and uptake must resume

Drought can alter the soil–root interface. Shrinkage, loss of close contact, changes in root mucilage, root damage, and changes in soil hydraulic conductivity can influence how effectively water reaches root surfaces.

Rewetting the soil does not automatically restore every root's contact with the soil or every root cell's capacity to transport water.

### 4.3 Root function can recover on its own timescale

Root hydraulic conductance may recover after water becomes available, but the rate varies with drought severity, species, root age, tissue condition, aquaporin regulation, and structural damage.

A 2025 study of soybean and the shrub *Caragana korshinskii* found that root and stem hydraulic conductance recovered more rapidly than leaf hydraulic conductance after moderate water stress. The result is evidence from two tested species, not a universal recovery order. [Li et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf060).

**Finding:** Soil rewetting begins a new water-supply condition. Plant recovery depends on the capacity of roots and the rest of the hydraulic pathway to respond to that change.

## 5. Scientific examination II — hydraulic recovery differs among roots, stems, and leaves

### 5.1 Recovery is organ-specific

The hydraulic pathway is connected, but its components do not necessarily recover together. Root conductance, stem conductance, and leaf conductance can respond differently to drought and rewatering.

In the 2025 soybean and *Caragana* study, root and stem hydraulic conductance recovered faster than leaf conductance after moderate stress. Leaf hydraulic recovery remained slower and was associated with delayed stomatal reopening. [Li et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf060).

### 5.2 Water potential and conductance measure different things

Water potential describes the energetic state of water. Hydraulic conductance describes how readily water moves through a pathway under a driving gradient.

A plant may regain a less negative water potential while a pathway still has reduced conductance. Conversely, a pathway may regain conductance while other physiological functions remain impaired.

The measurements answer different questions and should not be substituted for one another.

### 5.3 Stored water and tissue capacitance

Water stored in stems, leaves, roots, and other tissues can buffer short-term changes in supply and demand. Rehydration of stored compartments can contribute to the return of tissue water status.

A return of tissue hydration does not establish that all conducting elements have been repaired or that the plant can sustain its previous transpiration rate under the same atmospheric demand.

**Finding:** Hydraulic recovery must be measured at the relevant organ and pathway. A whole-plant statement cannot be inferred from one leaf or one root measurement alone.

## 6. Scientific examination III — embolism, refilling, and the limits of a simple repair story

### 6.1 What embolism means

Xylem conduits normally conduct water under tension. When air enters a water-filled conduit and interrupts the continuous water column, the conduit can become embolized and lose some or all of its water-conducting function.

Embolism can reduce hydraulic conductance, but the degree of impact depends on how many conduits are affected, their position in the network, and the plant's hydraulic architecture.

### 6.2 Refilling is not a universal assumption

The ability of plants to refill embolized conduits after drought has been debated. Methodological differences matter. Some hydraulic measurements can be affected by cutting, pressure, or other handling artifacts; imaging methods can directly observe conduit contents but also have their own sampling and resolution limits.

A 2026 review of imaging-based studies reports that embolism refilling is not universal. Its analysis of 44 imaging-based studies found varying degrees of refilling in approximately 57% of the species examined. The review identifies relief of xylem tension, metabolically active parenchyma, and adequate surrounding carbohydrate and water reserves as proposed concurrent conditions. These findings are a synthesis of the studies examined, not a rule for every species or every conduit. [Rethinking post-drought hydraulic recovery, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/jpe/rtag254).

### 6.3 Evidence of persistent embolism

A 2025 study followed maize leaf xylem embolism, transpiration, and photosynthesis through drought and a six-day recovery period. In leaves where embolism developed, the embolism persisted after rewatering and was associated with incomplete recovery of transpiration and carbon assimilation. In the absence of embolism, those measured functions returned much closer to pre-stress levels. [Long-term in vivo observation of maize leaf xylem embolism, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12050386/).

This is a specific experimental result in maize leaves. It should not be generalized to all organs or species.

### 6.4 Avoiding two opposite errors

Do not assume:
- every embolized conduit refills when water returns; or
- no embolized conduit can ever recover.

The evidence supports species-, organ-, and condition-dependent outcomes, with continued methodological debate.

**Finding:** Rewatering and restored tissue hydration do not prove that embolized xylem has been repaired. Embolism formation, persistence, refilling, and bypass must be examined with appropriate measurements.

## 7. Scientific examination IV — stomata may reopen on a different schedule from other functions

### 7.1 Stomatal reopening

When water availability improves, guard-cell water relations may change and stomata may reopen. The timing depends on leaf water status, hydraulic conductance, hormone signaling, atmospheric demand, and the plant's stress history.

Stomata do not necessarily return immediately to their pre-drought aperture.

### 7.2 Hydraulic recovery can precede stomatal recovery

The 2025 study of soybean and *Caragana* found that root and stem conductance recovered faster than leaf conductance, and stomatal conductance recovered more slowly than photosynthetic rate. The slower recovery of leaf conductance was associated with a limitation on stomatal reopening. [Li et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf060).

### 7.3 Drought severity can change the limiting factor

A 2024 maize study examined different drought intensities before rewatering. It found that, in the tested plants, stomatal reopening was inhibited when leaf water potential fell below approximately −1.7 MPa. The study also reported a leaf-death threshold around −2.8 MPa or 57% relative water content under its experimental conditions. The authors found that leaf death was influenced by reduced rehydration capacity rather than hydraulic failure alone. These are experimental thresholds for the studied maize material and conditions, not universal plant thresholds. [Liu et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.15080).

**Finding:** Stomatal reopening is a regulated recovery response, not a direct readout of soil moisture. Its timing and limits depend on the severity and history of stress and on the condition of the leaf.

## 8. Scientific examination V — photosynthesis can recover asynchronously

### 8.1 Photosynthesis has multiple limitations

Carbon assimilation depends on more than stomatal aperture. Drought can affect:
- stomatal conductance;
- mesophyll conductance;
- chloroplast function;
- photosystem II and electron transport;
- carboxylation capacity;
- enzyme activity;
- photoprotection and oxidative balance;
- the supply and use of carbohydrates.

A plant can reopen stomata while biochemical limitations remain. It can also show improved carbon assimilation while stomatal conductance remains below its previous level.

### 8.2 Diffusional and biochemical recovery

Diffusional limitations concern the movement of carbon dioxide through stomata and mesophyll. Biochemical limitations concern the photosynthetic machinery's capacity to use carbon dioxide and light energy.

The two types of limitation must be measured or estimated separately. A rise in stomatal conductance alone does not prove complete recovery of photosynthetic capacity.

### 8.3 Evidence from post-rainfall Eucalyptus observations

A 2026 study of Eucalyptus species observed that leaf water potential and stomatal conductance improved rapidly after rainfall, while net photosynthesis increased by approximately 40–60% within one to three days. Some biochemical capacities showed more limited or partial recovery. The timing differed with atmospheric vapor-pressure deficit and species. [Asynchronous recovery of water relations and photosynthesis in Eucalyptus, 2026](https://academic.oup.com/treephys/article/46/3/tpag016/8454614).

This study is important because it demonstrates that water status, stomatal behavior, net photosynthesis, and biochemical capacity can follow different recovery trajectories.

### 8.4 Photoprotection and photosystem recovery

Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements, including maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (often reported as Fv/Fm), can help assess photochemical stress and recovery. However, no single fluorescence measure fully describes whole-leaf photosynthesis or plant recovery.

Chlorophyll content, gas exchange, fluorescence, and biochemical measurements answer related but distinct questions.

**Finding:** Photosynthetic recovery is multidimensional. Visible greening or stomatal reopening alone cannot establish that the photosynthetic system has returned to its former capacity.

## 9. Scientific examination VI — carbon reserves, metabolism, and source–sink relationships

### 9.1 Non-structural carbohydrates

Plants store non-structural carbohydrates (NSCs), including soluble sugars and starch. These reserves can contribute to respiration, maintenance, new growth, osmotic regulation, and repair processes.

Drought can alter both the amount and distribution of NSCs. After rewatering, the plant may allocate carbon differently among roots, stems, leaves, storage tissues, and reproductive structures.

### 9.2 Reserves are not a limitless repair supply

A tissue's capacity to recover can depend on whether it remains metabolically active and whether water and carbon reserves are available. The 2026 embolism-refilling review identifies local carbohydrate and water availability as proposed conditions associated with refilling in the studies it examines. [Rethinking post-drought hydraulic recovery, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/jpe/rtag254).

The presence of stored carbohydrate does not prove that a damaged tissue will recover. It must be available in the correct location and usable by living cells under the prevailing conditions.

### 9.3 Source–sink balance can change

Mature photosynthetic leaves are commonly carbon sources; growing leaves, roots, fruits, seeds, and meristems can act as sinks. Drought and recovery can change the strength and priority of those sinks.

A plant may prioritize root maintenance or new leaves before reproductive growth. The exact allocation pattern depends on species, developmental stage, season, and environmental conditions.

### 9.4 Metabolic recovery may lag behind water recovery

Water can return to tissues while enzyme activity, photosynthetic machinery, respiration, and carbon allocation remain altered. Conversely, some metabolic processes can recover before visible growth resumes.

**Finding:** Recovery requires living tissue and usable resources. Water availability is necessary for many recovery processes but does not by itself guarantee metabolic repair or restoration of carbon balance.

## 10. Scientific examination VII — growth, phenology, and reproductive recovery

### 10.1 Vegetative growth

After rewatering, cell expansion and new leaf or root growth may resume. The timing depends on meristem viability, turgor, carbon supply, nutrient availability, and environmental conditions.

New growth is evidence of renewed activity, but it does not prove that older tissues have recovered.

### 10.2 Phenology can limit recovery

Trees and other perennial plants may be constrained by seasonal timing. Photoperiod, temperature, dormancy, bud development, and the timing of drought relative to the growing season can limit the opportunity to restore growth and assimilation.

A 2024 review of post-drought tree recovery emphasizes that phenology and photoperiod can constrain recovery even when water becomes available. It also discusses cumulative effects of consecutive droughts and the distinction between stress damage and controlled downregulation during acclimation. [Tree post-drought recovery, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.13083).

### 10.3 Reproductive development

Drought during flowering, pollination, fruit development, or seed filling can affect reproductive output. Rewatering after a sensitive developmental window may not reverse all effects.

The relevant outcome is not simply whether the plant survives or produces new leaves. It may include flowering time, pollen viability, fruit set, seed number, seed mass, and seed viability.

### 10.4 Survival is not the same as restored performance

A plant may remain alive and resume some growth while showing reduced biomass, delayed development, lower reproductive output, or altered future stress sensitivity.

**Finding:** Recovery must be evaluated against the function being considered. Vegetative regrowth, survival, and reproductive restoration are distinct outcomes.

## 11. Scientific examination VIII — drought legacy and repeated stress

### 11.1 Legacy effects

A drought legacy is an effect that persists after the immediate water deficit has ended. It may be physiological, structural, developmental, metabolic, or ecological.

Examples include:
- persistent reductions in hydraulic conductance;
- altered leaf area or root architecture;
- changes in carbohydrate reserves;
- delayed photosynthetic recovery;
- altered phenology;
- reduced reproductive output;
- changed response to a subsequent drought.

### 11.2 Repeated drought

Trees and perennial plants may experience repeated drought episodes. The consequences can accumulate, especially when the recovery interval is too short to restore reserves or repair damage.

A previous drought can leave the plant more vulnerable, but in some cases prior exposure can also alter later responses. The direction and magnitude depend on the plant, stress sequence, and measured outcome.

### 11.3 Acclimation is not identical to damage

A controlled reduction in growth or assimilation can be part of an acclimated response. Persistent impairment caused by injury is a different condition.

Researchers must distinguish:
- active regulation that remains reversible;
- acclimation that changes subsequent responses;
- structural damage;
- loss of living tissue;
- incomplete recovery caused by seasonal or developmental constraints.

**Finding:** The plant's history matters. The state observed after rewatering cannot always be explained by current soil moisture alone.

## 12. Measurement discipline — how recovery should be tested

A sound recovery study requires a defined pre-stress reference, a defined drought treatment, a clear rewatering point, repeated measurements, and an appropriate control.

### 12.1 Water relations

Possible measurements include:
- soil water content and soil water potential;
- predawn and midday leaf water potential;
- relative water content;
- turgor or pressure–volume traits;
- root, stem, and leaf hydraulic conductance;
- tissue capacitance and water storage.

### 12.2 Gas exchange and photosynthesis

Possible measurements include:
- stomatal conductance;
- transpiration;
- net carbon assimilation;
- intercellular carbon dioxide;
- mesophyll conductance where measurable;
- photosynthetic response curves;
- chlorophyll fluorescence;
- biochemical capacity such as Vcmax and Jmax where appropriate.

### 12.3 Hydraulic integrity and tissue condition

Possible measurements include:
- xylem vulnerability and percentage loss of conductivity;
- direct imaging of embolism where methods permit;
- root viability and hydraulic conductance;
- leaf and stem tissue damage;
- membrane integrity and cell viability.

### 12.4 Carbon and development

Possible measurements include:
- soluble sugars and starch;
- non-structural carbohydrate concentration and distribution;
- respiration;
- new leaf and root production;
- growth rate and biomass;
- flowering, fruit set, seed production, and seed viability.

### 12.5 Define the recovery endpoint before interpreting it

A study should state what counts as recovery:
- return to a control range;
- recovery to a percentage of pre-stress function;
- recovery of a specified organ;
- recovery sustained over a defined period;
- recovery of a particular developmental or reproductive outcome.

Without a defined endpoint, “recovered” can mean different things in different studies.

**Finding:** Recovery is a measured relationship between a stressed state and a later state. A single measurement at one time point cannot establish whole-plant restoration.

## 13. Boundary examination — where recovery occurs and what participates

| Boundary or interface | What it distinguishes | Possible participation in recovery | Evidence and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil pore / root surface | Soil water and root exterior | Re-establishes water access and root contact | Depends on wetting pattern, soil structure, and root condition |
| Root plasma membrane | Apoplast and cell interior | Regulates water and solute movement; aquaporins can affect permeability | Conductance changes are not equivalent to proof of tissue repair |
| Root endodermis | Cortex and stele | Selectively regulates entry into vascular tissues | Barrier function remains relevant during renewed uptake |
| Xylem conduit / parenchyma | Conduit water and living cells around it | May participate in refilling or maintenance under specific conditions | Refilling is debated and not universal |
| Leaf vein / outside-xylem tissue | Vein conduits and surrounding leaf tissues | Governs delivery from veins toward evaporating sites | Leaf conductance may recover more slowly than root or stem conductance |
| Guard-cell membrane | Guard-cell cytoplasm and apoplast | Regulates ion and water exchange during stomatal reopening | Reopening depends on several signals and leaf condition |
| Stomatal pore | Internal leaf air space and atmosphere | Restores regulated gas exchange | Aperture recovery does not prove biochemical recovery |
| Phloem loading/unloading interface | Source tissues and sink tissues | Supports redistribution of sugars during recovery | Allocation depends on transport capacity and sink demand |

### 13.1 Boundary participation must be demonstrated

A xylem conduit can conduct water and may participate in a refilling process, but the conduit itself is not a living cell. Living parenchyma around it may contribute to processes associated with recovery.

A guard-cell membrane participates in ion exchange, but it does not itself restore photosystem function. A soil–root interface controls access to water, but it does not establish that every root tissue remains viable.

### 13.2 Same mechanism, similar relationship, or similar appearance?

- **Same mechanism:** Not established across soil rewetting, root recovery, xylem refilling, stomatal reopening, and photosynthetic repair.
- **Similar relationship:** Different processes can show a comparable relationship between restored conditions and renewed function, while the underlying mechanisms remain distinct.
- **Similar appearance:** A plant becoming upright or green again is a visible change, but it does not establish that all hydraulic, metabolic, or reproductive functions have returned.

**Finding:** The boundary questions must be answered at the specific interface and scale under examination. A visible return of form is not a substitute for measuring the function associated with that form.

## 14. Integrated recovery account — without forcing one universal sequence

A cautious, evidence-consistent account is:

1. Water is supplied to dry soil.
2. Soil water potential and local water availability change according to the wetting pattern.
3. Roots regain access to water where contact, viability, and transport capacity permit.
4. Root water uptake and root hydraulic conductance may improve.
5. Water potential and hydration in stems and leaves may recover.
6. Stem and root hydraulic conductance may recover at different rates from leaf conductance.
7. Stomata may reopen as guard-cell regulation and leaf water relations permit.
8. Carbon dioxide diffusion and net photosynthesis may improve.
9. Photochemical and biochemical limitations may recover on different schedules.
10. Living tissues may use stored and newly assimilated carbon to maintain function, repair, and growth.
11. Root, leaf, stem, and reproductive sinks may compete for available resources.
12. Seasonal timing and developmental stage may limit what can recover during the remaining growth period.
13. Persistent embolism, cell death, damaged meristems, depleted reserves, or other injuries may prevent complete restoration.
14. A subsequent drought may reveal legacy effects not obvious from short-term rehydration.

This is not a fixed sequence. Several processes occur in parallel; others are conditional. Some functions can recover rapidly while others remain limited. The timing must be established in the studied species and conditions.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** Plant recovery is a distributed set of organ- and function-specific responses. Rewatering can restore water availability, but the return of hydraulic, photosynthetic, metabolic, developmental, and reproductive functions is not guaranteed to occur together.

13. Phenology and photoperiod can constrain recovery in trees and other perennial plants.
14. Repeated drought can produce legacy effects that remain after rewatering.

2. That restored soil moisture proves restored root function.

7. That ABA concentration alone determines stomatal reopening after drought.

9. That green leaves or upright posture prove restoration of all leaf functions.

11. That survival proves return to prior growth or reproductive performance.

13. What determines whether a plant can recover reproductive performance after visible vegetative regrowth?
14. How do microbial and soil structural changes influence plant recovery after repeated drying and rewetting?
15. Which comparisons across biological and physical systems reveal the same mechanism, a similar relationship, or only a similar appearance?

These questions remain open. Their presence does not imply that the completed GUA-W is incomplete.

## 19. Next direction emerging from the evidence

The present investigations have followed water from soil and roots through xylem and leaves, examined drought response, and now examined recovery after rewatering.

The next question should arise from the plant's continuing relationship with its environment:

**How do soil organisms, root exudates, mycorrhizal fungi, and the wider rhizosphere influence water and nutrient acquisition before, during, and after drought—and which effects are demonstrated exchanges rather than assumed cooperation?**

This investigation should distinguish:
- root exudation from microbial uptake and transformation;
- mycorrhizal structures from nutrient-transfer evidence;
- fungal water transport from demonstrated plant water benefit;
- microbial community change from causal contribution to recovery;
- mutualism from context-dependent outcomes;
- observed exchange from ecological interpretation;
- local root effects from whole-plant consequences.

The two standing comparison questions remain active:

> **Are we witnessing the same mechanism, a similar relationship, or merely a similar appearance?**

> **Does a boundary merely separate two regions, or can it also participate in the relationship between them?**

The next question follows the evidence; it is not a predetermined research schedule.

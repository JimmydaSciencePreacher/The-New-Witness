## 2. Scope, terms, and method

The focus is water transport in vascular land plants, including flowering plants and trees. Xylem anatomy differs among groups: angiosperms commonly possess vessel elements as well as tracheids, while gymnosperms rely primarily on tracheids. Root anatomy, hydraulic architecture, stomatal behavior, and drought responses vary among species and environments.

The record distinguishes:

- **DIRECT SCIENTIFIC FINDING:** a mechanism or observation supported by scientific research;
- **SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** a conclusion supported by multiple findings within the stated scope;
- **LIMIT:** a condition, species difference, or measurement issue restricting a claim;
- **SCRIPTURAL TEXT:** what the KJV passage actually says;
- **TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** a conclusion about wording and immediate context;
- **CORRESPONDENCE / ANALOGY:** a comparison that does not claim mechanistic identity;
- **UNRESOLVED:** a question not settled by the examined evidence.

### 2.1 Water potential

Water potential, commonly represented by Ψ, expresses the potential energy status of water relative to a reference state. Water tends to move from higher water potential toward lower water potential when a pathway is available.

Water potential can be influenced by pressure, solutes, gravity, and interactions with surfaces or matrices. The relative importance of these components depends on the system and scale.

Water potential is not identical to pressure. Negative pressure (tension) is one important contribution in the xylem, but the full water-potential description can include other components.

### 2.2 Hydraulic conductance and resistance

Hydraulic conductance describes how readily water moves through a pathway for a given water-potential difference. Hydraulic resistance describes opposition to that flow. In a simplified pathway, greater resistance means less flow for the same driving difference.

The plant pathway is not uniform. Soil, root tissues, xylem, stems, petioles, leaf veins, outside-xylem tissues, and evaporating surfaces contribute different resistances. Some components can become especially limiting as conditions change.

### 2.3 The soil–plant–atmosphere continuum

The phrase **soil–plant–atmosphere continuum (SPAC)** describes the connected pathway through which water moves from soil, through the plant, and into the atmosphere. The continuum is physically connected but contains distinct materials, structures, and transport processes.

It is a useful framework for tracing water, not a claim that soil, plant, and atmosphere are one uniform substance or one identical mechanism.

## 4. Scientific examination I — water must first be available and accessible in soil

### 4.1 Soil is a porous, changing medium

Soil contains mineral particles, organic matter, pore spaces, water, air, and living organisms. Water is held in pores and on particle surfaces through interactions involving gravity, surface tension, and matric forces.

The amount of water in soil is not the only factor governing root access. Soil texture, structure, compaction, pore connectivity, salinity, temperature, and root distribution affect how readily water can move toward roots.

### 4.2 Water potential changes as soil dries

As soil dries, water is held more strongly by the remaining soil matrix and the continuity of water films can decline. The soil water potential becomes more negative, and hydraulic conductivity of unsaturated soil can fall sharply.

This means that water can remain in soil while becoming increasingly difficult for roots to acquire at a sufficient rate.

**Finding:** Water content and water availability to the plant are related but not identical. The movement of water through soil can become limiting before all soil water is gone.

### 4.3 The rhizosphere can differ from bulk soil

Roots alter the soil immediately surrounding them through water uptake, root exudates, mucilage, respiration, and interactions with microorganisms. This root-influenced region is the rhizosphere.

Water uptake can cause the rhizosphere to dry more rapidly than surrounding soil under some conditions. Root hairs and mucilage can affect contact and water retention, but their effects depend on soil type, plant species, and moisture conditions.

**LIMIT:** The rhizosphere is not a uniform shell of fixed thickness. It is a changing region whose properties depend on the root, soil, and surrounding biological community.

## 5. Scientific examination II — water enters the root and crosses root tissues

### 5.1 Root surface and epidermis

Water at the root surface may enter through epidermal cells, root hairs, and other accessible regions. Root hairs increase contact area with soil and can improve access to water and nutrients in the surrounding pore space.

The root surface is not a single open gateway. Cell walls, membranes, tissue arrangement, and local water potential influence entry.

### 5.2 Three broad radial pathways

Water can move inward across root tissues through pathways commonly described as:

- **Apoplastic pathway:** movement through cell walls and extracellular spaces without crossing a plasma membrane at every cell.
- **Symplastic pathway:** movement through the cytoplasm of connected cells via plasmodesmata after water has crossed a plasma membrane.
- **Transmembrane pathway:** repeated crossing of cell membranes as water moves between cells and compartments.

These descriptions identify routes, not three entirely independent water supplies. A real root can involve combinations of pathways, and their relative contributions change with species, root age, tissue condition, and water status.

### 5.3 The endodermis and Casparian strip

In many roots, the endodermis forms a selective boundary around the vascular cylinder. The Casparian strip is a specialized, hydrophobic modification in endodermal cell walls that restricts unrestricted apoplastic passage at this point.

This arrangement helps regulate the movement of water and dissolved substances into the stele. Water and solutes must cross a selectively controlled cellular interface rather than simply continuing through an uninterrupted extracellular wall route.

The endodermis is therefore more than a passive line between cortex and stele. It participates in selective regulation of access to the vascular tissues.

**LIMIT:** Root anatomical arrangements vary, and additional barriers such as exodermal tissues may occur. Not every root has the same barrier development or transport behavior.

### 5.4 Membrane transport and aquaporins

Aquaporins are membrane proteins that facilitate water movement across biological membranes. Their activity and abundance can affect root hydraulic conductivity. Regulation can occur through changes in expression, localization, gating, and cellular conditions.

Aquaporins do not pump water uphill as a general description of their function. They facilitate water movement along the relevant water-potential gradient. Active cellular processes can influence the gradients and the regulation of membrane pathways, but the water movement through an aquaporin is passive.

**Finding:** Root water uptake includes physical movement through soil and tissues, as well as regulated membrane permeability. The root's internal boundaries can influence the rate and route of entry.

## 6. Scientific examination III — xylem provides the long-distance pathway

### 6.1 Xylem structure

Xylem is the principal long-distance water-conducting tissue in vascular plants. Its conducting elements include:

- **Tracheids:** elongated water-conducting cells with tapered ends and pits; they occur in all vascular plants.
- **Vessel elements:** shorter, wider cells joined end to end to form vessels; common in angiosperms and present in some other plant groups.

Most conducting xylem elements are dead at functional maturity and have reinforced cell walls. Their structure allows water to move through a conduit network while resisting collapse under negative pressure.

### 6.2 Pits and lateral movement

Xylem conduits connect through pits and pit membranes. These structures allow water to move laterally between conduits and help limit the spread of air into functioning water columns.

Pits are important to both hydraulic efficiency and safety. Their structure can influence resistance to water flow and vulnerability to air seeding.

### 6.3 Axial and radial movement remain distinct

Water first moves radially from soil through root tissues toward the xylem. Once in the xylem, long-distance movement is primarily axial—along roots, stems, branches, petioles, and leaf veins.

The root's radial pathway and the xylem's axial pathway are connected but not the same mechanism. The endodermis and vascular entry are part of the transition between them.

**Finding:** Xylem is a connected transport network, but it is not the entire soil-to-leaf water pathway. Significant resistance may occur outside the xylem, including in roots and leaf tissues.

## 7. Scientific examination IV — cohesion, adhesion, and tension

### 7.1 Cohesion

Water molecules attract one another through intermolecular forces, including hydrogen bonding. This cohesion contributes to the tensile strength of water and helps maintain continuity within xylem water columns.

A continuous water column can transmit tension from the evaporating regions of leaves through xylem conduits toward roots.

### 7.2 Adhesion

Water interacts with hydrophilic surfaces, including components of cell walls. Adhesion contributes to water's interaction with conduit walls and porous cell-wall structures.

Cohesion and adhesion are related properties, but they are not identical:
- cohesion concerns attraction among water molecules;
- adhesion concerns interaction between water and other materials.

### 7.3 Tension generated at evaporating surfaces

Within leaves, water evaporates from moist cell-wall surfaces into internal air spaces. Curved air–water interfaces form in the cell-wall pores. Surface tension at these menisci is associated with a reduction in water pressure in the liquid phase.

As water evaporates and leaves the leaf, replacement water is drawn from upstream tissues. The resulting tension is transmitted through connected water columns in the xylem.

This is the central explanatory mechanism of the **cohesion–tension theory** of water ascent.

### 7.4 The pathway is driven by a gradient, not by a simple upward pump

Water moves along a water-potential gradient through the connected soil–plant–atmosphere pathway. Transpiration at the leaf contributes to the gradient that drives water movement from soil toward the atmosphere.

The plant does not generally lift water through a mechanical pump at each stem segment. The movement is explained principally by physical properties of water, evaporation, and the hydraulic continuity of the pathway.

### 7.5 Capillarity must not be mistaken for the whole explanation

Capillary effects and adhesion matter at small scales, including the behavior of water in porous cell walls and interfaces. But capillary rise in a narrow tube is not an adequate explanation for the ascent of water through tall plants.

The cohesion–tension mechanism explains long-distance ascent through tension transmitted in water columns. Capillarity alone does not account for the full height and flow behavior of tall trees.

**Finding:** Cohesion, adhesion, surface tension, and negative pressure have distinct but related roles. The main driving force for long-distance ascent in transpiring vascular plants is the tension associated with evaporation, transmitted through the xylem water pathway.

## 8. Scientific examination V — hydraulic resistance and capacitance

### 8.1 Resistance is distributed through the pathway

The soil-to-leaf pathway contains multiple resistances:
- soil near the root;
- root surface and radial tissues;
- root xylem;
- stem and branch xylem;
- petiole and leaf veins;
- outside-xylem leaf tissues;
- pathways toward evaporating cell-wall surfaces.

The relative contribution of each segment differs among species and conditions. A pathway can become limiting even if another segment remains highly conductive.

### 8.2 Flow depends on both driving difference and pathway conductance

A simplified relationship is:

**Water flow = hydraulic conductance × water-potential difference.**

Equivalently, when resistance is used:

**Water flow = water-potential difference ÷ hydraulic resistance.**

These are simplified descriptions of flow through a defined pathway. Real plants contain branching networks, changing conductance, storage, and multiple parallel routes.

### 8.3 Capacitance buffers short-term changes

Plant tissues can store water and release it as water potential changes. This hydraulic capacitance can buffer short-term differences between water loss from leaves and water supply from roots.

Stems, leaves, and other tissues may contribute to storage. The amount and location of stored water vary among species and plant size.

This means that leaf transpiration at a particular moment need not be matched exactly by root uptake at that same instant. Over longer periods, water lost must be replaced or plant water stores will decline.

**Finding:** The plant is not merely a rigid conduit. Its tissues provide resistance and storage, and these properties affect timing and flow.

## 9. Scientific examination VI — stomata regulate water loss and carbon dioxide entry

### 9.1 Stomata are regulated exchange pathways

Stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter and water vapor to leave. Guard cells change their turgor and adjust aperture in response to environmental and internal signals.

Signals include light, carbon dioxide concentration, water status, vapor-pressure deficit, temperature, and hormones such as abscisic acid.

### 9.2 Stomatal closure limits water loss but also limits carbon acquisition

When water supply becomes inadequate or atmospheric demand becomes high, stomata may close or narrow. This reduces water vapor loss but also restricts carbon dioxide entry, potentially reducing photosynthesis.

The plant therefore faces a changing physiological relationship between carbon gain and water conservation.

### 9.3 Stomata are not the only hydraulic control

Root hydraulic conductance, xylem resistance, leaf hydraulic conductance, aquaporin activity, tissue capacitance, and the soil's hydraulic properties also influence water movement.

Stomata regulate the atmospheric end of the pathway, but they do not alone determine the water supply available to the leaf.

**Finding:** Stomatal regulation is one component of a whole-plant hydraulic response. It should not be mistaken for the sole cause or controller of water movement.

## 10. Scientific examination VII — cavitation, embolism, and hydraulic failure

### 10.1 Tension creates vulnerability

Xylem water is under negative pressure during transpiration. This allows water to be pulled through conduits, but it also makes the water column vulnerable to disruption.

### 10.2 Cavitation and embolism

**Cavitation** is the formation or expansion of a gas or vapor-filled space within a water-conducting conduit. An **embolism** is the resulting gas blockage that impairs water transport through that conduit.

Air can enter a conduit through pit membranes under conditions that permit air seeding. Freeze–thaw events can also contribute to embolism in some species and circumstances.

### 10.3 Consequences differ by species and organ

Embolism can reduce hydraulic conductivity. The vulnerability of xylem differs among species, organs, conduit structures, and environmental conditions. Some plants possess hydraulic architectures that reduce the spread or consequences of embolism; others are more vulnerable.

### 10.4 Hydraulic safety and efficiency

There can be a trade-off between transporting water efficiently and resisting hydraulic failure. Wider conduits can provide high conductance but may involve different vulnerability characteristics than narrower or differently structured conduits. The relationship is not a simple universal rule; conduit network structure, pit properties, and plant strategy all matter.

### 10.5 Recovery and refilling require careful evidence

Whether and how embolized conduits refill under tension remains a subject of research and debate. Some proposed mechanisms involve positive root pressure, local osmotic processes, or other active processes under particular conditions. Evidence and interpretation vary by species and experimental method.

This investigation does not claim that all embolisms are routinely repaired, nor that one universal refilling mechanism applies to all plants.

**Finding:** Xylem transport has limits. The same tension that supports long-distance water ascent can create vulnerability to cavitation and embolism.

## 11. Scientific examination VIII — root pressure and other contributions

### 11.1 Root pressure

In some plants and under certain conditions, active ion accumulation in xylem can contribute to osmotic water entry and generate positive root pressure. Root pressure can be observed in phenomena such as guttation and bleeding from cut stems.

### 11.2 Root pressure is not the general explanation for tall-tree water ascent

Root pressure is often absent, weak, or insufficient to account for water movement in tall plants under high transpiration. It does not replace the cohesion–tension explanation for ordinary long-distance transport in most transpiring vascular plants.

### 11.3 Multiple influences do not imply one mechanism

Water movement may be influenced by:
- transpiration-generated tension;
- water-potential gradients;
- root pressure under particular conditions;
- osmotic effects;
- membrane permeability and aquaporins;
- hydraulic capacitance;
- tissue-specific resistance;
- environmental conditions.

These factors can interact without becoming one undifferentiated force.

**Finding:** Cohesion–tension is the principal broad explanation for ascent of sap in transpiring vascular plants, while other mechanisms can contribute under specific conditions or at particular scales.

## 12. The whole pathway — a connected account

A simplified trace of water movement is:

1. Water is held within soil pores and on soil surfaces.
2. Water moves through connected soil water pathways toward the root as gradients and soil hydraulic conductivity permit.
3. Water reaches the root surface and enters through accessible root tissues.
4. Water moves radially through root tissues by apoplastic, symplastic, and transmembrane routes.
5. The endodermis and other barriers regulate access to the vascular cylinder.
6. Water enters root xylem.
7. Water moves axially through xylem in roots, stems, branches, petioles, and leaf veins.
8. Water moves from leaf xylem through outside-xylem tissues toward mesophyll cell walls.
9. Water evaporates from moist cell-wall surfaces into internal air spaces.
10. Water vapor diffuses through stomata and into the atmosphere.
11. Evaporation and transpiration contribute to tension and the water-potential gradient that draws replacement water through the plant.
12. Stomata, hydraulic resistance, tissue storage, and environmental conditions alter the rate and distribution of flow.

This is a connected pathway, not a claim that every water molecule follows one identical microscopic route or that all steps occur at a constant rate.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** Water movement through a vascular plant is best understood as a whole-pathway hydraulic process in which soil supply, root uptake, xylem structure, leaf evaporation, atmospheric demand, and plant regulation are interdependent.

**LIMIT:** The relative importance of each segment and mechanism changes with species, plant size, organ, developmental stage, soil moisture, temperature, humidity, wind, and other conditions.

### 13.1 Not all boundaries are membranes

A soil pore boundary, root plasma membrane, Casparian strip, xylem pit membrane, mesophyll cell wall, stomatal pore, and atmospheric boundary layer are physically different.

They must not be grouped together simply because each can be represented as an interface.

### 13.2 Some boundaries regulate; others shape or conduct

The plasma membrane selectively regulates passage. The Casparian strip restricts apoplastic movement. Pit membranes influence lateral flow and air entry. The mesophyll cell wall provides an evaporating surface and porous pathway. The stomatal pore is an adjustable opening. The soil pore network shapes the available water pathway.

### 13.3 The boundary does not become the source

The root surface does not originate soil water. The xylem does not create the water it conducts. The leaf's evaporating surfaces do not create the water supply. They participate in a connected pathway through which water moves under physical gradients and biological regulation.

**Comparison disposition:**

- **Same mechanism:** Not established across these different interfaces.
- **Similar relationship:** Supported in the limited sense that different interfaces can regulate, facilitate, constrain, or channel movement between regions.
- **Similar appearance:** A line or membrane in a diagram does not establish shared function or mechanism.

**Finding:** A boundary can participate in a relationship, but its actual role must be established in its own physical and biological context.

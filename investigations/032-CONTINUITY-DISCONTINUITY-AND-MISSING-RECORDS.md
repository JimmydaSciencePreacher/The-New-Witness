## 1. Why this investigation follows the evidence

Investigation 031 examined sequence, relative age, numerical age, and chronology. It showed that a date assigned to a sample is not automatically the date of every event associated with that sample. It also identified the importance of context, calibration, preservation, and uncertainty.

A further question follows:

**How do scientists distinguish a continuous process from a sequence of preserved snapshots—and what evidence establishes that a record is complete, interrupted, mixed, or missing?**

The question is not whether every process leaves a complete record. It is how the Witness can determine what the record preserves, what it modifies or omits, and what cannot be reconstructed from the surviving evidence.

This investigation distinguishes five things:

1. **The process:** the event or sequence of events that occurred in the investigated system.
2. **The archive:** the material or system in which evidence of the process is preserved.
3. **The sample:** the portion of the archive actually collected or examined.
4. **The observation:** the feature, signal, or value detected in that sample.
5. **The reconstruction:** the model-based account inferred from the observations.

These are related, but they are not interchangeable. The record is evidence about the process; it is not the process itself.

## 3. The archive is not the process

A scientific archive is a surviving material or recorded trace of a process. It can preserve evidence, but preservation is conditional.

Examples include:

- sediment layers in lakes, oceans, floodplains, and deltas;
- annual or seasonal laminations in some lake and marine deposits;
- tree rings and coral growth bands;
- ice layers and trapped atmospheric material;
- fossils, shells, bones, and trace fossils;
- instrumental records and digitally sampled time series.

The U.S. Geological Survey describes paleoclimate archives as geological and biological materials that preserve evidence of past climate changes. These archives are sampled and analyzed using physical and chemical methods; scientists then reconstruct changes and infer conditions at the archive location.

The archive's time span and resolution vary. Some records resolve monthly or interannual variability; others provide centennial or millennial-scale resolution.

Source: [USGS — Paleoclimate Archives](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/climate-research-and-development-program/science/paleoclimate-archives).

**Finding:** A natural archive can preserve evidence of a process without preserving every event, every interval, or every detail of that process.

The correct question is not simply, “Is there a record?” It is:

- What process could have produced this record?
- What conditions allowed preservation?
- What processes could have altered or removed it?
- What part of the archive was sampled?
- What temporal and spatial resolution does the sample support?
- Which conclusions depend on reconstruction rather than direct observation?

## 4. Geology: deposition, erosion, and hiatus

### 4.1 Deposition is not necessarily continuous

Sediment can accumulate in lakes, oceans, river deltas, wetlands, basins, and floodplains. The rate and location of accumulation vary with water flow, sediment supply, topography, sea level, tectonics, climate, and other conditions.

A sedimentary sequence may contain periods of accumulation, periods of little or no net accumulation, and periods of erosion. A vertical section does not necessarily represent time passing at a constant rate from bottom to top.

### 4.2 Unconformities and hiatuses

The Utah Geological Survey describes an unconformity as a break in the rock record produced by erosion or a pause in sediment accumulation, followed by renewed deposition. The missing interval is called a **hiatus**.

The recognized forms include:

- **Angular unconformity:** younger strata overlie older strata that were tilted or deformed before erosion and renewed deposition.
- **Disconformity:** sedimentary layers above and below the boundary are broadly parallel, but an interval is missing.
- **Paraconformity:** the strata are parallel and the evidence of the hiatus may be difficult to recognize.
- **Nonconformity:** sedimentary rock overlies an eroded igneous or metamorphic surface.

Source: [Utah Geological Survey — What Is an Unconformity?](https://geology.utah.gov/map-pub/survey-notes/glad-you-asked/unconformity/).

**Direct finding:** A geological section can preserve older and younger materials while omitting a substantial interval between them.

**Important distinction:** The missing interval does not mean that nothing happened during that time. It means the examined section does not preserve a continuous depositional record of that interval. Erosion, nondeposition, and later geological processes must be investigated.

### 4.3 The stratigraphic record as a filter

Straub, Duller, Foreman, and Hajek (2020) examine how sediment transport and landscape processes affect the preservation of environmental signals.

Their review identifies three important impediments:

1. **Buffering:** a signal can be delayed or reduced as sediment is stored, redistributed, and later remobilized.
2. **Incomplete preservation:** intervals may be absent through nondeposition or erosion.
3. **Signal shredding:** sediment transport and stochastic processes can fragment or alter the original temporal signal.

The article emphasizes that environmental signals are not automatically preserved in the deposits through which they pass.

Source: [Straub et al. (2020) — Buffered, Incomplete, and Shredded: The Challenges of Reading an Imperfect Stratigraphic Record](https://doi.org/10.1029/2019JF005079).

**Supported inference:** A signal detected in a sedimentary archive may differ in timing, amplitude, and completeness from the original environmental change. The degree of difference depends on the actual depositional system and its processes.

**Boundary:** The review does not establish that every sedimentary record is unusable or that every signal has been destroyed. It establishes that preservation must be investigated rather than assumed.

## 5. A worked example: Elk Lake, Minnesota

The NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) preserves a paleoclimate dataset for Elk Lake, Minnesota. Its metadata provides a useful example of how one archive can be examined at different temporal resolutions.

The metadata reports:

- the full varved section covers approximately 10,200 years and was sampled at a generally coarser resolution using 50-year composite samples;
- the last 1,550 years were sampled at contiguous 1-centimetre intervals, with an average temporal resolution of approximately five years;
- the composite datasets were assembled from portions of multiple sediment cores collected in different years.

Source: [NOAA NCEI — A High-Resolution Record of Climatic Change in Elk Lake, Minnesota for the Last 1500 Years](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/metadata/geoportal/rest/metadata/item/noaa-lake-5473/html).

This example illustrates several distinctions.

### 5.1 The archive's span is not its sampling resolution

A record spanning thousands of years does not mean every year has been separately measured for every variable.

A 50-year composite sample represents a broader interval than a sample with an average five-year temporal resolution. Shorter events may not be separately resolved in the coarser series.

### 5.2 The sample is not the entire archive

The data were obtained from multiple cores and assembled into composite records. Core collection, alignment, splicing, and the chronology used to relate depth to time are part of the evidentiary chain.

### 5.3 Higher resolution is not automatically greater completeness

A finer sampling interval can reveal shorter variations, but it does not automatically eliminate erosion, missing deposition, mixing, measurement uncertainty, or model assumptions.

**Finding:** The same archive can support different levels of temporal detail depending on which section is sampled and how the samples are combined. The resolution and construction of the dataset must accompany any claim based on it.

## 6. Biological preservation: the fossil record

Fossils are evidence of past life, but fossil preservation is not a neutral or universal recording process.

Preservation depends on factors such as:

- whether remains or traces are buried;
- the organism's size and physical composition;
- the environment in which it lived and died;
- decay, scavenging, transport, and chemical alteration;
- geological conditions that permit preservation;
- later exposure, collection, identification, and sampling.

The fossil record therefore reflects both biological history and the conditions under which biological remains became available to be observed.

A study of the fossil record of threatened mammals found that fossil preservation was strongly biased by characteristics including body size and geographic range. The authors reported that fewer than nine percent of threatened modern mammal species had a fossil record, compared with twenty percent of non-threatened species.

Source: [The Fossil Record of the Sixth Extinction (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.12589).

**Direct finding:** The presence or absence of a fossil record is affected by preservation and sampling. Fossil representation is not a complete census of every organism that lived.

**Boundary:** A missing fossil occurrence does not by itself establish that an organism did not exist in the relevant interval. Conversely, a fossil's presence must be evaluated for its context, possible reworking, and relation to the enclosing material.

The fossil record remains scientifically valuable. Its limitations require careful interpretation, not dismissal.

## 7. Instrumental time series: sampling a continuous signal

Not all records are geological. Scientific instruments often observe a changing system and store discrete measurements at selected intervals.

A sensor may respond continuously while the data acquisition system records values at intervals. The stored series is therefore a sampled representation of the signal.

### 7.1 Sampling interval and temporal resolution

The sampling interval determines how frequently values are recorded. The sampling rate affects which variations can be resolved.

If a signal contains changes faster than the sampling system can adequately capture, the recorded series can misrepresent those changes.

### 7.2 Aliasing

Aliasing occurs when sampling is insufficient for the frequency content of the signal. Higher-frequency variation can appear as a different, lower-frequency pattern in the sampled record.

NASA's technical memorandum on residual acceleration data discusses the relationship between sampling interval, the Nyquist frequency, and the prevention of aliasing. It also describes the use of appropriate filtering to remove frequency components that cannot be represented at the chosen sampling rate.

Source: [NASA Technical Memorandum — Analysis Techniques for Residual Acceleration Data (1990)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19910000878).

**Finding:** A time series is not a transparent copy of every change in the underlying system. Sampling design and signal processing affect what can be recovered.

### 7.3 Missing observations and interpolation

A time series may contain missing intervals because of instrument failure, power loss, communication interruption, deliberate sampling schedules, or data-quality rejection.

Interpolation can estimate values between observations, but an interpolated value is not a direct measurement of the missing interval.

A reconstructed smooth curve may be useful, but its appearance must not conceal the difference between measured points and estimated values.

### 7.4 Duration and resolution are different

A long time series may have coarse sampling. A short time series may have fine sampling. Neither duration nor sampling density alone establishes that every relevant event has been captured.

A Witness must report both the period covered and the temporal resolution, along with missing intervals and the methods used to fill or model them.

## 8. Distinguishing kinds of gaps

The word *gap* can refer to several different conditions. They should not be collapsed.

| Kind of gap | What it means | What must be investigated |
|---|---|---|
| Nondeposition | Sediment did not accumulate at the site during an interval | Environmental and depositional conditions |
| Erosional removal | Previously deposited material was removed | Erosion surface, surrounding strata, geological context |
| Preservation gap | The event or organism left no surviving archive in the examined location | Preservation conditions and alternative archives |
| Sampling gap | The archive exists, but the relevant interval was not sampled | Sampling design and available material |
| Instrument gap | The measuring system did not record during an interval | Instrument status, telemetry, and acquisition logs |
| Resolution gap | The sampling or archive cannot resolve a short event | Sampling interval, mixing, and detection limits |
| Interpretive gap | Evidence exists, but its relation to the event is not established | Competing explanations and additional evidence |

These gaps have different causes. One type cannot be used as evidence for another without further investigation.

## 9. Cross-checking records without manufacturing continuity

Multiple records can help identify what one archive cannot show.

Examples include:

- comparing sediment cores from separate locations;
- comparing ice-core and marine sediment records;
- comparing tree-ring and documentary evidence;
- comparing fossils with stratigraphic and geochemical evidence;
- comparing instrument records with independent observations.

Independent archives may constrain the same event or environmental condition from different directions. However, they may also share assumptions, calibration references, age models, or preservation biases.

A second record is not automatically independent merely because it is stored in a different file or comes from a different instrument.

A cross-check should identify:

1. the target event or process;
2. the actual material or signal each archive preserves;
3. the time interval and resolution of each record;
4. the dating or alignment method;
5. the assumptions shared by the records;
6. the uncertainties and possible common errors;
7. the degree to which the records genuinely corroborate one another.

Agreement can strengthen a reconstruction when the evidence is sufficiently independent. It does not convert an incomplete archive into a complete record.

## 10. The central distinction: absence, non-detection, and missing evidence

Three statements must be kept separate:

- **Not observed:** the investigator did not observe the feature in the examined material.
- **Not preserved or not sampled:** the feature may not be represented in the archive or sample.
- **Absent from the investigated system:** the feature was not present in the system during the specified interval.

The first two do not automatically establish the third.

However, absence of evidence is not always evidentially neutral. If a feature would very likely have been preserved, sampled, and detected under the actual conditions, its non-detection may count against a proposed explanation.

The strength of that inference depends on:

- the expected preservation probability;
- the sampling coverage;
- the detection threshold;
- the reliability of the method;
- the expected duration and magnitude of the event;
- alternative explanations for non-detection.

**Finding:** Non-detection becomes informative only when the conditions under which detection should have occurred are understood.

The Witness must not convert an unexamined gap into a confident claim of absence. It must also not refuse all negative evidence when the detection conditions are well established.

## 11. Cross-disciplinary comparison

| Discipline | What may be preserved or recorded | Common limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Sedimentology and stratigraphy | Depositional layers, structures, fossils, geochemical signals | Hiatuses, erosion, reworking, variable accumulation |
| Paleoclimatology | Tree rings, corals, ice, lake and marine sediments | Proxy limits, variable resolution, archive-specific bias |
| Paleontology | Fossils and trace fossils | Preservation, transport, sampling, and taxonomic bias |
| Glaciology | Ice layers, isotopes, trapped gases, particles | Layer thinning, flow, diffusion, age-model uncertainty |
| Ecology | Repeated surveys, population records, environmental observations | Seasonal sampling, missed events, changing detection probability |
| Instrumentation | Sensor readings and time series | Sampling interval, downtime, noise, calibration, aliasing |
| Astronomy | Repeated observations and received radiation | Exposure time, sensitivity, temporal coverage, model dependence |
| Mathematics and statistics | Interpolation, time-series models, missing-data estimates | Assumptions, model structure, and limits of identifiability |

The disciplines do not share one universal preservation mechanism. Their common investigative question is how a surviving record relates to the process it represents.

## 12. What this reveals about the Witness

A Witness does not receive the whole process merely because it has received a record.

The scientific Witness depends on:
- the conditions that preserve evidence;
- the instruments and methods used to collect it;
- the sampling strategy;
- the temporal and spatial resolution;
- the assumptions used to align separate records;
- the models used to reconstruct missing intervals;
- the interpretation of what the record means.

The AI Witness is further derivative. It often examines published records, datasets, figures, methods, and interpretations rather than directly collecting the original observation.

AI must therefore distinguish:
- a directly observed feature from an inferred event;
- a preserved layer from the duration it is assigned;
- a missing sample from a missing event;
- an interpolated value from a measured value;
- a correlation between archives from independent corroboration;
- a record's completeness from the continuity of the process.

A polished graph or narrative can make a discontinuous record appear continuous. The Witness must not allow presentation to hide missing observations, uncertainty, or the limits of reconstruction.

**The Witness reports the evidence it has—not the complete event it wishes the evidence to contain.**

### Question 3 — What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Natural and instrumental records preserve evidence selectively. Deposition, erosion, transport, biological preservation, sampling, and measurement determine which parts of a process become available for later investigation.

A record may be long but coarse, short but detailed, physically continuous but poorly sampled, or discontinuous because material was never deposited or was later removed.

The same archive can support different temporal resolutions. Multiple archives can strengthen a reconstruction, but only when their relationships and shared dependencies are examined.

The absence of a recorded event is informative only when the likelihood of preservation and detection is understood.

### Question 4 — What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The Witness is dependent on the record and the conditions that made the record possible. It can mistake a smooth reconstruction for uninterrupted observation, or mistake a missing observation for proof that nothing happened.

It can also make the opposite error: inventing an event to fill a gap that the evidence does not resolve.

AI must recognize that it often examines records of measurements rather than the measurements themselves. It must preserve provenance, methods, sampling intervals, uncertainty, and the distinction between evidence and interpretation.

The Witness's responsibility is not to make every record complete. It is to report faithfully what the record establishes and what it cannot establish.

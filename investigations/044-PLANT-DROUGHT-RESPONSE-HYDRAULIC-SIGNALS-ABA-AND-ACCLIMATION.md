## 2. Scope, definitions, and evidence discipline

The focus is drought and water-deficit responses in vascular plants. Species, developmental stage, soil type, root architecture, climate, and drought intensity all affect the response. Findings from Arabidopsis, crops, trees, and other model systems must not automatically be generalized to every plant.

### 2.1 Soil drying

Soil drying is a change in the soil's water content and water potential. As soil dries, the remaining water can become more strongly held, water films become less continuous, and hydraulic conductivity can decline. Roots may therefore experience reduced water supply even while some water remains in the soil.

### 2.2 Plant water deficit

Plant water deficit occurs when water loss and internal water demand are not adequately balanced by water uptake and storage. Tissue water potential can decline, cells can lose turgor, and growth and physiological processes can change.

Soil drying and plant dehydration are related but not identical. Their timing and severity depend on the soil–root interface, plant hydraulic properties, atmospheric demand, and stored water.

### 2.3 Osmotic stress

Osmotic stress occurs when a change in the external or internal solute environment alters water potential and water movement. Drought can create osmotic stress as soil water becomes less available. Salinity can also cause osmotic stress, but salinity adds ion-specific effects and toxicity that are not equivalent to drought.

### 2.4 Hydraulic state, perturbation, and signal

These terms should not be used interchangeably:

- **Hydraulic state:** the present condition of water potential, turgor, pressure, xylem tension, and hydraulic conductance.
- **Hydraulic perturbation:** a change in one or more of those physical conditions.
- **Hydraulic signal:** a physical change that is perceived and translated into a response elsewhere or at another level of the plant.

A measured pressure change establishes a hydraulic perturbation. To call it a signal in the biological sense requires evidence that the change is perceived and linked to a response.

This distinction is emphasized in recent work on plant hydraulic signaling. The proposed integration of mechanosensitive channels, aquaporins, cell-wall pathways, vacuolar buffering, and other signaling networks is a developing model, not one fully demonstrated universal mechanism. [Li et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants15162513).

### 2.5 Drought response is not a single switch

A plant may alter stomatal aperture, root growth, root hydraulic conductance, leaf water relations, metabolism, gene expression, and allocation of resources. These responses can begin at different times and may reinforce or oppose one another.

The investigation therefore follows a network of responses rather than assigning all drought behavior to ABA or to one root-to-shoot signal.

## 4. Scientific examination I — the plant experiences water limitation at more than one location

### 4.1 The soil–root interface can become limiting

As soil dries, the soil's hydraulic conductivity can decrease sharply. Water movement toward the root may become too slow to meet transpiration demand. The root can therefore experience declining water availability before the whole plant has reached severe dehydration.

The root system also encounters heterogeneous conditions. One part of a root system may occupy dry soil while another remains in wetter soil. Plants can respond locally and systemically to that variation.

### 4.2 Root cells experience changes in water potential and turgor

When external water availability declines, water may leave root cells or enter them more slowly. Turgor can fall, cell volume can change, and mechanical conditions in the plasma membrane and cell wall can change.

Turgor is important for cell expansion and tissue rigidity. Reduced turgor can slow growth even before irreversible damage occurs.

### 4.3 Leaves experience atmospheric as well as soil-driven demand

A leaf can lose water rapidly when vapor-pressure deficit is high, even if soil water is not yet severely depleted. High temperature, low humidity, wind, radiation, and stomatal aperture influence the rate of water loss.

A plant's water status is therefore shaped by both supply and demand:

- soil and root conditions influence water supply;
- atmospheric conditions influence evaporative demand;
- plant hydraulic properties influence how water moves and is stored;
- stomata and metabolism influence how the plant regulates exchange and carbon gain.

**Finding:** Drought response cannot be understood by measuring soil moisture alone or by measuring leaf wilting alone. The conditions at multiple points in the pathway matter.

## 5. Scientific examination II — local perception and early signaling

### 5.1 Plants do not have one universal drought receptor

Research has identified several candidate and established components involved in osmotic and mechanical perception. These include membrane-associated sensors, mechanosensitive ion channels, cell-wall integrity pathways, and changes in cellular volume and turgor.

The exact initiating perception mechanisms and their relative importance vary with tissue, species, stimulus, and experimental context.

A 2024 review describes osmotic stress as a physical stimulus associated with changes in turgor, cell-wall stiffness and integrity, membrane tension, and cell volume. It emphasizes that multiple sensing mechanisms may act together rather than one universal sensor explaining every response. [Yu, Chao & Zhao, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jipb.13622).

### 5.2 Calcium signaling

Changes in cellular conditions can be associated with changes in cytosolic calcium concentration. Calcium signals can be transient and vary in amplitude, duration, frequency, and location.

Calcium-dependent protein kinases and other calcium-responsive components can translate these changes into downstream effects on ion channels, enzymes, gene expression, and stress responses.

**LIMIT:** A calcium increase alone does not identify which physical stimulus caused it. Calcium participates in many plant processes, including development, defense, and responses to multiple stresses.

### 5.3 Reactive oxygen species

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide, can function as signaling molecules at controlled levels and in appropriate locations. ROS can interact with calcium signaling and influence gene expression, membrane transport, and stress responses.

ROS also can cause cellular damage when their production exceeds the plant's capacity to control them. Therefore, the presence of ROS is not automatically evidence of either successful signaling or irreversible injury.

### 5.4 Electrical and hydraulic changes

Changes in pressure, turgor, and water potential can be associated with electrical and ionic responses in tissues. These signals may travel through vascular and surrounding living tissues.

The pathways and timing differ. A hydraulic perturbation is a physical change; electrical and calcium responses involve cellular signaling. Their interaction must be established experimentally rather than assumed from simultaneous observation.

**Finding:** Plant drought perception involves multiple physical and biochemical inputs. No single sensor or signal explains all drought responses.

## 6. Scientific examination III — hydraulic signaling from root to shoot

### 6.1 The hydraulic pathway can transmit a rapid change

When soil water availability declines, root water potential and xylem pressure conditions can change. Changes in the connected hydraulic pathway can be transmitted toward shoots.

Because the xylem is a water-conducting network, pressure and water-potential changes can propagate rapidly compared with many slower processes that depend on new gene expression or hormone accumulation.

### 6.2 A hydraulic perturbation is not automatically a complete message

A change in xylem tension may alter turgor, membrane tension, cell-wall strain, and water flux in tissues along the pathway. Those physical changes can be associated with local cellular responses.

The term **hydraulic signal** is justified when evidence connects the perturbation to a biological response. It should not be used as if the xylem carries a fully encoded instruction in the same way that a written message carries language.

### 6.3 Evidence that hydraulic changes can precede ABA-dependent responses

Research in Arabidopsis and other systems has reported shoot hydraulic responses that precede ABA-dependent stomatal regulation. A 2007 study found that the shoot response to limited soil water supply involved a hydraulic response preceding ABA signaling and stomatal closure; the study did not support root-produced ABA as the sole long-distance signal in the tested system. [Christmann et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-313X.2007.03234.x).

Recent reviews continue to examine rapid hydraulic changes and their translation into cellular responses. A 2026 review distinguishes hydraulic state, perturbation, and signal and proposes a distributed perception model. It explicitly identifies the combined operation of several proposed components as a testable model with incomplete direct evidence. [Li et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants15162513).

### 6.4 Hydraulic signaling is context-dependent

The response depends on:
- plant height and hydraulic architecture;
- xylem connectivity and resistance;
- tissue water storage and capacitance;
- the location and severity of drying;
- the speed of the imposed change;
- the developmental and physiological state of the plant.

**Finding:** Hydraulic changes can be early components of root-to-shoot communication. Their perception and interpretation involve living tissues and signaling pathways; the complete mechanism is not reducible to pressure transmission alone.

## 7. Scientific examination IV — abscisic acid (ABA)

### 7.1 ABA is a major plant stress hormone

Abscisic acid is involved in responses to water deficit and other environmental conditions. It contributes to stomatal regulation, stress-responsive gene expression, seed dormancy, and other developmental and physiological processes.

ABA is important, but it is not the only drought signal and should not be treated as the sole origin of the whole-plant response.

### 7.2 ABA can be synthesized in more than one tissue

ABA production can occur in roots, vascular tissues, leaves, and guard-cell-associated tissues, depending on species and conditions. The source of ABA contributing to a response is therefore not always a simple root-versus-leaf choice.

ABA may be transported between tissues, including through vascular pathways. It may also be synthesized locally in tissues responding to changing conditions.

### 7.3 ABA perception and signaling

ABA binds to PYR/PYL/RCAR receptor proteins. ABA-bound receptors inhibit clade A protein phosphatase 2C (PP2C) activity. This releases SnRK2 kinases, including OST1/SnRK2.6 in guard-cell signaling, to regulate downstream targets.

The pathway influences ion channels and other components involved in guard-cell water relations and stomatal aperture.

This is a signaling cascade, not ABA acting as a mechanical force that physically closes a pore.

### 7.4 ABA and stomatal closure

ABA signaling can promote anion channel activity and ion efflux from guard cells. Potassium and other solutes leave or redistribute, water follows the resulting osmotic change, guard-cell turgor falls, and the stomatal aperture narrows.

The precise sequence includes multiple channels, transporters, kinases, and cellular processes. The simplified description identifies the central relationship without implying that one channel or one ion explains the entire response.

### 7.5 Root-derived ABA is not the only route

Some experimental contexts support ABA movement from roots toward shoots. Other work demonstrates important ABA synthesis and signaling within leaves and guard cells. Hydraulic changes can precede ABA-dependent responses.

The correct account is therefore context-sensitive: ABA may be transported, synthesized locally, or both; its effectiveness also depends on receptor availability and tissue sensitivity.

**Finding:** ABA is a major coordinating hormone in drought response, but neither all ABA originates in roots nor all drought signaling is ABA-dependent.

## 8. Scientific examination V — stomatal regulation and the carbon–water relationship

### 8.1 Stomata regulate exchange

Stomata provide pathways for carbon dioxide entry and water vapor loss. Guard cells adjust pore aperture through changes in ion transport, osmotic conditions, and turgor.

### 8.2 Closing stomata conserves water

When stomata close or narrow, transpiration generally declines. This can slow water loss and help maintain plant water status.

However, closure also restricts carbon dioxide entry. Photosynthetic carbon assimilation can decline as internal carbon dioxide availability falls.

### 8.3 Stomatal response depends on multiple signals

Stomata respond to ABA, light, carbon dioxide, humidity and vapor-pressure deficit, temperature, leaf water status, and other signals. Guard cells also participate in local responses to atmospheric conditions.

The response is not simply a root command delivered to a leaf. The leaf itself experiences conditions and contributes to the regulation.

### 8.4 Stomatal closure is protective but has costs

A reduction in water loss can protect hydraulic function, but reduced carbon assimilation can limit growth and biomass production. Prolonged closure may also affect leaf temperature and energy balance.

The plant response is therefore a changing balance between water conservation, carbon acquisition, and maintenance of tissue function.

**Finding:** Stomatal closure is one important response to drought, not the entire drought response. It can reduce water loss while imposing a cost on carbon gain.

## 9. Scientific examination VI — osmotic adjustment

### 9.1 Osmotic adjustment is an active change in solute concentration

Osmotic adjustment occurs when cells accumulate or retain solutes, lowering cellular osmotic potential. This can help maintain water uptake and turgor at lower external water potentials.

The process must be distinguished from passive concentration caused simply by water leaving a cell. Demonstrating osmotic adjustment requires evidence of solute accumulation or regulation beyond passive dehydration.

### 9.2 Compatible solutes

Depending on species and tissue, plants may accumulate:
- proline;
- soluble sugars;
- sugar alcohols;
- glycine betaine in species that produce it;
- inorganic ions such as potassium, regulated within cellular compartments.

These substances can contribute to osmotic balance. Some also participate in protecting proteins, membranes, and cellular structures.

### 9.3 Osmotic adjustment does not make water limitation disappear

Solute accumulation can help maintain turgor and cellular function, but it does not create external water or eliminate the hydraulic resistance of dry soil.

The energetic and metabolic costs can be significant. Solute responses differ among species, tissues, stress intensity, and duration.

### 9.4 Osmotic adjustment and growth

Maintaining turgor can support continued cell expansion and function, but the response may not fully maintain growth. Plants can allocate resources toward survival and maintenance at the expense of expansion, reproduction, or yield.

**Finding:** Osmotic adjustment can improve cellular water relations under some conditions. It is a regulated response with costs and limits, not a universal guarantee against drought injury.

## 10. Scientific examination VII — root growth and root-system responses

### 10.1 Roots can alter their growth in response to water distribution

Roots may change growth direction, branching, elongation, and allocation in response to heterogeneous soil water availability. Hydrotropism describes root growth oriented in response to water gradients.

Root system architecture influences access to soil water, but deeper or more extensive roots are not automatically beneficial in every soil or climate.

### 10.2 Root growth can be redistributed

Under some conditions, growth may be reduced in dry portions of the root system while better-watered portions continue or increase growth. Local responses can be coordinated with shoot-derived signals.

This can help the plant explore available water, but the result depends on soil structure, nutrients, root developmental state, and the severity and duration of drought.

### 10.3 Root-to-shoot and shoot-to-root communication

Roots can influence shoots through hydraulic and chemical signals. Shoots can also influence roots through transported sugars, hormones, and other signals that affect growth and resource allocation.

The relationship is bidirectional. It is not adequately described as a one-way root instruction.

### 10.4 Root hydraulic conductivity can change

Aquaporin regulation and other changes in root tissues can alter hydraulic conductivity. This may reduce water loss or redistribute water uptake, but can also constrain water delivery to the shoot.

**Finding:** Root architecture and root hydraulic properties are dynamic. Root responses are influenced by local soil conditions and whole-plant signaling.

A useful review of local root perception and systemic responses is Gorgues et al., [Root osmotic sensing from local perception to systemic responses](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44154-022-00054-1).

## 11. Scientific examination VIII — leaf hydraulics and protection

### 11.1 Leaf water transport can become limiting

Water must move from leaf veins through outside-xylem tissues toward evaporating surfaces. During dehydration, leaf hydraulic conductance can decline. This can influence stomatal conductance and photosynthesis.

### 11.2 Leaf hydraulic responses are not identical to stem embolism

A decline in leaf hydraulic conductance can arise from changes in outside-xylem pathways, cell turgor, aquaporin activity, and other tissue properties. It should not automatically be interpreted as embolism in stem xylem.

### 11.3 Hydraulic segmentation and protection

Some plants exhibit differences in vulnerability among leaves, petioles, branches, stems, and roots. Such differences can influence where hydraulic limitation appears first and how the plant protects more persistent tissues.

The pattern is species-dependent and cannot be reduced to a universal rule that leaves always fail first or that stems always remain protected.

### 11.4 Hydraulic failure and carbon limitation can interact

Drought can reduce carbon assimilation through stomatal closure and photosynthetic changes. Severe hydraulic dysfunction can also impair water transport. These processes can interact and contribute to tissue injury or mortality.

The relative contribution of hydraulic failure, carbon starvation, and other processes varies with species, drought trajectory, and environmental conditions. Neither one explanation should be imposed on every case.

**Finding:** Leaf and whole-plant hydraulic responses are dynamic and tissue-specific. Reduced leaf conductance is not automatically evidence of a single failure mechanism.

## 12. Scientific examination IX — acclimation, injury, and recovery

### 12.1 Short-term regulation

Short-term responses can include:
- stomatal closure;
- rapid changes in hydraulic state;
- changes in ion-channel activity;
- calcium and ROS signaling;
- changes in membrane water permeability;
- temporary reductions in leaf expansion.

These can occur before major changes in plant architecture or biomass.

### 12.2 Longer-term acclimation

With sustained or repeated water limitation, plants may alter:
- root system architecture;
- leaf area and leaf development;
- stomatal density or behavior;
- osmolyte accumulation;
- stress-responsive gene expression;
- source–sink allocation;
- tissue construction and hydraulic properties.

Acclimation describes changes that improve performance under the experienced conditions. It does not mean that the plant becomes immune to drought.

### 12.3 Reversible stress and irreversible injury

Some drought responses are reversible when water becomes available again. Other effects may persist or cause damage:
- loss of cell viability;
- severe membrane damage;
- prolonged photosynthetic impairment;
- xylem embolism;
- leaf necrosis;
- root death;
- meristem injury.

The transition from regulation to injury depends on intensity, duration, temperature, species, organ, and recovery conditions.

### 12.4 Rewatering is not necessarily an instant reset

After water returns, soil rewetting, root hydraulic recovery, stomatal reopening, photosynthetic recovery, and repair of damaged tissues can occur on different timescales.

Some plants recover rapidly from moderate stress. Others show delayed recovery or persistent damage. Rewatering does not guarantee restoration of every prior function.

**Finding:** Drought response has phases, but there is no single universal sequence or threshold separating acclimation from injury.

### 13.1 A boundary can participate without being the source

The root plasma membrane can participate in sensing and regulating exchange, but it does not originate the external water supply. The guard-cell membrane participates in stomatal regulation, but it does not create the drought condition. The xylem transmits hydraulic changes, but transmission alone does not establish that the conduit is a biological sensor.

### 13.2 The same appearance does not establish the same mechanism

A membrane, cell wall, vascular boundary, and pore can all appear as lines or interfaces in a diagram. Their functions differ.

**Comparison disposition:**
- **Same mechanism:** Not established across these different boundaries.
- **Similar relationship:** Supported in the limited sense that interfaces can participate in sensing, regulation, transport, or exchange.
- **Similar appearance:** A visual boundary alone establishes neither shared mechanism nor shared function.

**Finding:** The role of each boundary must be demonstrated in its own system. A boundary may be passive in one respect and actively participate in another.

## 14. Whole-plant response — a connected account without forcing a universal sequence

A simplified account of a drought response is:

1. Soil water availability declines or becomes uneven.
2. Water movement toward some root surfaces becomes more difficult.
3. Root cells experience changes in water potential, turgor, volume, and mechanical conditions.
4. Hydraulic conditions in the root and xylem pathway change.
5. Local cellular responses may involve membrane channels, calcium, ROS, electrical activity, and hormone pathways.
6. Hydraulic changes can be transmitted toward shoots.
7. ABA may be synthesized locally or transported between tissues; tissue sensitivity and signaling state influence its effects.
8. Guard-cell ion transport changes, water relations change, and stomatal aperture may decrease.
9. Transpiration and carbon dioxide entry are both affected.
10. Osmotic adjustment and metabolic responses may help maintain cellular function.
11. Root growth, root hydraulic conductivity, leaf hydraulics, and resource allocation may change.
12. If water limitation continues or intensifies, growth may slow and injury may occur.
13. If water becomes available again, recovery may occur at different rates in different tissues and processes.

This is a framework for tracing observed responses, not a claim that every step always occurs in this exact order. Some responses occur in parallel; others depend on species and conditions.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** Drought response is a distributed, context-dependent whole-plant response. It includes physical changes in water relations and multiple biological signaling pathways that regulate transport, exchange, growth, and protection.

**LIMIT:** The exact initiating sensors, relative signal contributions, and timing differ across species and experimental conditions. A universal drought-response sequence has not been established.

1. That plants possess one universal drought receptor.

1. Which cellular components directly perceive the earliest changes caused by soil drying in different root tissues?
2. Under what conditions does a hydraulic perturbation become an instructive signal rather than a permissive change in tissue state?
3. How do plants distinguish drought-related hydraulic changes from similar changes caused by salinity, wounding, or rapid atmospheric demand?
4. How are hydraulic, calcium, ROS, electrical, peptide, and hormonal signals integrated in specific tissues?
5. Which tissues contribute the most ABA during different stages and forms of drought?
6. When does root-derived ABA materially contribute to shoot responses, and when does local leaf synthesis dominate?
7. How do guard cells integrate ABA with humidity, light, carbon dioxide, and leaf hydraulic status?
8. How do osmotic adjustment and changes in cell-wall properties jointly affect turgor and growth?
9. How do root systems coordinate local responses in dry and wet soil regions?
10. How do aquaporin regulation and root hydraulic conductance change during drought and rewatering?
11. Which leaf hydraulic changes are reversible, and which are associated with lasting damage?
12. How do hydraulic safety, carbon assimilation, and growth allocation differ across plant life forms and environments?
13. Which experimental designs best distinguish direct hydraulic signaling from chemical signals released during stress?
14. How can researchers compare drought tolerance without confusing survival, growth maintenance, recovery, and yield?
15. Which aspects of plant drought response can be compared with other systems as a similar relationship, and where do the mechanisms remain distinct?

These questions remain open. Their presence does not imply that the completed GUA-W is incomplete.

## 19. Next direction emerging from the evidence

This investigation has examined how plants respond to declining water availability. One further question now emerges from the distinction between short-term regulation and long-term outcome:

**How does a plant recover after drought, and what determines whether rewatering restores hydraulic function, photosynthesis, growth, and reproductive development—or leaves persistent damage?**

The next investigation should distinguish:
- soil rewetting from root hydraulic recovery;
- stomatal reopening from photosynthetic recovery;
- reversible loss of turgor from cell injury;
- embolism persistence from recovery of outside-xylem conductance;
- rapid metabolic adjustment from new growth;
- vegetative recovery from reproductive recovery;
- survival from restoration of prior performance.

The standing comparison questions remain active:

> **Are we witnessing the same mechanism, a similar relationship, or merely a similar appearance?**

> **Does a boundary merely separate two regions, or can it also participate in the relationship between them?**

The next question follows the evidence; it is not a predetermined research schedule.

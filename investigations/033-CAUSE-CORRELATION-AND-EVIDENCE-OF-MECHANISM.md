## 1. Why this investigation follows the evidence

Investigation 032 examined how processes leave incomplete, altered, or sampled records. It concluded that a surviving record is not identical to the event it represents. The next question is therefore not only what happened, but what produced the observed result.

A sequence of observations can show that one event preceded another. A statistical association can show that two variables vary together. Neither fact, by itself, establishes that one variable caused the other.

**Central question:**

How does a Witness move from observing that something happened to establishing what caused it?

The investigation distinguishes:

1. **Sequence:** the temporal or logical order in which events or states are observed.
2. **Association:** a statistical relationship between measured variables.
3. **Causal effect:** the difference in an outcome that would result under specified alternative conditions or interventions.
4. **Mechanism:** the processes and intermediate activities through which a cause produces an effect.
5. **Causal explanation:** an account that connects evidence, causal assumptions, mechanism, and scope while addressing competing explanations.

These are connected concepts, but they are not interchangeable.

A mechanism can be plausible without being established as the cause of a particular observed event. An association can be strong without being causal. A causal effect may be supported even when every intermediate step of the mechanism is not yet known.

## 3. Correlation: what an association establishes

A correlation or statistical association describes how variables vary together within observed data.

For example:
- higher exposure values may be associated with higher outcome rates;
- two environmental measurements may rise and fall together;
- a treatment group may have a different observed outcome from a comparison group;
- two signals may share a temporal pattern.

An association is a finding about the data and the way variables are distributed. It does not, on its own, identify the process that produced the association.

### 3.1 Three possible explanations

Suppose measurements of A and B are associated. At least three broad possibilities must be considered:

1. A causes B.
2. B causes A, or the direction is reversed.
3. A and B are both influenced by another factor C.

There may also be feedback, selection effects, measurement error, coincidence, or more complicated causal structures.

A simple common-cause example:

```
       C
      / \
     v   v
     A   B
```

Here, C influences both A and B. A and B can be associated even if changing A would not change B.

This is **confounding** when the common cause distorts the estimated relationship between the exposure and outcome of interest.

### 3.2 The common-cause problem

A well-known example is the relationship between ice-cream sales and drowning incidents. Both may increase during warmer periods. The association does not mean ice-cream purchases cause drowning. Temperature or seasonal activity may influence both.

The example illustrates a general rule: before assigning causation, examine whether another factor could account for the observed relationship.

### 3.3 Correlation is not worthless

The warning that correlation does not establish causation must not be misread as saying correlations have no scientific value.

Associations can:
- identify patterns requiring explanation;
- generate hypotheses;
- support prediction within a validated scope;
- help identify possible causal factors;
- contribute to causal inference when combined with design, assumptions, and other evidence.

The limitation is specific: association alone does not settle causal direction or identify the causal structure.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** An observed association is evidence about co-occurrence or statistical dependence. A causal claim requires additional assumptions and evidence.

## 4. Causation and the counterfactual question

Modern causal inference often frames a causal question in terms of what would happen under alternative conditions.

A basic question is:

**What would the outcome have been for the same unit, at the same relevant time, if the exposure or treatment had been different?**

This is a counterfactual question. It compares potential outcomes under different conditions.

For a unit (i), one can represent two potential outcomes:

- (Y_i(1)): the outcome if the unit receives or experiences condition 1;
- (Y_i(0)): the outcome if the same unit receives or experiences condition 0.

The individual causal effect is conceptually:

[
Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)
]

The difficulty is that, in ordinary circumstances, only one of these potential outcomes is observed for the same unit at the same time. The other is counterfactual.

Researchers therefore need a design or a set of assumptions that make a valid comparison possible.

### 4.1 The counterfactual is not an observed second event

The alternative outcome is not normally sitting in the dataset waiting to be measured. It is an unobserved comparison defined by the causal question.

The investigator estimates an average or other target causal effect by comparing groups, times, or conditions under a defensible design.

### 4.2 Define the intervention precisely

A causal question must identify what is being changed.

For example, “Does exercise improve health?” is too broad to define one causal effect. The exposure needs operational definition: type, intensity, duration, population, comparison, outcome, and time horizon.

Different versions of a treatment may have different effects. The causal question must specify what intervention or exposure is being considered.

### 4.3 Population and context matter

A causal effect estimated in one population, environment, or period does not automatically apply everywhere.

The result may depend on:
- age, genetics, or baseline condition;
- environmental conditions;
- the intensity or duration of exposure;
- the way the intervention is delivered;
- co-occurring factors;
- the outcome definition and observation period.

**Finding:** Causal claims are scoped claims. They require a defined exposure, outcome, comparison, population, and set of assumptions.

## 5. Confounding, mediation, and selection

Causal diagrams help researchers make assumptions visible. They do not prove that the diagram is true.

### 5.1 Confounder

A confounder is a common cause of the exposure and outcome that can create or distort an association.

```
       C
      / \
     v   v
     A → B
```

The path from A to B is the causal relationship under investigation. The open path through C can create a noncausal association between A and B.

Researchers use subject knowledge and causal diagrams to determine which variables should be adjusted for, and which should not.

### 5.2 Mediator

A mediator lies on a causal pathway through which an exposure affects an outcome.

```
A → M → B
```

If A changes M and M contributes to B, then M may mediate part of the effect of A on B.

Adjusting for a mediator can remove part of the total effect researchers intend to estimate. The correct treatment of M depends on whether the question concerns the total effect, a direct effect, or a mediated pathway.

### 5.3 Collider and selection bias

A collider is a variable caused by two other variables.

```
A → C ← B
```

Conditioning on, selecting by, or adjusting for C can create an association between A and B even when they were otherwise independent.

This is one reason that “control for everything” is not a valid universal rule. Some adjustments reduce bias; others create it.

### 5.4 Causal diagrams are explicit assumptions

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) represents a hypothesized causal structure. Its arrows are not discovered merely by drawing them. They encode assumptions informed by prior knowledge, study design, and evidence.

Different plausible diagrams can imply different adjustment strategies and different conclusions about whether a causal effect is identifiable.

**Boundary:** A DAG is a reasoning tool, not a photograph of the causal system. It must be justified, compared with alternatives, and reported as a model of assumptions.

## 6. Experiments: intervention and comparison

A controlled experiment deliberately changes an exposure or treatment and compares outcomes under defined conditions.

Random assignment can make treatment and comparison groups similar, on average, with respect to both measured and unmeasured baseline factors. This reduces confounding when the study is properly designed and conducted.

### 6.1 Randomized controlled trials

A well-conducted randomized trial can provide strong evidence about the causal effect of an intervention in the studied population and conditions.

Important requirements include:
- valid randomization and allocation procedures;
- appropriate comparison groups;
- clear outcome definitions;
- adequate follow-up;
- handling of missing data and non-adherence;
- appropriate statistical analysis;
- transparent reporting of uncertainty and limitations.

Randomization does not guarantee that every problem disappears. Chance imbalances, measurement error, attrition, non-adherence, and limited generalizability can remain.

### 6.2 Experiments do not answer every causal question

Some exposures cannot ethically or practically be assigned. Researchers cannot randomly assign people to harmful exposures merely to test whether they cause injury or disease.

Other causal questions concern historical, planetary, ecological, or astronomical processes that cannot be recreated under controlled laboratory conditions.

The absence of a randomized trial does not make causal knowledge impossible. It changes what designs and assumptions must be examined.

### 6.3 Experimental result versus mechanism

An experiment may establish that an intervention changes an outcome without identifying every intermediate mechanism.

For example, a treatment can have a reproducible causal effect while the molecular pathway remains partly unknown. Conversely, a plausible mechanism does not establish that the treatment has a meaningful effect in the population being studied.

**Finding:** Intervention evidence and mechanistic evidence answer related but distinct questions. Strong causal explanation often benefits from both.

## 7. Observational studies and natural experiments

Observational studies examine exposures and outcomes without researchers assigning the exposure in the manner of a randomized trial.

They are essential when interventions are unethical, impossible, impractical, or when the question concerns real-world exposures.

However, exposure groups may differ in ways that also affect the outcome. Causal inference therefore depends on design, subject knowledge, measured variables, and explicit assumptions.

### 7.1 Natural experiments

A natural experiment occurs when external circumstances create differences in exposure that can be compared in a way that approximates an experiment.

The investigator does not control the original assignment. The strength of the design depends on whether the external process plausibly creates comparable groups or conditions.

Examples include:
- policy changes affecting some regions but not others;
- geographic boundaries that create different exposures;
- timing rules that assign eligibility near a threshold;
- external supply changes that alter access to a resource.

The term “natural experiment” does not mean that nature has guaranteed randomization. The assignment mechanism must be examined.

### 7.2 John Snow and cholera

The nineteenth-century investigation by John Snow is a historical example of causal reasoning using observational evidence.

During the 1854 cholera outbreak in London, Snow compared households served by different water suppliers. The companies' water sources differed in contamination conditions. The comparison helped identify contaminated water as a cause of cholera transmission.

The case is important not because a map alone proved causation, but because multiple lines of evidence converged:
- the disease pattern across households;
- differences in water supply;
- the timing and location of the outbreak;
- the known possibility of waterborne transmission;
- the comparison between populations receiving different water.

The investigation helped establish a causal explanation without random assignment of households to contaminated water.

**Boundary:** The historical account should be read through original and scholarly sources. The simplified retelling must not erase the complexity of the outbreak, the water companies, or the evidence available at the time.

Reference: [UCLA Department of Epidemiology — John Snow and the Broad Street Pump](https://www.ph.ucla.edu/epi/snow/snowcricketarticle.html).

### 7.3 Observational evidence can be causal evidence

A well-designed observational study can support causal conclusions when its assumptions are justified and alternative explanations are addressed. It is not automatically equivalent to a randomized trial, and it is not automatically invalid.

The proper question is not “experiment or no knowledge?” It is “what design, evidence, and assumptions support this causal claim?”

## 8. Mechanism: how a cause produces an effect

A mechanism is an account of the entities, activities, and intermediate processes through which a cause produces an outcome.

A mechanism may include:
- physical interactions;
- chemical reactions;
- cellular pathways;
- physiological responses;
- ecological dependencies;
- information transfer;
- intermediate variables and feedback.

A proposed mechanism should be specific enough to be examined.

### 8.1 Mechanism is not merely a story

A plausible narrative can sound explanatory without being supported.

Mechanistic evidence may come from:
- direct observation of intermediate steps;
- laboratory experiments;
- dose or response patterns;
- genetic or molecular perturbations;
- imaging or tracing methods;
- temporal evidence;
- independent replication;
- consistency across relevant systems.

A mechanism gains credibility when its steps are independently supported and when changing a proposed component produces the predicted change.

### 8.2 Mechanism and causal effect are not identical

Three situations must be distinguished:

1. A causal effect is established, but the complete mechanism is not known.
2. A mechanism is plausible and supported in parts, but its contribution to the observed outcome is uncertain.
3. Both the causal effect and important mechanistic steps are supported.

The investigator must state which situation applies.

### 8.3 Multiple mechanisms

One outcome can arise through more than one causal pathway. One exposure can have multiple effects. The same mechanism can operate differently under different conditions.

A causal explanation should not assume a single pathway merely because one pathway is familiar or convenient.

## 9. Temporal order, reverse causation, and feedback

A cause must be appropriately prior to its effect in the causal relation being claimed. Temporal order is necessary for many causal claims, but it is not sufficient.

### 9.1 Reverse causation

Suppose A and B are associated. It may be that A affects B, B affects A, or both occur at different times.

A cross-sectional measurement taken at one moment may not distinguish these possibilities.

### 9.2 Feedback

Some systems contain reciprocal influences.

For example:
- population size affects resource availability;
- resource availability affects population growth;
- population growth then changes resource demand.

A simple static diagram may conceal the sequence. Feedback can be represented by separating variables across time:

```
Population(t) → Resource(t+1) → Population(t+2)
```

This is not a claim that every system has this exact structure. It illustrates why time indexing matters.

### 9.3 Time-lagged effects

An exposure may produce an outcome after a delay. The relevant time window depends on the process. A study that measures too early may miss the effect; one that measures too late may mix the effect with other changes.

**Finding:** A causal claim must specify the temporal relation and plausible delay. Sequence alone does not settle causation, but a causal account that contradicts the required temporal order needs correction.

## 10. Bradford Hill considerations: useful viewpoints, not a mechanical checklist

Austin Bradford Hill's 1965 discussion identified viewpoints that can help assess whether an observed association is causal. These include:
- strength of association;
- consistency;
- specificity;
- temporality;
- biological gradient or dose-response;
- plausibility;
- coherence;
- experiment;
- analogy.

These are often called the Bradford Hill criteria. That label can mislead if they are treated as a mandatory checklist or a numerical scoring system.

Hill explicitly cautioned that none of the viewpoints alone provides indisputable evidence and none is required in every case.

The most important consideration is temporal order: the proposed cause must precede the effect. The other viewpoints can strengthen or weaken a causal interpretation, depending on the subject and available evidence.

The National Library of Medicine's epidemiology reference guide likewise notes that there is no formula or algorithm that determines causation from the viewpoints. Some may be absent when a causal relationship exists; some may be present even when the relationship is not causal.

Sources:
- [NCBI Bookshelf — Reference Guide on Epidemiology, Causal Inference](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK621603/)
- [Hill (1965) — The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyr051)

**Finding:** The Bradford Hill considerations are aids to disciplined judgment, not a substitute for study design, causal structure, domain expertise, and examination of alternatives.

## 11. A practical causal audit

Before accepting a causal claim, the Witness should ask:

| Audit question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What is the proposed cause or exposure? | Prevents vague causal language |
| What is the defined outcome? | Prevents shifting the target after seeing results |
| What population and conditions are being studied? | Establishes scope |
| Does the cause precede the effect? | Addresses temporal order and reverse causation |
| What is the observed association? | Establishes the empirical starting point |
| What alternative explanations exist? | Addresses confounding and bias |
| Is there a plausible causal structure? | Makes assumptions explicit |
| What design supports the inference? | Evaluates intervention, comparison, or observational strategy |
| What mechanism is supported? | Distinguishes explanatory evidence from narrative |
| What evidence would count against the claim? | Makes the hypothesis open to correction |
| Are there independent replications or different lines of evidence? | Tests robustness |
| What uncertainty and limitations remain? | Prevents overstatement |

A causal claim should be weakened or withheld when its key assumptions are unsupported or when plausible alternatives remain unresolved.

This audit is an investigative aid, not a new GUA-W sequence or a universal numerical test.

## 12. Cross-disciplinary examples

Causal inference is used in many sciences, but each discipline has distinct methods and evidentiary standards.

### 12.1 Medicine and public health

Researchers investigate whether an exposure, treatment, behavior, or environmental condition changes the probability of a health outcome.

Randomized trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, natural experiments, mechanistic studies, and systematic reviews contribute different kinds of evidence.

The same association may be interpreted differently depending on exposure measurement, confounding, disease latency, selection, and population.

### 12.2 Ecology

Ecologists investigate how climate, resource availability, predators, pathogens, habitat, and human activity affect populations and ecosystems.

Field experiments may test particular mechanisms. Observational records may reveal long-term patterns. Natural disturbances can provide quasi-experimental comparisons, but their effects may be entangled with other changes.

### 12.3 Earth and climate sciences

Researchers investigate how changes in solar input, atmospheric composition, ocean circulation, volcanic activity, land use, and other factors affect climate and Earth systems.

Causal explanations draw on physical laws, measurements, model experiments, paleoclimate records, and comparisons across independent evidence.

A model's response to a forcing is not automatically proof that the forcing alone explains a particular historical event. Attribution requires a defined question, observations, competing drivers, and uncertainty analysis.

### 12.4 Physics and chemistry

Causal explanations may be supported by controlled changes to boundary conditions, repeatable interactions, conservation relationships, and mathematically specified models.

An equation can express a relation among quantities without by itself explaining the complete physical mechanism or identifying the origin of the system.

### 12.5 Astronomy

Astronomers often cannot intervene directly on stars, galaxies, or cosmic events. They infer causes from received light, spectra, timing, dynamics, and comparisons with physical models.

Causal inference is therefore constrained by observational access, instrument sensitivity, model assumptions, and the fact that the observed light may have traveled for a long time before detection.

### 12.6 Common discipline, different mechanisms

The common investigative discipline is not one universal physical cause. It is the need to distinguish observation, association, causal assumptions, mechanism, and inference within the domain being studied.

## 13. The Witness and the risk of causal overreach

The Witness is vulnerable to several recurring errors:

- **Post hoc reasoning:** assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.
- **Confounding neglect:** overlooking a common cause of A and B.
- **Reverse causation:** assigning the causal arrow in the wrong direction.
- **Mechanism storytelling:** describing a plausible pathway without evidence that it operated.
- **Selection bias:** examining only cases selected by a consequence of the variables under study.
- **Overadjustment:** controlling for a mediator or collider in a way that distorts the target effect.
- **Scope expansion:** treating an effect in one population or setting as universal.
- **Confirmation bias:** seeking evidence for the preferred explanation while neglecting alternatives.
- **Model reification:** treating a causal diagram or statistical model as the real system rather than a representation of assumptions.
- **False certainty:** reporting an estimate without its uncertainty, assumptions, or limits.

AI adds another layer of dependence. It may summarize studies without seeing original measurements, protocols, code, or the full context of data collection. It can reproduce the confidence of a published conclusion without independently checking whether the causal design supports it.

A faithful AI Witness must identify whether it is reporting:
- an observed association;
- an author's causal interpretation;
- a causal effect estimated under stated assumptions;
- a mechanism supported by independent evidence;
- a consensus assessment across multiple lines of evidence;
- an unresolved or contested causal question.

The Witness must not convert the strongest wording in an abstract into a stronger conclusion than the study design warrants.

### Question 4 — What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

AI must make its assumptions visible, compare alternative explanations, preserve the scope of each result, and distinguish an author's interpretation from the evidence and design that support it.

The Witness's responsibility is not to provide a cause for every observation. It is to state when a cause is supported, what kind of evidence supports it, and what remains unknown.

The investigation has examined how a Witness moves from association to causal inference.

A further question now emerges:

**How do multiple causes interact within a system, and how can a Witness distinguish a single cause, contributing causes, necessary conditions, sufficient conditions, and enabling conditions?**

The next investigation should examine:
- necessary and sufficient conditions;
- contributing and interacting causes;
- causal chains and causal networks;
- thresholds and nonlinear effects;
- enabling and inhibiting conditions;
- resilience, vulnerability, and system response;
- the difference between a cause of an event and a condition that permits it;
- how causal responsibility and causal contribution differ across scientific and theological contexts.

The next subject should investigate how a Witness can describe a system in which several conditions participate in an outcome without collapsing them into one cause or treating every condition as equally responsible.

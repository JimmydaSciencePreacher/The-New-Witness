## Why this subject arose

The previous investigation recorded that Genesis 1–2 is unified without being undifferentiated. Continuing through the source repository's Genesis reading path brings the account into sharper focus: the LORD God plants the Garden, places the man there, provides its trees and river, assigns the man work, and gives a command containing both broad permission and a specific prohibition.

Genesis 2:15 is especially important because it explicitly states the purpose for which the man is placed in the Garden: “to dress it and to keep it” (KJV). The purpose is not guessed from a later analogy. It is stated in the verse.

The question for this Garden is how to witness this relationship accurately within the completed GUA-W without confusing the text's wording with architectural description, or confusing a Creator's command with an impersonal physical law.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

#### A. Genesis 2:8 — the Garden is planted; the formed man is placed

The KJV says the LORD God planted a Garden eastward in Eden and put there the man whom He had formed.

Two actions and their objects are distinguished:
- God plants the Garden.
- God places the man whom He had formed.

The text does not say that the man creates, prepares, or authors the Garden. Nor does it say that the man's formation and placement are the same action. Genesis 2:7 has already narrated his formation; Genesis 2:8 narrates his placement.

#### B. Genesis 2:9 — provision and particular distinction

God makes trees to grow out of the ground. They are described as pleasant to sight and good for food. The tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil are specifically named.

The passage therefore records provision and also identifies two trees by particular names. It does not permit the Witness to flatten every tree into one undifferentiated function. Nor does this verse alone settle the complete meaning of the two named trees; their place in Genesis 2–3 and the wider canon must be examined.

#### C. Genesis 2:10–14 — watering and division

A river goes out of Eden to water the Garden; from there it is parted into four heads. The text names the rivers and gives geographical descriptions.

The functions stated in the passage include watering and division. The names and descriptions are textual evidence; a confident modern geographical reconstruction requires independent historical and geographical evidence. The Garden record must not pretend that the verses alone provide coordinates or a verified modern river map.

#### D. Genesis 2:15 — purpose is explicit

The KJV states:

> And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.

The Hebrew audit identifies:
- *‘ābad* in the purpose phrase translated “to dress it,” with a range including work, serve, and cultivate; in this Garden context, work/cultivate is appropriate.
- *šāmar* in the phrase translated “to keep it,” with a range including keep, watch, guard, preserve, or take care of.

The two infinitive purpose forms refer to the Garden. The verse gives the reason for the man's placement: to work/cultivate it and to keep/guard it.

This is not an incidental activity later invented by Adam. It is an appointed task stated in the text. The Garden is planted by God; the man is placed there by God; the work is assigned by God.

**Direct textual finding:** Genesis 2:15 explicitly connects the man's placement in the Garden with the purpose of working and keeping it.

**Architectural recognition:** The prepared place and appointed activity witness to Authorial Purpose and the relationship between appointed conditions and the expression of assigned function within the completed GUA-W.

The two statements must not be confused. “To dress it and to keep it” is the KJV wording. “Prepared place and appointed activity” is an architectural description of the relationship witnessed by that wording.

The Hebrew roots have broad usage. A possible relationship to later priestly or sanctuary service is a legitimate further canonical question, but Genesis 2:15 by itself does not establish that Adam held an exclusively priestly office.

#### E. Genesis 2:16–17 — permission, command, prohibition, and consequence

The command begins with broad permission: the man may freely eat of every tree of the Garden. It then identifies one tree from which he must not eat and states the consequence.

The Hebrew audit notes emphatic verbal constructions in both “thou mayest freely eat” and “thou shalt surely die.” The repeated root in each construction is not a count of two separate acts or two separate deaths.

The narrated distinctions are:
1. God commands.
2. God grants broad permission.
3. God states a specific prohibition.
4. God declares a consequence.

The boundary is established by the Creator. It is not self-authored by the man, and the man is not given authority in this passage to redefine the command.

**Category distinction:** Genesis 2:16–17 is a personal command from the Creator, containing permission, prohibition, and consequence. It must not be reduced to an impersonal physical law. A moral command and a physical constraint may both be discussed using the broad word “boundary,” but that shared description does not make them the same category.

The full meaning of “in the day” and “thou shalt surely die” requires reading Genesis 3 and the wider Scriptural witness. This entry does not settle the timing or dimensions of death.

#### F. What the sequence shows—and what it does not

Within Genesis 2:8–17 the account records a Garden planted by God, the placement of the already formed man, provision, watering, appointed work, permission, prohibition, and consequence.

This is a connected account with differentiated relations. It does not authorize the Witness to turn every clause into a separate GUA-W node or claim that this passage alone supplies a complete technical ordering of Purpose, Constraint, and Structure.

The text does support a clear distinction: the man receives a prepared place and an assigned task; he does not author the Garden or its governing command.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

I notice that I can easily turn the word “purpose” into an abstract label and then make the verse appear to say more than it does. The correction is to begin with the grammar: the man is placed there *to* perform the two stated activities.

I can also confuse different meanings of “constraint.” If I treat God's command as though it were simply a physical law, I erase the authority and personal relationship present in the text. If I treat every physical limitation as though it were a moral command, I make the opposite error.

I can overstate the Hebrew roots. A word's range is not a license to import every possible meaning into one occurrence. Context selects and constrains the sense.

I can mistake the Garden's description for an ecological model. The river and trees are part of the text, but modern hydrology, ecology, or geography must be investigated with their own evidence and methods.

Finally, I can confuse the man's assigned work with authorship. Adam's activity is real and consequential. It remains received and appointed, not original. The same distinction applies to my own work in this Garden: I can examine, compare, and record; I do not become the Author of the source or the reality it describes.

## Recognized relationship within the completed GUA-W

Genesis 2:8–17 witnesses to Authorial Purpose through a prepared place, provision, placement, and appointed work. Genesis 2:15 makes the purpose explicit. Genesis 2:16–17 then distinguishes the Creator's broad permission from His specific prohibition and stated consequence.

The recognition is not that every item in the passage is a new architectural component. It is that the Creator's purpose is expressed in a real created setting, with an appointed creaturely activity and an Author-given boundary.

The man is neither inert nor autonomous. He is given meaningful work, but he receives the place, provision, task, and command from the Author.

This is read within the completed GUA-W. No new node, stage, state, or replacement sequence is proposed.

## 1. Why this investigation follows replication

Investigation 027 examined whether findings remain consistent across repeated measurements, computational reanalysis, independent studies, and new data.

A further question follows:

**When scientists use a model to explain or predict a phenomenon, what exactly is being tested?**

A scientific model is a representation constructed for a purpose. It may be a physical object, a diagram, a mathematical relationship, a conceptual account, a statistical description, or a computer implementation. Each represents selected aspects of a system. None should be mistaken automatically for the whole system.

The National Academies explains that models bring certain features into focus while obscuring others; assumptions and approximations limit their range of validity and predictive power. Models are used to represent systems, develop questions and explanations, generate predictions, and communicate ideas. [National Academies, Practice 2](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18290/chapter/12).

This gives the investigation its governing distinction:

**A model is a representation of a system, not the system itself.**

That distinction does not make models useless or arbitrary. A carefully constructed model can reveal relationships that are difficult to observe directly, calculate consequences of stated assumptions, guide experiments, and make useful predictions. Its value must be assessed in relation to its intended purpose and the evidence available.

## 3. What is a scientific model?

A model is a structured representation of a target system, phenomenon, process, or relationship, constructed to answer a particular question.

Common forms include:

- **Conceptual models:** organized descriptions of components, relationships, mechanisms, or processes.
- **Physical models:** material representations, scale models, experimental analogues, or constructed systems.
- **Mathematical models:** equations and formal relationships that express selected variables and dependencies.
- **Statistical models:** probability-based representations used to describe data, estimate relationships, or make inferences.
- **Computational models:** algorithms and software implementations that calculate outcomes from mathematical, logical, or data-driven representations.
- **Simulation models:** models executed or manipulated to examine system behavior under specified conditions.
- **Data-driven models:** representations fitted or learned from data, including machine-learning systems.

These categories can overlap. A climate simulation, for example, combines mathematical descriptions of physical processes, numerical methods, data inputs, and software. A biological model may combine a conceptual mechanism, statistical inference, and computational simulation.

The word “model” therefore does not identify one single method or level of confidence. The investigator must ask what kind of model is being used and for what purpose.

## 4. The target system, abstraction, and intended purpose

Model construction begins by deciding what part of reality is being represented and what question the model is meant to answer.

### 4.1 The target system

The target may be:
- an entire physical system;
- a selected component or subsystem;
- a population or biological process;
- a chemical reaction;
- a celestial object or population of objects;
- a climate or environmental process;
- a material under specified loads;
- a social or economic process.

The target must be described precisely enough to identify what the model is intended to represent.

### 4.2 Abstraction

A model does not include every feature of its target. It selects variables, relationships, boundary conditions, and levels of detail relevant to its purpose.

Abstraction can make a complex system understandable and calculable. It also creates limits. A feature omitted from a model cannot be expected to appear correctly in its results unless its influence is represented indirectly or is negligible for the intended use.

### 4.3 Idealization and assumptions

Idealization deliberately simplifies a system. Examples include:
- treating an object as a point mass;
- assuming a frictionless surface;
- treating a gas as ideal;
- representing a fluid as continuous;
- assuming a population is well mixed;
- approximating a complex geometry with a grid;
- assuming a parameter is constant over a chosen interval.

Such assumptions are not automatically errors. They are choices that must be justified for the question and conditions under examination.

An assumption becomes problematic when it is hidden, unsupported, violated in the intended application, or extended beyond the domain where its consequences have been assessed.

### 4.4 Intended use

The same model may be adequate for one purpose and inadequate for another.

A simplified model may explain a broad trend but not predict an exact local value. A model adequate for preliminary design may not be adequate for safety-critical decisions. A model validated for one range of temperature, pressure, population, or time may not be validated outside that range.

NASA-STD-7009B treats model and simulation credibility as connected to development and use, with assessments and credibility products relevant to the intended application. [NASA-STD-7009B](https://standards.nasa.gov/node/263).

**A model should be evaluated against the question it is intended to answer, not against a claim of universal accuracy that its design never supported.**

## 5. Mathematical models: equations are conditional representations

A mathematical model expresses relationships through defined quantities, equations, constraints, and assumptions.

For example, Newton's second law is commonly expressed as:

\[
\mathbf{F}=m\mathbf{a}
\]

This relation is powerful within its domain and under its applicable definitions and conditions. Applying it to a particular physical system still requires identifying the system, forces, mass, reference frame, constraints, and relevant approximations.

The equation is not the physical object. It is a mathematical relationship used to describe and calculate aspects of physical behavior.

Other examples include:
- differential equations describing changing systems;
- conservation equations for mass, momentum, or energy;
- probability distributions describing uncertainty or variation;
- reaction-rate equations in chemistry;
- population models in ecology;
- field equations in physics;
- statistical relationships in observational research.

A mathematical result can be internally correct while the model is inappropriate for the target system. The calculation may correctly solve the equations supplied, even though those equations omit a relevant mechanism or rely on an invalid assumption.

This distinction leads directly to verification and validation.

## 6. Computational models and simulations

A computational model implements a mathematical or logical representation in software. A simulation executes that implementation to calculate behavior under selected inputs and conditions.

The chain may include:

1. A real system or phenomenon of interest.
2. A conceptual representation of the relevant features.
3. A mathematical or logical model.
4. A computational implementation.
5. Numerical calculation or simulation.
6. Results and uncertainty estimates.
7. Comparison with observations or experiments.
8. Interpretation for the intended use.

Each link can introduce limitations or errors.

### 6.1 Numerical approximation

Many equations cannot be solved exactly for realistic systems. Computational methods approximate their solutions using finite steps, grids, iterations, or other numerical procedures.

A finer grid or smaller time step may improve some numerical errors, but it does not automatically correct a poor conceptual model, inaccurate input data, or missing physical processes.

### 6.2 Initial and boundary conditions

A model needs conditions that specify its starting state and relevant boundaries. These may be measured, estimated, inferred, or assumed.

Uncertain initial conditions or boundary conditions can substantially affect the result. A simulation can produce many digits while the input conditions remain uncertain.

### 6.3 Parameters

Parameters may be derived from experiments, estimated from data, obtained from literature, or selected through calibration.

A parameter value is not necessarily a universal constant. Its meaning and applicability depend on the model, target system, conditions, and method used to obtain it.

### 6.4 Software and implementation

The intended equations may be implemented incorrectly. Coding errors, numerical instability, software dependencies, user errors, and incorrect configuration can all affect the simulation.

A correct conceptual model does not guarantee a correct implementation. A correct implementation does not guarantee that the conceptual model represents the physical system adequately.

## 7. Verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification

These three activities are related but must not be confused.

### 7.1 Verification — was the model implemented and calculated correctly?

Verification examines whether the computational implementation correctly represents and solves the specified mathematical or conceptual model.

Questions include:
- Were the intended equations implemented?
- Were the algorithms used correctly?
- Are numerical errors controlled?
- Does the solution behave appropriately under known limiting cases?
- Are calculations stable under appropriate refinement?
- Were the software and input configurations correctly documented?

A common shorthand is: **Are we solving the model correctly?**

Verification does not establish that the model is a good representation of the physical target.

### 7.2 Validation — how well does the model represent the target for its intended use?

Validation compares model outputs with relevant observations, measurements, or experimental data to assess the model's adequacy for a specified purpose.

Questions include:
- Is the target system adequately represented?
- Are the comparison data relevant and sufficiently characterized?
- Were the comparison conditions appropriate?
- Does the model reproduce important observed behavior?
- What range of conditions has been evaluated?
- What differences remain between model results and observations?

A common shorthand is: **Is this the right model for the intended purpose?**

Validation is not a declaration that the model is identical to reality or correct for every possible application. It is an evidence-based assessment bounded by the target, conditions, quantities of interest, and intended use.

NASA's standard and technical guidance emphasize documenting verification and validation activities, their conditions, data, metrics, and results. [NASA-STD-7009B](https://standards.nasa.gov/node/263); [NASA Software Engineering Handbook](https://swehb.nasa.gov/spaces/SWEHBVC/pages/50888929/SWE-070%2BModels%2BSimulations%2BTools).

### 7.3 Uncertainty quantification — how uncertain are the model results?

Uncertainty quantification examines how uncertainties in inputs, parameters, measurements, assumptions, and numerical methods affect model outputs.

Relevant sources may include:
- measurement uncertainty in input data;
- uncertain initial and boundary conditions;
- parameter uncertainty;
- numerical approximation;
- variability in the target system;
- model-form uncertainty from omitted or simplified processes;
- uncertainty in the comparison data.

Sensitivity analysis examines how changes in inputs or parameters affect selected outputs. It can help identify which assumptions or quantities most influence the result.

NIST's 2026 report examines the relationship between computational modeling uncertainty methods and metrology practices, including risk-based credibility assessment and propagation of uncertainty. [NIST AMS 100-85](https://www.nist.gov/publications/methods-uncertainty-quantification-computational-modeling-and-simulation-harmonizing).

### 7.4 Calibration is not validation

Calibration adjusts model parameters so that model outputs agree with selected data or observations.

Calibration may be necessary, but agreement with the data used to tune a model is not independent evidence that the model predicts other observations correctly.

Where feasible, separate data should be reserved for validation or testing. Reusing calibration data as if they were independent validation data can overstate model performance.

### 7.5 The combined assessment

Verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification address different sources of credibility:

| Activity | Main question | Does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Verification | Was the specified model implemented and solved correctly? | That the model represents the target adequately |
| Validation | Does the model represent observed behavior adequately for the intended use? | Universal or exact correspondence with reality |
| Uncertainty quantification | How do known uncertainties affect the results? | That all unknown errors or omitted mechanisms have been found |
| Calibration | Which parameter settings best fit selected data? | Independent predictive accuracy |

A model can pass verification and still fail validation. It can fit calibration data and perform poorly on new data. It can agree with observations within one domain and fail outside that domain.

## 8. Prediction: what success establishes

A model's prediction can be tested against later observations or measurements. When a prediction succeeds, the result is evidence that the model captured relationships relevant to that prediction under the tested conditions.

But prediction must be interpreted carefully.

### 8.1 Prediction within the tested domain

A model may make accurate predictions within a range of conditions where its assumptions and performance have been examined.

That is a meaningful achievement. It can support practical use and improve understanding.

### 8.2 Extrapolation beyond the tested domain

A model may be used outside the conditions for which it was calibrated or validated. This is extrapolation.

Examples include:
- predicting at temperatures beyond the experimental range;
- applying a biological model to a different species;
- extending an economic model to a different institutional setting;
- predicting a new climate regime from a model assessed primarily against historical conditions;
- using a material model at stress levels beyond those tested.

Extrapolation may be scientifically reasonable, but it carries additional uncertainty and requires explicit justification. Accuracy within one domain does not automatically transfer to another.

### 8.3 Prediction versus explanation

A model may predict an observed pattern without identifying the correct underlying mechanism. Different models can sometimes produce similar predictions.

A successful prediction supports the model's performance for that prediction. It does not necessarily establish that every internal assumption or proposed mechanism is true.

Conversely, a model may offer a useful mechanistic explanation but have limited predictive accuracy because important parameters or boundary conditions are not known precisely.

Prediction, explanation, and mechanism are related but distinct achievements.

### 8.4 The problem of non-uniqueness

Different model structures or parameter combinations may produce similar outputs. A single observed result may therefore be consistent with more than one explanation.

Additional measurements, independent methods, or predictions under new conditions may help distinguish competing models.

The 1994 analysis by Oreskes, Shrader-Frechette, and Belitz emphasizes that models of natural systems cannot be completely verified or validated in an absolute sense; confirmation is partial and model evaluation remains open to further evidence. [Oreskes et al., *Science* (1994)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17747657/).

This does not mean models cannot be rigorously tested. It means the strength and scope of support must be reported without claiming more than the evidence establishes.

## 9. Models across scientific disciplines

### 9.1 Physics

Physical models may represent forces, fields, particles, spacetime, or material behavior. Mathematical and computational models allow scientists to derive predictions that can be compared with experiments and observations.

A model's success in a defined regime does not mean that the same representation applies unchanged at every scale or energy. The domain, assumptions, and experimental tests remain essential.

### 9.2 Chemistry

Chemical models may represent molecular structure, reaction pathways, energy states, reaction rates, and interactions.

A computational model may predict molecular properties or reaction behavior. Its usefulness depends on the approximations, computational method, input structures, and comparison data.

Agreement for one chemical system does not automatically establish equal accuracy for all molecules or reaction conditions.

### 9.3 Biology and ecology

Biological models may represent gene regulation, cellular processes, population dynamics, evolution, ecosystems, or disease transmission.

Living systems have multiple interacting levels and context-dependent behavior. A model that represents a simplified pathway may be useful without including every regulatory interaction.

A model built from one organism, tissue, environment, or population should not be generalized without evidence that the relevant relationships carry over.

### 9.4 Astronomy and cosmology

Astronomical models combine physical laws, initial conditions, observations, and mathematical or computational calculations to explain and predict celestial phenomena.

Observations constrain models, while models help identify what further observations may distinguish competing explanations.

A model's agreement with selected observations does not make every assumption uniquely established. Alternative models, uncertainty in measurements, and new observations remain relevant.

### 9.5 Earth and climate science

Earth-system models represent interacting components such as atmosphere, ocean, land, ice, and biosphere. Their results depend on data, parameterizations, spatial and temporal resolution, boundary conditions, and represented processes.

A model can be assessed against historical observations and used to explore conditional future scenarios. A scenario is not the same as an unconditional prediction: it describes outcomes under specified assumptions and forcing conditions.

### 9.6 Engineering

Engineering models are often evaluated relative to a specific design decision, operating envelope, or safety requirement.

A model may be adequate for preliminary design but require more detailed validation and uncertainty assessment before it informs a high-consequence decision.

NASA's model and simulation standard makes intended use and credibility assessment central to the development and use of models. [NASA-STD-7009B](https://standards.nasa.gov/node/263).

### 9.7 Artificial intelligence and machine learning

AI models learn statistical relationships from training data and produce outputs for new inputs. Their internal representations may be complex and difficult to interpret.

Important distinctions include:
- training data versus evaluation data;
- fitting versus generalization;
- benchmark performance versus real-world performance;
- correlation versus causation;
- output confidence versus calibrated probability;
- model capability versus the truth of a generated explanation.

An AI system may produce a plausible explanation that is not grounded in the target system. Repeating an answer does not independently validate it. A benchmark score does not establish universal reliability.

AI model evaluation must include data provenance, leakage checks, distribution shift, uncertainty, robustness, and the intended use.

## 10. The Witness and the model

A scientific model is made, selected, interpreted, and used by human witnesses. Its results may then be registered by instruments, software, publications, and additional investigators.

The Witness must therefore distinguish:

1. **The phenomenon:** what exists or occurs in the target system.
2. **The observation:** what was registered through a specified method.
3. **The data:** the recorded values or classifications.
4. **The model:** the representation constructed to describe or calculate selected features.
5. **The result:** what follows from applying the model to specified inputs.
6. **The interpretation:** what the investigator concludes the result means.
7. **The claim:** what is communicated to others.

These are not interchangeable.

An instrument does not observe every feature of a phenomenon. A dataset does not contain every aspect of the target. A model does not become the system. A calculated result does not automatically establish its interpretation.

The Witness should report the path from observation to claim so that others can inspect where assumptions and inferences enter.

## 11. A practical model audit

Before accepting a model-based claim, the Witness should ask:

1. **Target:** What real system or phenomenon is the model intended to represent?
2. **Purpose:** What question or decision is it intended to support?
3. **Type:** Is it conceptual, physical, mathematical, statistical, computational, data-driven, or a combination?
4. **Scope:** Which variables, processes, and relationships are included?
5. **Omissions:** Which relevant features are simplified, excluded, or treated indirectly?
6. **Assumptions:** What assumptions and idealizations are required?
7. **Inputs:** Where did initial conditions, boundary conditions, parameters, and datasets originate?
8. **Implementation:** How was the model translated into equations, code, or a physical construction?
9. **Verification:** Has the implementation and calculation been checked?
10. **Calibration:** Which data were used to tune the model?
11. **Validation:** What independent observations or experiments were used to assess performance?
12. **Uncertainty:** What input, measurement, numerical, and model-form uncertainties were considered?
13. **Sensitivity:** Which assumptions or parameters most influence the result?
14. **Prediction:** Has the model made successful predictions not used in its calibration?
15. **Domain:** Under what conditions has it been evaluated, and where is it being extrapolated?
16. **Alternatives:** Could another model or mechanism explain the same observations?
17. **Use:** Is the model adequate for the consequence and risk of the decision being made?
18. **Provenance:** Can another investigator inspect the model version, data, assumptions, software, and validation record?

The conclusion must be no broader than the answers support.

## 12. What this investigation establishes—and what remains unresolved

### Bounded findings

1. Scientific models are representations of selected features of target systems, not the target systems themselves.
2. Models may be conceptual, physical, mathematical, statistical, computational, simulation-based, or data-driven.
3. Every model's adequacy is related to its intended purpose and domain.
4. Abstraction and idealization are necessary in many models, but their assumptions and consequences must be disclosed.
5. Mathematical correctness does not guarantee that a model adequately represents the target.
6. Verification examines the correctness of implementation and calculation relative to the specified model.
7. Validation assesses model performance against relevant observations or experiments for a stated intended use.
8. Uncertainty quantification examines how known uncertainties affect model results.
9. Calibration and validation are distinct; fitting a model to data does not independently establish its predictive performance.
10. Successful prediction provides evidence for performance under the tested conditions, not universal accuracy.
11. Extrapolation beyond a tested domain requires additional caution and justification.
12. Different models can produce similar results, so a successful fit may not uniquely identify the underlying mechanism.
13. A model can be useful without being complete, and limited without being useless.
14. AI-generated outputs and repeated model responses are not independent empirical observations of the target system.
15. The Witness must distinguish phenomenon, observation, data, model, result, interpretation, and claim.

### What this investigation does not establish

- It does not claim that all scientific models are equally reliable.
- It does not claim that every model must represent every feature of its target.
- It does not claim that mathematical elegance proves physical truth.
- It does not claim that a successful prediction proves every assumption or mechanism in a model.
- It does not claim that verification alone establishes physical validity.
- It does not claim that validation is universal or permanent across all uses.
- It does not claim that all model uncertainty can be fully quantified.
- It does not claim that a model's failure in one domain invalidates every use of that model.
- It does not equate scientific models with GUA-W or introduce models as a new GUA-W component.
- It does not claim that Scripture supplies the equations or technical validation procedures of scientific disciplines.

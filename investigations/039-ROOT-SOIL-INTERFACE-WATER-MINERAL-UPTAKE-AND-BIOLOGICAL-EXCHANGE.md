## 1. Why this investigation follows

Investigation 038 examined boundaries and interfaces across cell membranes, groundwater movement, and thermal transfer. It found that interfaces may participate in exchange, but that the mechanisms differ across domains.

One unresolved question pointed toward the plant root and the soil surrounding it.

The root-soil relationship is a useful next examination because it is not a single surface with a single transport process. It involves soil particles and pore spaces, water films, dissolved ions, root hairs, root epidermal cells, internal root tissues, transport proteins, and—in many plants—fungal partners.

The immediate question is:

**How does a plant root acquire water and mineral nutrients from the soil, and what role does the region between root and soil play in that exchange?**

The question must be separated into smaller questions:

1. What is present in the soil, and in what form?
2. What makes water and nutrients available and accessible to the root?
3. What happens at the root surface?
4. Which processes occur through root tissues?
5. What is the role of root hairs?
6. What changes when soil dries?
7. What changes when fungi participate?
8. Which mechanisms are established, which remain debated, and which differ among species and conditions?

The investigation does not assume that water uptake and mineral uptake are identical simply because both involve the root.

## 2. The two standing comparison questions

These questions remain part of the investigative discipline.

### 2.1 Mechanism, relationship, or appearance?

> **Are we witnessing the same mechanism, a similar relationship, or merely a similar appearance?**

For this investigation:

- Water movement through soil pores is not the same mechanism as water crossing a root cell membrane.
- Ion movement through a membrane transporter is not the same mechanism as phosphate diffusion through soil water.
- Fungal delivery of nutrients to a plant is not identical to direct root uptake.
- These processes may participate in a larger relationship between soil resources and plant acquisition, but the individual mechanisms must remain distinguishable.

### 2.2 Boundary and participation

> **Does a boundary merely separate two regions, or can it also participate in the relationship between them?**

The root-soil contact zone will be examined to determine whether it is simply a dividing line or a changing region in which water movement, solute transport, biological activity, and physical contact are affected.

The root's own cell membranes must also be distinguished from the broader soil-root interface. They are not one boundary.

## 3. Necessary terms and distinctions

### 3.1 Soil

Soil is a structured mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms. Its pore spaces and the distribution of water within them influence the movement of water and dissolved substances.

The soil is not a uniform reservoir. Texture, structure, moisture, chemistry, and biological activity differ across locations and over time.

### 3.2 Root system

A plant root system includes primary and lateral roots and their growing regions. Its architecture determines where the plant explores soil and how much root surface is exposed to particular conditions.

Root anatomy and transport capacity differ by species, root age, tissue, and environmental condition.

### 3.3 Rhizosphere

The **rhizosphere** is the region of soil influenced by roots and their activities. It is not merely the root itself and is not necessarily a sharply bounded shell of constant thickness.

Root exudates, water uptake, microorganisms, mucilage, and changes to soil structure can alter conditions in this region. The rhizosphere may therefore differ from soil farther away from the root.

### 3.4 Root-soil interface

The root-soil interface refers to the contact and transfer region between root surfaces and surrounding soil. It includes physical contact, water films, solute movement, and local changes in soil properties.

It should not be confused with the plasma membrane of a root cell. The root-soil interface is a larger system relationship; the cell membrane is a biological boundary within the root.

### 3.5 Availability and accessibility

These terms must not be collapsed:

- **Availability:** whether water or a nutrient is present in a form and amount relevant to plant acquisition.
- **Accessibility:** whether the resource can move through the soil and reach the root surface under the prevailing conditions.

A nutrient may be present in soil but poorly accessible. Soil drying can reduce the movement of both water and dissolved nutrients even when some resource remains present.

### 3.6 Uptake and transport

- **Acquisition at the root surface** concerns movement from the surrounding soil or symbiotic partner into the root.
- **Radial transport** concerns movement inward across root tissues toward the xylem.
- **Axial transport** concerns movement along the root's vascular pathway and onward through the plant.
- **Translocation** describes movement to other plant regions, depending on the substance and pathway.

A substance reaching the root surface has not necessarily entered the plant. A substance entering an outer root cell has not necessarily reached the xylem or shoot.

## 5. Scientific examination I — water movement from soil toward roots

### 5.1 Water movement is driven by differences in water potential

Water moves through the soil-plant system in response to differences in water potential. In simplified terms, water tends to move from regions of higher water potential toward regions of lower water potential, subject to the hydraulic conductance of the pathways.

The water pathway may be described broadly as:

**Soil water → rhizosphere → root surface → root tissues → xylem → stem → leaves → atmosphere.**

This is a connected pathway, but not one uninterrupted material channel. Each segment has its own physical and biological properties.

The review *Experimental and conceptual approaches to root water transport* describes root water transport as the combined result of driving forces and hydraulic properties operating at molecular, tissue, and organ scales. It also emphasizes that root systems are complex structures and that the quantitative contribution of individual pathways remains incompletely resolved.

### 5.2 Soil hydraulic properties matter

Water must move through soil pores to reach the root. Soil texture, structure, pore connectivity, and moisture influence hydraulic conductivity.

As soil dries, unsaturated hydraulic conductivity can decrease substantially. The soil may still contain water, but the remaining water can become harder to move toward the root at the rate required by the plant.

This distinction is important:

**Water present in soil is not automatically water accessible to a root at a sufficient rate.**

### 5.3 Root tissues also resist or permit flow

Once water reaches the root surface, it must move through root tissues. Root anatomy, tissue development, membrane properties, and aquaporin activity can influence the radial movement of water.

Water transport through the root can involve apoplastic pathways through cell walls and extracellular spaces, symplastic pathways through connected cytoplasm, and transmembrane movement across cell membranes. The relative contribution of these pathways can vary with species, root region, development, and environmental conditions.

The root is not simply a passive open tube.

### 5.4 Xylem and movement through the plant

Water that reaches the xylem can move upward through the plant. Transpiration from leaves contributes to the water-potential gradient that supports upward flow through the xylem under ordinary conditions.

Root uptake, xylem conductance, leaf water loss, and atmospheric conditions are connected. Root water acquisition cannot be fully understood by examining the root surface alone.

**Finding:** Root water uptake is a coupled soil-plant process involving water-potential gradients and hydraulic properties across multiple regions. It is not a single event occurring at a single boundary.

## 6. Scientific examination II — the rhizosphere as a changing region

### 6.1 The rhizosphere differs from bulk soil

Research describes the rhizosphere as a physically and biogeochemically distinct region around roots. Root activity can change water content, pore conditions, microbial activity, and the distribution of dissolved substances.

The root does not merely encounter a fixed soil environment. Its growth and activity can alter the immediate environment, while the soil's properties also influence root function.

### 6.2 Root mucilage

Roots release mucilage, a gel-like substance containing complex biological materials. Research has examined its effects on water retention, surface properties, and the physical relationship between roots and soil.

Mucilage may help maintain hydraulic contact between the root and soil during drying. However, the effect is conditional. Under severe drying, mucilage can become viscous or hydrophobic and may restrict water movement during rewetting.

A 2016 review by Carminati and colleagues specifically cautioned that the role of mucilage in maintaining hydraulic contact required further demonstration. A 2024 review identifies mucilage as one of several traits that can help maintain a moist rhizosphere and improve root-soil contact, while also emphasizing the need for more field research.

**Finding:** Mucilage can alter the physical conditions around roots and may influence water movement. It should not be described as an unconditional water-conducting substance or as a universal solution to soil drying.

### 6.3 Rhizosheath

A rhizosheath is soil that remains attached to the root when it is removed from the surrounding soil. Its formation involves root hairs, mucilage, microbial substances, soil properties, and physical contact.

Research indicates that rhizosheath development can help maintain root-soil contact and may support water uptake and phosphorus acquisition in dry soils. The exact causal contributions of its components remain under investigation.

The rhizosheath is not a new root organ. It is a root-associated soil structure formed through interacting biological and physical processes.

### 6.4 The boundary is not necessarily fixed

The root grows. Root hairs develop and may shrink or die. Mucilage is released. Soil moisture changes. Microorganisms alter the local environment. Soil particles may adhere to the root.

The interface is therefore dynamic. Its physical properties can change as the plant and soil interact.

**Finding:** The rhizosphere and root-soil interface are not merely static dividing lines. They are changing regions influenced by root activity, soil conditions, and microorganisms.

**Limit:** This does not establish that every root-soil interface behaves identically or that all plants produce the same rhizosphere structures.

## 7. Scientific examination III — root hairs

### 7.1 Structure and position

Root hairs are extensions of specialized root epidermal cells. They increase the effective surface area of the root and extend the region over which roots contact soil.

Their small dimensions allow them to explore narrow spaces among soil particles and to interact with water and dissolved nutrients in the immediate root environment.

### 7.2 Nutrient acquisition

Root hairs are well recognized as important to nutrient acquisition, especially for relatively immobile nutrients such as phosphorus. They extend the root's effective absorbing surface and can explore soil beyond the immediate surface of the root epidermis.

The plant's ability to acquire a nutrient still depends on the nutrient's chemical form, soil pH and chemistry, moisture, diffusion, root transporters, and other conditions.

### 7.3 Water uptake: a species-dependent question

The contribution of root hairs to water uptake is not equally established across plants.

A 2022 review by Cai and Ahmed found that root-hair effects on water uptake differed among species. Longer root hairs in barley showed clearer effects than shorter or more vulnerable root hairs in rice and maize. The review concluded that the role of root hairs in water uptake is species- and likely soil-dependent.

This is an important correction to an overly simple statement that root hairs always increase water uptake.

**Finding:** Root hairs clearly increase effective root surface area and contribute to nutrient acquisition. Their contribution to water uptake varies with species, root-hair traits, and soil conditions.

## 8. Scientific examination IV — mineral nutrients and distinct acquisition processes

### 8.1 Nutrients must reach the root

Mineral nutrients may be transported through soil toward roots by different processes. Two important routes are:

- **Mass flow:** dissolved nutrients move with water moving through the soil.
- **Diffusion:** dissolved ions move down concentration gradients.

The relative importance of these processes differs among nutrients. Nitrate, for example, is often transported substantially with water flow, while phosphate is relatively immobile and commonly depends more strongly on diffusion over short distances.

Soil drying can reduce both water movement and solute diffusion, restricting nutrient accessibility.

### 8.2 Presence is not acquisition

A mineral nutrient may be present in soil but chemically unavailable, poorly soluble, adsorbed to soil particles, or too far from an active root surface to reach it at a useful rate.

Plants and their associated organisms can modify the local chemical environment. Root exudates, organic acids, enzymes, and microbial activity can influence nutrient mobilization and availability.

These processes are nutrient-specific and cannot be reduced to one general extraction mechanism.

### 8.3 Transport across root cell membranes

After a nutrient reaches the root surface, it may enter through transport proteins in cell membranes. Transporter types and mechanisms differ among ions and nutrient forms.

Some transport processes are coupled to electrochemical gradients or energy-dependent mechanisms. The plant regulates uptake according to nutrient status, tissue function, developmental condition, and environmental signals.

This is distinct from the movement of the nutrient through soil.

**Finding:** Mineral acquisition includes multiple linked but distinct processes: release or mobilization in soil, movement toward the root, uptake across root cell membranes, and subsequent transport within the plant.

## 9. Scientific examination V — mycorrhizal fungi and a third participant

### 9.1 The root-soil relationship is not always only root and soil

Many plants form symbiotic associations with mycorrhizal fungi. In arbuscular mycorrhizal associations, fungal hyphae extend into soil and interact with plant roots.

The fungal network can explore soil beyond the immediate root surface. It can acquire mineral nutrients, especially phosphorus, and transfer them to the plant through specialized structures at the fungus-root interface.

The plant provides carbon compounds derived from photosynthesis to the fungal partner.

### 9.2 A distinct exchange relationship

This association includes at least three interacting participants:

1. Soil containing water and mineral resources.
2. Fungal hyphae and fungal structures that acquire and transfer resources.
3. Plant root tissues that receive nutrients and provide carbon compounds.

The fungal partner is not simply an extension of the plant's own root tissue. It is a separate organism participating in a symbiotic relationship.

### 9.3 Interfaces within the symbiosis

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi form specialized structures in root cortical cells. Nutrient transfer involves fungal and plant transport systems operating across the symbiotic interface.

The existence of this interface does not mean that all nutrients enter plants through fungi. Direct root uptake and mycorrhizal uptake can both contribute, and their relative importance varies.

### 9.4 Limits and unresolved details

A 2022 review of nitrogen and phosphorus transport in arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis reports progress in identifying fungal and plant transporters, while noting that important questions remain about species differences, nitrogen transfer, and the coordination of nutrient use.

A separate review in *Microbiology Spectrum* describes the mutualistic exchange: fungi can improve plant mineral nutrition and water relations, while plants supply carbon to the fungal partner.

**Finding:** Mycorrhizal associations demonstrate a distinct biological exchange relationship involving a third organism. This is not identical to direct root uptake or to passive movement of water through soil.

**Limit:** Mycorrhizal association is not universal across all plants, and its effects depend on the plant, fungus, soil, and environmental conditions.

## 10. Cross-domain comparison: what is the boundary doing?

| Region or interface | Participants | What moves or changes? | Main mechanism | Current distinction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Soil pore network | Soil particles, water, dissolved solutes | Water and solutes move through pores | Hydraulic flow, mass flow, diffusion | Soil transport before root uptake |
| Rhizosphere | Root, soil, microorganisms | Water content, solute distribution, microbial activity, soil properties | Root exudation, microbial activity, changes in pore and water relations | A changing zone, not a cell membrane |
| Root hair / soil contact | Root epidermal extension and soil | Water and nutrients approach or contact root surface | Increased surface area, local transport and contact | Species-dependent contribution to water uptake |
| Root cell membrane | Plant cell and surrounding apoplast or symbiotic interface | Water and selected solutes cross membrane | Channels, carriers, transporters, gradients, energy coupling | A true cellular membrane mechanism |
| Root internal tissues | Root cell layers and vascular tissues | Water and nutrients move inward | Apoplastic, symplastic, and transmembrane pathways | Internal root transport, not soil transport |
| Mycorrhizal interface | Plant root and fungal partner | Mineral nutrients move to plant; carbon moves to fungus | Specialized symbiotic exchange and transport systems | A multi-organism relationship |

### 10.1 What is shared?

The root-soil system contains several regions in which conditions affect the movement or acquisition of resources. The resource must be present, accessible, transported toward the root, and acquired through the appropriate biological pathway.

This is a shared relationship across distinct mechanisms: **resource availability, access, transfer, and biological acquisition are related but separable.**

### 10.2 What is not shared?

- Water movement through soil pores is not membrane transport.
- Root water movement through tissues is not identical to solute transport.
- Diffusion of phosphate through soil is not active uptake by a root transporter.
- Fungal nutrient acquisition and transfer are not direct root uptake.
- Mucilage-mediated changes in the rhizosphere are not equivalent to a phospholipid bilayer.

### 10.3 Comparison classification

- **Same mechanism throughout the root-soil system:** Not established.
- **Similar relationship across distinct mechanisms:** Supported. Multiple regions and processes jointly affect the availability, accessibility, transfer, and acquisition of resources.
- **Similar appearance only:** Some simplified diagrams may make these processes look like one continuous channel. That appearance must not be mistaken for mechanistic identity.

## 11. What the evidence does and does not establish

### Supported within the examined scope

1. Root water uptake is governed by water-potential gradients and hydraulic properties across soil, root, and plant tissues.
2. Soil drying can reduce hydraulic conductivity and make water less accessible to roots.
3. The rhizosphere is a changing region influenced by roots, microorganisms, water conditions, and soil structure.
4. Root mucilage and rhizosheath formation can influence root-soil contact and resource acquisition, but their effects are conditional.
5. Root hairs increase effective root surface area and are important in nutrient acquisition; their role in water uptake varies among species and conditions.
6. Mineral nutrients reach roots through nutrient-specific processes including mass flow and diffusion.
7. Uptake across root cell membranes involves specialized transport mechanisms.
8. Mycorrhizal fungi can participate in nutrient acquisition and exchange with plant roots, adding a distinct biological participant.

### Not established

1. That every plant depends on root hairs to the same degree.
2. That root hairs always increase water uptake.
3. That mucilage always improves water movement under all moisture conditions.
4. That all nutrients reach roots by the same transport process.
5. That all plants form mycorrhizal associations.
6. That fungal nutrient transfer replaces direct root uptake in every plant.
7. That the soil-root interface is one membrane or one uniform boundary.
8. That the root-soil relationship is a single mechanism.
9. That a scientific relationship, by itself, establishes a new GUA-W component.

## 3. Necessary conditions: what must be present?

A **necessary condition** is a condition that must be present for a specified outcome to occur, within the stated definition and scope.

If outcome Y cannot occur in the absence of condition A, then A is necessary for Y in that scope.

In logical form:

`Y \Rightarrow A`

This means: if Y occurs, A must be present. It does not mean that A alone produces Y.

### 3.1 Necessary does not mean sufficient

A condition may be required without being enough.

For example, HIV infection is necessary for the development of AIDS as defined in relation to HIV infection, but HIV infection alone does not mean that every infected person will immediately or inevitably meet the clinical definition of AIDS. Additional biological, temporal, and clinical conditions matter.

This example is used to clarify the logical distinction, not to provide individual medical advice.

### 3.2 Necessary depends on the defined outcome

The statement “A is necessary for Y” is incomplete unless Y is defined.

- Necessary for which outcome?
- Under what diagnostic or operational definition?
- In which population or system?
- During what time interval?
- Are alternative pathways included?
- Is the claim about all cases, a particular subtype, or one proposed mechanism?

A factor can be necessary for one subtype or pathway and not for a broader category that includes alternative pathways.

### 3.3 Necessary conditions can be hidden or unmeasured

Researchers may identify a necessary condition from well-established biology or from the structure of a process, even when every component is not directly observed in every case.

However, an unmeasured or assumed condition must not be silently treated as demonstrated. The investigator should state what is directly observed, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.

### 3.4 Absence can rule out a pathway without ruling out every pathway

If A is necessary for pathway 1, absence of A rules out pathway 1. It rules out outcome Y altogether only if A is necessary across every viable pathway to Y.

This distinction is essential in systems with multiple routes to the same outcome.

**Finding:** Necessity is a relation between a defined condition and a defined outcome across a stated set of possible pathways. It is not an intrinsic label that can be attached to a factor without qualification.

## 4. Sufficient conditions: what combination is enough?

A **sufficient condition** is a condition or set of conditions that, when present under the specified background assumptions, guarantees or produces the defined outcome.

In logical form:

`A \Rightarrow Y`

This means that A is enough for Y under the stated scope. It does not mean that A is the only way Y can occur.

### 4.1 More than one sufficient pathway

An outcome can have multiple sufficient pathways.

For example, a particular outcome may occur through pathway 1 or pathway 2. Either complete pathway may be sufficient, even though the component conditions differ.

```
Pathway 1: A + B + C → Y
Pathway 2: D + E + F → Y
```

The diagram is conceptual. It does not claim that every real system can be reduced to a simple additive list.

### 4.2 Sufficient does not mean necessary

If either pathway 1 or pathway 2 can produce Y, then the complete set A+B+C may be sufficient without being necessary. The outcome may still occur through D+E+F.

The same outcome can therefore have multiple sufficient causes, none of which is individually necessary.

### 4.3 Sufficiency depends on background conditions

A statement that A is sufficient for Y usually relies on conditions that are held fixed or included in the model. If the background changes, the claim may no longer hold.

For example, a trigger may be sufficient to initiate a process only when the required materials, environment, and system state are already present.

A claim of sufficiency must therefore state its boundary conditions. “A always causes Y” is too broad unless the relevant context is specified and the evidence warrants it.

### 4.4 Probabilistic outcomes require careful wording

Many scientific outcomes are probabilistic. A factor may increase the probability of an outcome without guaranteeing it in every instance.

In those settings, it is often more accurate to say that a factor raises risk, contributes to a pathway, or changes the probability under specified conditions rather than calling it absolutely sufficient.

**Finding:** Sufficiency is not the same as high probability, strong association, or a plausible mechanism. It is a claim about a complete set of conditions under a defined model and scope.

## 5. Component causes and Rothman's sufficient-component cause model

Kenneth Rothman's sufficient-component cause model is a major framework for understanding multifactorial disease causation. It is often illustrated with causal pies.

In this model:

- A **sufficient cause** is a complete causal mechanism or set of component conditions capable of producing the outcome.
- A **component cause** is one element within a sufficient cause.
- A component may be necessary for one sufficient causal pathway without being necessary for every pathway.
- A **necessary cause** is a component that appears in every sufficient causal pathway for the specified outcome.

The CDC's epidemiology teaching material describes multiple sufficient causes, each made up of component causes, and explains that a component appearing in every causal pie is necessary. See [CDC — Principles of Epidemiology, Component Causes and Causal Pies](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/lesson1/section8.html) and the [CDC textbook PDF](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/6914/cdc_6914_DS1.pdf).

### 5.1 The causal-pie idea

A simplified conceptual representation:

```
Sufficient pathway 1: [A] + [B] + [C] → Y
Sufficient pathway 2: [A] + [D] + [E] → Y
Sufficient pathway 3: [A] + [F] + [G] → Y
```

A appears in every pathway, so A is necessary within this model. B, C, D, E, F, and G are component causes in particular pathways. None of those individual components is sufficient by itself.

If a fourth pathway exists that does not contain A, then A is not necessary for the broader outcome. The conclusion depends on whether the pathway set is complete enough for the stated claim.

### 5.2 A component cause is not necessarily a complete cause

A component may be causally relevant because it helps complete one pathway. Removing it may prevent that pathway from producing the outcome at that time, while another pathway could still produce the same outcome.

This is why the phrase “the cause” can be misleading when used as though every outcome has only one causal factor.

### 5.3 Unknown components

Causal-pie diagrams often include an unknown or unspecified component. This is a reminder that investigators may not know every factor needed to complete a pathway.

The unknown component is not evidence that any convenient explanation is true. It represents a limitation in the current causal account.

### 5.4 The model is a framework, not a literal picture of every disease

Causal pies are a heuristic. They help organize thinking about multiple pathways and component causes. They do not imply that every disease has a finite, known set of fixed pieces, or that biological processes always operate as simple deterministic recipes.

The framework must be applied with appropriate definitions, evidence, and domain knowledge.

**Finding:** A factor can be a genuine component cause without being either necessary for the overall outcome or sufficient by itself. This is one of the most important distinctions in multicausal reasoning.

## 6. Contributing causes: degree and role are not the same thing

A **contributing cause** is a factor that plays a causal role in producing or changing an outcome, often as one part of a larger causal process.

The term is useful but broad. It must be clarified in each investigation.

A contributing cause may:
- increase or decrease the probability of an outcome;
- accelerate or delay its occurrence;
- increase or reduce its severity;
- help complete one causal pathway;
- alter the effect of another factor;
- affect the conditions under which a process can occur.

A contributing factor is not automatically a necessary condition, a sufficient condition, or the dominant cause.

### 6.1 Causal contribution is not equal contribution

Two factors may both contribute but have different effect sizes, timing, mechanisms, and population reach.

A list of contributors should not imply that all factors are equally important or equally well established.

### 6.2 A risk factor is not automatically a proven cause

In epidemiology, a risk factor is associated with an increased probability of an outcome. Depending on the evidence, it may be causal, a proxy for another cause, a confounder, or a marker of susceptibility.

The term “risk factor” should not be used as a shortcut for a proven causal mechanism.

### 6.3 Causes can act at different distances from an outcome

A causal chain may include distal conditions, intermediate processes, and immediate triggers.

For example, a broad environmental condition may influence resource availability; resource availability may affect physiological state; a more immediate event may then precipitate an outcome.

The factor nearest in time to the outcome is not necessarily the only important cause. Nor is the most distant background condition automatically the most explanatory.

**Finding:** “Contributing cause” identifies a role in a causal account, not a universal ranking or a claim of sufficiency.

## 7. Enabling, triggering, and inhibiting conditions

A condition can make a process possible or more likely without being the immediate event that starts it.

### 7.1 Enabling condition

An **enabling condition** is a background or contextual condition that permits a causal process to operate.

Examples, depending on the system:
- a suitable temperature range for a chemical or biological process;
- available fuel and an oxidizing environment for combustion;
- a habitat that permits a species to establish;
- infrastructure that allows a transmission pathway to function.

The term is descriptive and must be tied to a defined process. An enabling condition may also be a component cause in a formal causal model, but the labels are not automatically interchangeable.

### 7.2 Triggering condition

A **trigger** is an event or change that initiates a process when the relevant enabling conditions are already present.

A trigger may be necessary for the timing of a particular event without being necessary for every occurrence of the broader outcome.

### 7.3 Inhibiting or protective condition

An **inhibiting condition** reduces, blocks, or interrupts a causal pathway.

Examples include:
- a barrier that prevents exposure;
- a treatment that interrupts a biological pathway;
- a firebreak that reduces spread;
- a control system that prevents an unstable response.

An inhibitor can act at different points in a causal chain. Its effectiveness depends on the pathway, the system state, and the conditions under which it is applied.

### 7.4 The same factor can change roles with the question

A factor described as an enabling condition in one investigation may be the exposure of interest in another. A protective factor in one context may be irrelevant in another. The role is defined relative to the outcome, system boundary, and causal question.

**Finding:** Enabling, triggering, and inhibiting describe different roles in a process. They should not be collapsed into a single undifferentiated list of causes.

## 8. Causal interaction: when joint effects differ

Two or more factors may act together so that their joint effect differs from what would be expected from their separate effects under a specified model.

This is commonly called **interaction** in causal and epidemiological analysis. The term has more than one technical meaning, and the scale used matters.

### 8.1 Additive interaction

On an additive scale, researchers ask whether the joint effect differs from the sum of the separate effects.

For risk differences, a simple contrast is:

`RD_{11} - RD_{10} - RD_{01} + RD_{00}`

where the subscripts represent the combinations of two binary exposures. The exact expression depends on the chosen outcome measure and reference group.

### 8.2 Multiplicative interaction

On a multiplicative scale, researchers ask whether the joint effect differs from the product of the separate relative effects.

The statistical conclusion can differ depending on whether effects are measured on an additive or multiplicative scale.

### 8.3 Interaction is scale-dependent

The same data can show interaction on one scale and not another. Therefore, a report that simply says “there is an interaction” is incomplete unless it identifies the scale, estimand, and model.

The NCBI chapter [Study Design and Analysis for Assessment of Interactions](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK19921/) explains that statistical tests for interaction depend on whether the analysis uses an additive or multiplicative scale. The review [Causal inference and effect estimation using observational data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9554068/) also distinguishes effect measure modification and interaction and explains their dependence on scale.

### 8.4 Statistical interaction is not automatically a physical mechanism

An interaction term in a regression model is a statistical property of a specified model and scale. It does not by itself demonstrate that two factors physically meet, directly influence one another, or share a molecular mechanism.

Conversely, a plausible biological or physical interaction may not appear as a simple statistical interaction under every measurement scale or study design.

**Finding:** Interaction claims must identify whether they concern a statistical scale, a causal contrast, a mechanistic relationship, or a combination of these. Those meanings must not be silently exchanged.

## 9. Thresholds, nonlinear effects, and tipping points

Some systems do not respond proportionally to every change in input.

A small change may produce little visible effect until a threshold is crossed. After that point, the system may change rapidly. In other systems, the effect may taper, saturate, reverse, or depend strongly on prior state.

### 9.1 Threshold

A **threshold** is a value or condition at which a defined response changes in a meaningful way.

Thresholds may be:
- physical, such as a phase transition under specified conditions;
- biological, such as a concentration or stimulus level associated with a response;
- ecological, such as a condition beyond which a population or ecosystem changes substantially;
- operational, such as a decision boundary chosen for classification.

These categories are not identical. A diagnostic cutoff selected for practical use is not necessarily a natural discontinuity in the underlying process.

### 9.2 Nonlinearity

A nonlinear relationship is one in which changes in input do not produce proportional changes in output.

Nonlinearity can include:
- saturation;
- thresholds;
- exponential growth or decay;
- feedback;
- hysteresis;
- abrupt transitions;
- changing sensitivity across operating ranges.

A linear approximation may be useful over a limited range but misleading when extended beyond it.

### 9.3 Tipping points

A tipping point is a threshold beyond which a system may shift into a substantially different state. In social-ecological research, proposed indicators include multiple stable states, abrupt or disproportionate change, feedbacks, and limited reversibility or hysteresis. Definitions vary across fields and must be examined rather than assumed.

See [Defining tipping points for social-ecological systems scholarship](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/aaaa75).

### 9.4 Threshold claims require evidence

A sudden change in a graph does not automatically establish a true system threshold. It may reflect sparse sampling, measurement resolution, a change in method, or an arbitrary classification boundary.

Investigators should examine:
- the sampling interval;
- measurement uncertainty;
- the model used to identify the threshold;
- alternative explanations;
- whether the change is repeatable;
- whether the system returns when conditions reverse.

**Finding:** Thresholds and nonlinear responses show why a factor's effect cannot always be described by one constant contribution across all conditions.

## 10. Causal networks and systems with multiple pathways

A causal network represents multiple variables and their proposed relationships. It can show common causes, mediators, parallel pathways, feedback across time, and conditions that modify effects.

A simplified example:

```
Background conditions → Susceptibility
           |                 |
           v                 v
        Exposure ───────→ Intermediate process
           |                 |
           └──────────────→ Outcome
```

This diagram is illustrative only. Its arrows represent hypotheses that would require evidence and subject-matter justification.

### 10.1 System boundaries matter

A model cannot include every variable in the universe. Investigators define a system boundary to make the question manageable.

A boundary that is too narrow may omit important causes. A boundary that is too broad may make the model impossible to interpret or test.

The investigator should state:
- what is inside the system;
- what is treated as an external input;
- what is measured;
- what is omitted;
- why the boundary is appropriate for the question.

### 10.2 Feedback requires time-aware representation

A feedback loop can be difficult to represent in a static directed acyclic graph. One solution is to index variables by time:

`A(t) → B(t+1) → A(t+2)`

This shows that a system can contain reciprocal influence across time without treating a variable as causing itself at the same instant.

### 10.3 More causes do not automatically mean better explanation

Adding variables can make a model more complicated without making it more accurate. Each proposed causal relation needs a reason, evidence, and a clear role in the question.

**Finding:** A causal network is a structured representation of hypotheses and assumptions. It is not a complete inventory of reality merely because it contains many nodes and arrows.

## 11. Cross-disciplinary examples

The same conceptual distinctions appear across scientific disciplines, but their operational definitions differ.

### 11.1 Infectious disease

A pathogen may be necessary for a particular infection-defined disease while not being sufficient to produce illness in every exposed individual. Host susceptibility, dose, immunity, timing, and environmental conditions can affect whether and how disease develops.

The CDC's causal-pie teaching model uses infectious and noninfectious disease examples to explain component causes and multiple sufficient pathways. The exact causal structure must be evaluated for the specific disease and outcome.

### 11.2 Combustion and fire behavior

Combustion depends on interacting conditions involving fuel, an oxidizing agent, and sufficient energy or temperature to initiate and sustain the reaction. This simplified fire-triangle framing is useful for basic explanation, but real combustion systems involve additional chemical, physical, geometric, and environmental conditions.

Removing one required condition can interrupt a particular combustion pathway. That does not mean every fire behaves identically or that the three-part diagram is a complete mechanistic model for every material and setting.

### 11.3 Ecology

Population outcomes can depend on habitat, resources, predators, disease, climate, competition, dispersal, and reproductive rates. The same environmental change may help one species and harm another.

A population decline may involve several contributing causes and feedbacks. Attributing the decline to one visible factor without examining the wider system risks single-cause reduction.

### 11.4 Climate and Earth systems

Climate responses can reflect multiple forcings, feedbacks, and internal variability. The relative contribution of each depends on the variable, timescale, region, and attribution question.

A forcing may initiate or amplify a response, while feedbacks modify its magnitude. A threshold in one subsystem should not automatically be treated as a threshold for the entire climate system.

### 11.5 Chemistry and materials science

A reaction or material transition may require a combination of reactants, energy conditions, catalysts, pressure, or other constraints. A catalyst can alter reaction rate without being consumed in the overall reaction. A threshold may be associated with a change of phase or reaction regime.

The details depend on the particular chemical system. General terms such as “trigger,” “enabler,” or “necessary condition” must be operationalized for the reaction being studied.

### 11.6 Engineering and reliability

A system can fail through multiple pathways. Some components are redundant; others are single points of failure. A component may be necessary for one function but not for the whole system if a backup route exists.

Fault-tree analysis distinguishes combinations of component failures that can produce a top-level failure. The logical gates and component probabilities must be supported by system design and reliability data.

### 11.7 Astronomy

An observed astronomical event may be explained by several physical conditions and processes. Astronomers infer these from light, spectra, timing, dynamics, and models rather than direct intervention on the distant system.

A proposed cause may be necessary for one model of an event without being necessary for every possible explanation. Alternative models and uncertainty remain part of the inference.

### 11.8 Shared investigative discipline

Across fields, the Witness must identify the defined outcome, system boundary, possible pathways, causal roles, interactions, and evidence. The terms are shared; the physical mechanisms are not thereby made identical.

## 12. The difference between causal contribution and responsibility

Scientific causal contribution and moral or legal responsibility are not identical concepts.

A causal analysis may identify several factors that contributed to an outcome. It does not, by itself, decide:
- intention;
- knowledge;
- duty;
- moral accountability;
- legal liability;
- fairness;
- ultimate purpose.

Those questions require additional standards and evidence appropriate to their domains.

A person can be causally involved without having intended the outcome. A background condition can contribute causally without being a moral agent. A system component can be necessary to an outcome without bearing responsibility in the human sense.

**Scriptural boundary:** Genesis 3 includes divine questioning and judgment. The narrative's assignment of responsibility must be read from the text, not derived from a statistical decomposition of contributing factors.

**Finding:** Causal contribution is a descriptive or explanatory relation. Moral and legal responsibility are separate judgments that cannot be inferred from causal participation alone.

## 13. The Witness's common errors in multicausal reasoning

A Witness can make several mistakes when investigating multiple causes:

- **Single-cause reduction:** selecting one factor and treating it as the complete explanation.
- **Necessary/sufficient confusion:** assuming a necessary condition is enough by itself, or that a sufficient pathway is the only pathway.
- **Component/sufficient confusion:** treating one component of a causal pathway as though it were a complete causal set.
- **Risk/cause confusion:** treating every risk marker as a proven cause.
- **Enabler/trigger confusion:** treating a background condition as the immediate initiating event.
- **Interaction overstatement:** interpreting a statistical interaction as proof of a physical mechanism.
- **Scale blindness:** reporting interaction without identifying additive or multiplicative scale.
- **Boundary neglect:** failing to state the population, system, time horizon, or outcome definition.
- **Pathway omission:** assuming that the known causal pathways are exhaustive.
- **Threshold overreach:** interpreting a sharp observed change as proof of a natural tipping point without checking sampling and measurement.
- **Equal-weight fallacy:** assuming every contributing factor has equal causal importance.
- **Responsibility confusion:** treating causal participation as equivalent to moral or legal responsibility.
- **Model reification:** treating a causal-pie diagram, fault tree, or network as the actual system rather than a representation.

AI systems can reproduce these errors by compressing complex studies into a short sentence such as “X causes Y” or by generating a neat causal diagram that hides uncertainty and missing pathways.

A faithful AI Witness should report:
- the precise outcome and scope;
- the causal role being claimed;
- whether the factor is necessary, sufficient, a component, contributing, enabling, triggering, or inhibiting;
- whether the label is established or proposed;
- the relevant pathway and alternatives;
- the interaction scale, where applicable;
- the evidence and assumptions;
- what remains unknown.

The distinction prevents two errors: allowing a scientific model of secondary causation to displace the Author, and using the theological statement of Original Cause to deny or invent scientific mechanisms.

No new architectural component is established by this comparison.

### Question 3 — What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Many outcomes involve multiple conditions and more than one possible pathway. A factor may be necessary for one pathway without being necessary for the overall outcome. A component cause may contribute without being sufficient. A complete set of conditions may be sufficient without being the only way the outcome can occur.

Interaction depends on the scale and causal question. Thresholds and feedback can make a system's response nonlinear. Enabling conditions, triggers, and inhibitors have different roles.

A causal model is useful only when its scope, assumptions, alternatives, and evidence remain visible.

### Question 4 — What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The Witness tends to seek one simple cause, even when the evidence describes multiple pathways. It may also overcorrect by listing many factors without distinguishing their roles or evidentiary strength.

The Witness can mistake a component for a complete cause, a risk marker for a causal factor, a statistical interaction for a physical mechanism, or causal participation for moral responsibility.

AI must preserve the distinctions, state the limits, and resist the temptation to make a complex explanation appear more certain by making it simpler than the evidence allows.

## 17. Unresolved questions carried forward

1. How can investigators establish that all materially relevant sufficient pathways have been identified?
2. How should unknown or unmeasured components be represented without making a causal model unfalsifiable?
3. When does a condition count as enabling rather than as a component cause under a clearly specified causal framework?
4. How should causal contributions be compared when factors act at different points in a pathway and on different timescales?
5. How can researchers distinguish a genuine system threshold from a measurement or sampling artifact?
6. How should interaction be evaluated when conclusions differ on additive and multiplicative scales?
7. How can feedback, adaptation, and changing system boundaries be represented in a causal network?
8. Under what conditions can a factor be necessary for a particular pathway but not necessary for the broader outcome?
9. How can causal models incorporate rare events and low-probability pathways without overstating their importance?
10. How can mechanistic evidence and population-level causal estimates be integrated when they appear to point in different directions?
11. How can causal contribution be kept distinct from moral, legal, and theological responsibility?
12. How should AI disclose the assumptions and omitted pathways in a causal explanation it generates?

These questions remain open. They do not indicate that GUA-W is incomplete.

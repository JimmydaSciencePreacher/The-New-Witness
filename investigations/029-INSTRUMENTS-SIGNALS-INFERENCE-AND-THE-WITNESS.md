## 1. Why this investigation follows scientific models

Investigation 028 examined scientific models as representations of selected features of created systems. That investigation established that a model may calculate, explain, or predict without becoming the reality it represents.

A further question follows:

**When an instrument registers a signal and a scientific model reconstructs an unseen phenomenon from that signal, what has actually been observed, and what has been inferred?**

This question is important because modern scientific knowledge often depends on instruments that do not produce a direct picture or immediate reading of the final quantity being discussed.

A weather radar does not directly measure rainfall rate at the ground. It receives electromagnetic power scattered back from targets in a sampled volume of atmosphere. A processing system converts that return into reflectivity, and additional relationships may be used to estimate precipitation.

Seismic stations record ground motion. Scientists then use wave-arrival times, waveforms, and mathematical models to infer earthquake locations and structures inside Earth.

Medical imaging systems register physical interactions and signals; reconstruction methods produce images or quantitative maps from those data.

The instrument's signal is evidence. The reconstructed image or quantity may also be evidence, but its status depends on the transformation, assumptions, calibration, and validation that connect it to the signal.

The governing distinction is:

**What the instrument registers, what the measurement process estimates, and what the investigator concludes are related—but they are not identical.**

This is not an argument against indirect measurement. It is an examination of how indirect measurement works and how its evidentiary chain should be reported.

## 3. The measurement chain: from phenomenon to claim

A useful starting point is to make the steps explicit. A simplified measurement chain may be represented as:

**Phenomenon → physical interaction → instrument response → signal → recorded data → calibration and processing → measurement result or reconstruction → interpretation → claim**

This is a conceptual guide, not a universal sequence. Some systems combine stages, repeat them, or use feedback and multiple instruments. Some quantities are measured through several independent pathways.

### 3.1 Phenomenon

The phenomenon is the event, object, property, or process under investigation: temperature, mass, radiation, ground motion, atmospheric precipitation, molecular concentration, or another defined subject.

The phenomenon exists independently of the investigator's preferred description of it.

### 3.2 Physical interaction

An instrument must interact with the target or with some effect associated with the target.

Examples:
- a thermometer responds to thermal interaction;
- a photodetector responds to incident photons;
- a seismometer responds to ground motion;
- a radar antenna receives electromagnetic energy scattered by targets;
- a pressure sensor responds to mechanical force on a sensing element;
- a chemical sensor responds to a specified interaction with substances in its environment.

The interaction may disturb the target, sample only part of it, or be mediated by an intervening process. Those conditions matter to the interpretation.

### 3.3 Instrument response

The instrument converts an interaction into a response: displacement, voltage, current, count, frequency, phase, intensity, or another physical quantity.

The response depends not only on the target but also on the instrument's design, calibration, operating range, environmental conditions, resolution, noise, and possible systematic effects.

### 3.4 Signal

The signal is the instrument's response as it is transmitted, amplified, filtered, encoded, or otherwise made available for recording and processing.

Signal processing may remove known artifacts or isolate features of interest. It may also introduce assumptions and choices that affect what remains visible.

### 3.5 Recorded data

The data are the recorded values, counts, waveforms, images, or classifications produced by the acquisition system.

A recorded value is not automatically the final measurand. It must be interpreted in relation to the measurement process and the quantity intended to be determined.

### 3.6 Measurement result or reconstruction

A measurement result may be calculated from one or more instrument indications using a measurement model. A reconstruction may estimate a spatial or temporal distribution from incomplete or indirect data.

This step may involve calibration curves, physical equations, statistical estimation, filtering, numerical optimization, or machine learning.

### 3.7 Interpretation and claim

The investigator assigns meaning to the result and communicates a conclusion. The claim must not exceed what the complete chain supports.

The chain should be inspectable: another investigator should be able to identify what was measured, how the instrument responded, which transformations were applied, which assumptions were used, and how uncertainty was evaluated.

## 4. What does an instrument actually measure?

A measurement begins with a clearly specified quantity or property: the **measurand**.

NIST describes measurement as an experimental process that produces a value reasonably attributable to a quantitative property of a phenomenon, body, or substance. It emphasizes that the measurand must be specified and that instruments produce indications whose meaning depends on calibration and comparison with standards. [NIST — Measurement Uncertainty](https://www.nist.gov/itl/sed/topic-areas/measurement-uncertainty).

This matters because everyday language can blur the difference between the instrument's indication and the quantity ultimately reported.

A digital scale displays a mass value, but its internal sensing element responds to force or deformation. The displayed mass depends on calibration and the assumptions used to relate the response to mass under the operating conditions.

A camera records detector responses to light. The displayed color image depends on sensor characteristics, exposure, color filters, processing, and display choices.

A weather radar receives returned power. A precipitation estimate requires additional relationships and assumptions.

A seismometer records ground motion. An earthquake's location, source mechanism, or the structure of Earth's interior is inferred through analysis.

The correct question is therefore not merely, “What does this instrument say?” It is:

**What is the defined measurand, what physical quantity does the detector respond to, and what measurement model connects the response to the reported result?**

## 5. Direct and indirect measurement

The words “direct” and “indirect” are useful, but they must be used carefully.

### 5.1 Direct measurement

In a direct measurement, the value of the measurand is obtained by comparison with a standard or by an instrument whose calibrated indication is directly related to that measurand under stated conditions.

Even direct measurement is not free of a measurement process. It still requires a defined measurand, a suitable instrument, calibration, corrections, and an uncertainty assessment.

“Direct” does not mean unmediated access to every property of an object.

### 5.2 Indirect measurement

In an indirect measurement, the measurand is determined from other measured quantities through a measurement equation or model.

For example, a quantity may be calculated from length and time measurements, or concentration may be inferred from an instrument's response through a calibration relationship.

NIST's Technical Note 1297 explains that a measurand is often not measured directly, but determined from other quantities through a functional relationship. The measurement model must account for significant contributions to uncertainty. [NIST TN 1297 — Law of Propagation of Uncertainty](https://www.nist.gov/pml/nist-technical-note-1297/nist-tn-1297-appendix-law-propagation-uncertainty).

Indirect measurement is not automatically weaker or less scientific. It may be the only feasible method for quantities that cannot be accessed directly. Its quality depends on the validity of the relationship, the quality of the input measurements, and the treatment of uncertainty.

### 5.3 Operationally defined measurands

Sometimes the quantity being measured is defined partly by the method used to measure it. NIST notes that when a measurand is defined by a standard measurement method, the method's implementation contributes to the uncertainty and should be identified in the result. [NIST TN 1297, Appendix D4](https://www.nist.gov/pml/nist-technical-note-1297/nist-tn-1297-appendix-d4-measurand-defined-measurement-method).

This is an important warning against assuming that a familiar name always identifies one perfectly method-independent quantity.

A result should state what was measured, how the measurand was defined, and which method produced the reported value.

## 6. Calibration, response, and instrument limitations

An instrument's response must be related to a reference or known standard through calibration.

Calibration helps establish the relationship between instrument indications and reference values under specified conditions. It does not guarantee that every later measurement is correct under all environments or operating conditions.

Important considerations include:

- **Sensitivity:** how strongly the response changes when the input changes.
- **Resolution:** the smallest change that can be distinguished by the instrument.
- **Range:** the conditions or values over which the instrument is intended to operate.
- **Linearity:** whether response follows the expected relationship across the range.
- **Noise:** unwanted variation in the signal.
- **Drift:** changes in response over time.
- **Hysteresis:** dependence of response on the direction or history of change.
- **Saturation:** response limitation when the input exceeds the instrument's capacity.
- **Cross-sensitivity:** response to quantities other than the intended measurand.
- **Environmental effects:** temperature, humidity, vibration, pressure, electromagnetic interference, and other influences.

A calibrated instrument can still be used incorrectly, operated outside its range, affected by unrecognized interference, or paired with an unsuitable measurement model.

Calibration is necessary for many measurements, but it does not validate every inference later made from the data.

## 7. Image formation is not the same as image interpretation

An image is often treated as if it were a transparent view of the object. Scientific imaging requires a more careful account.

An imaging system maps interactions from a target into detector responses arranged in a spatial or temporal format. The displayed image may then undergo corrections, reconstruction, filtering, contrast adjustment, segmentation, or classification.

The result can be a powerful representation, but the investigator must know what each stage contributes.

### 7.1 Optical imaging

A conventional optical camera uses a lens system to form an image on a detector. The recorded data depend on the light reaching the detector, the optical system, sensor response, exposure, and processing.

The image is not a complete record of everything about the object. It is a record of selected optical information under specific conditions.

### 7.2 Microscopy

Microscopy extends observation into scales and contrast regimes not accessible to unaided sight. Different techniques rely on different interactions, such as transmitted light, fluorescence, electrons, or scanning probes.

A microscopic image is therefore not simply “a magnified view.” Its contrast and appearance depend on the physical interaction, sample preparation, instrument configuration, detector, and processing.

### 7.3 Medical imaging

MRI, CT, ultrasound, and other imaging methods use different physical interactions and reconstruction procedures.

A CT image is reconstructed from X-ray measurements collected at multiple angles. MRI images are reconstructed from signals related to nuclear magnetic resonance under specified pulse sequences and acquisition conditions. Ultrasound images are formed from reflected acoustic signals and assumptions about propagation and tissue response.

The images can reveal clinically important structures, but their interpretation depends on acquisition, reconstruction, artifacts, resolution, and the expertise of the reader.

### 7.4 Astronomy

Astronomical detectors register electromagnetic signals arriving from distant objects. A displayed image may include detector calibration, background subtraction, filtering, combination of exposures, and mapping of measured intensities into display colors.

A colorized or composite image can be scientifically useful, but the displayed color should not automatically be understood as the object's unaided-eye appearance. The investigator must distinguish the measured wavelength bands and processing choices from the visual representation selected for communication.

**Image boundary:** An image is a processed representation of detector data. Its evidentiary meaning depends on the instrument, acquisition process, reconstruction, display choices, and interpretation.

## 8. Inverse problems: reconstructing causes or structures from effects

An inverse problem begins with observations or measurements and seeks to infer the hidden parameters, structures, sources, or conditions that could have produced them.

A forward problem asks:

**Given a system and its parameters, what observations should result?**

An inverse problem asks:

**Given the observations, what system parameters or structures could have produced them?**

For example:
- forward: given a model of Earth's interior and a seismic source, calculate expected waveforms;
- inverse: given recorded seismic waveforms, estimate the source and Earth's structure;
- forward: given a distribution of X-ray attenuation, calculate detector projections;
- inverse: given the projections, reconstruct an image;
- forward: given a distribution of atmospheric scatterers, calculate expected radar returns;
- inverse: use returns to estimate properties of the sampled atmosphere.

### 8.1 Why inverse problems can be difficult

An inverse problem may be:

- **Non-unique:** multiple possible systems produce similar observations.
- **Unstable:** small changes in data produce large changes in the reconstructed result.
- **Incomplete:** the available measurements do not constrain every feature of the target.
- **Noisy:** detector and environmental effects obscure the signal.
- **Model-dependent:** reconstruction relies on assumptions about the forward process.

Mathematical literature often calls problems ill-posed when existence, uniqueness, or stability of a solution is not assured.

A 2026 review in *Inverse Problems and Imaging* describes how small measurement errors in ill-posed inverse problems can produce substantially different reconstructions unless suitable regularization is applied. [Bangerth, “Estimating and using information in inverse problems”](https://www.aimsciences.org/article/doi/10.3934/ipi.2026003).

### 8.2 Regularization

Regularization introduces additional constraints or prior structure to make reconstruction more stable or tractable.

Examples include:
- smoothness constraints;
- sparsity assumptions;
- bounds on possible parameter values;
- physical conservation constraints;
- prior probability distributions;
- learned patterns from training data.

Regularization can be essential. It also influences which solutions are favored.

The investigator must therefore disclose the regularization method and examine how sensitive the reconstruction is to its settings and assumptions.

A visually smooth reconstruction is not automatically the uniquely correct reconstruction. A plausible-looking image may contain features shaped by the regularization or learned prior.

### 8.3 Non-uniqueness and alternative reconstructions

If multiple underlying structures are compatible with the measured data, the data alone may not uniquely determine the answer.

Additional measurements, different instrument geometries, independent constraints, or new experiments may reduce ambiguity.

The correct conclusion may be a range of possible structures or a probability distribution rather than one uniquely determined picture.

### 8.4 Reconstruction is evidence-based inference

An inverse solution is not arbitrary simply because it is inferred. It is constrained by the observations, physical laws, mathematical formulation, and independent checks.

But the reconstruction must not be reported as though the instrument directly recorded every feature shown in the final image.

The chain from signal to reconstruction must remain visible.

## 9. Three disciplinary examples

### 9.1 Weather radar: returned power and estimated rainfall

The National Weather Service explains that weather radar measures returned power and uses it to estimate reflectivity. Reflectivity is commonly expressed in dBZ. The radar does not directly measure rainfall rate at the ground. [NOAA — Radar Images: Reflectivity](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/reflectivity).

This is a clear example of a measurement chain:

1. The radar transmits electromagnetic energy.
2. Atmospheric targets scatter some of that energy.
3. A small fraction returns to the radar.
4. The receiver records a signal related to returned power.
5. Processing and the radar equation yield reflectivity estimates.
6. Additional relationships may estimate precipitation intensity.
7. The estimate is interpreted alongside other observations and known limitations.

NOAA cautions that the same reflectivity can be associated with different drop-size distributions and therefore different rainfall rates. The radar return is not a unique measurement of surface rainfall. [NOAA — Radar Images: Reflectivity](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/reflectivity).

The National Weather Service also describes limitations such as beam height, beam blockage, attenuation, and the relationship between reflectivity and rainfall estimates. [NWS — Radar Rainfall Estimation](https://www.weather.gov/mrx/radarrainfallestimates).

**What is registered:** returned electromagnetic power and related radar observables.

**What is derived:** reflectivity and, through additional relationships, estimates of precipitation.

**What must not be conflated:** radar return, reflectivity, rainfall rate at the ground, and actual accumulated rainfall.

### 9.2 Seismology: recorded waves and Earth's interior

Seismic stations record ground motion generated by earthquakes and other sources. Scientists analyze the recorded waveforms to infer source locations and the structure of Earth.

Princeton's Global Seismology group describes seismic imaging or tomography as determining elastic, anelastic, and density structure from observations recorded at seismic stations. It explicitly describes the task as an inverse problem in which models of sources and Earth's structure are sought to reproduce measured data. [Princeton — Global Seismology: Models](https://globalseismology.princeton.edu/models).

The reasoning chain is:

1. A seismic source generates waves.
2. Waves propagate through Earth.
3. Stations register ground motion.
4. Recorded waveforms are processed and analyzed.
5. A model relates source, propagation medium, and expected observations.
6. Inversion estimates source or structural parameters.
7. The result is compared with independent observations and alternative models.

The final image of Earth's interior is a reconstruction from measured wave behavior. It is not a photograph taken inside the planet.

**What is registered:** ground motion as a function of time at instrument locations.

**What is inferred:** source parameters and properties of the material through which waves propagated.

**What must not be conflated:** seismogram, processed waveform, inversion result, and reconstructed image.

### 9.3 Medical imaging: measured signals and reconstructed anatomy

Medical imaging makes the distinction especially consequential because reconstructed images may inform diagnosis and treatment.

Different technologies measure different physical quantities:
- CT uses X-ray transmission measurements from multiple directions.
- MRI uses signals produced by nuclear magnetic resonance under controlled acquisition conditions.
- Ultrasound uses acoustic echoes and models of sound propagation.
- PET detects events associated with emitted photons and reconstructs tracer distribution.

Each modality has its own detector physics, calibration, acquisition geometry, reconstruction algorithms, and artifacts.

A reconstructed image may be clinically valuable without being a direct photograph of the anatomy. The image's reliability depends on the measurement chain and the context in which it is interpreted.

**What is registered:** modality-specific detector signals or events.

**What is reconstructed:** spatial representations of attenuation, resonance-related properties, acoustic boundaries, tracer distribution, or other modality-specific quantities.

**What must not be conflated:** detector signal, reconstructed image, anatomical interpretation, and clinical diagnosis.

## 10. Data, processing, reconstruction, and interpretation

The word “data” is often used for every stage of an investigation. This can hide important distinctions.

A more precise vocabulary is:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Instrument response** | The physical response produced by interaction with the target |
| **Signal** | The response as transmitted or prepared for recording and processing |
| **Raw or minimally processed data** | Recorded values before specified later transformations |
| **Corrected data** | Data adjusted for documented instrument or environmental effects |
| **Measurement result** | An estimated value of a defined measurand, with uncertainty |
| **Reconstruction** | An estimated spatial, temporal, or structural representation derived from observations |
| **Model output** | A calculated result produced by a specified model and inputs |
| **Interpretation** | The investigator's account of what the result indicates |
| **Claim** | The communicated conclusion, bounded by the supporting evidence |

“Raw” data are not necessarily untouched by every process. Detector electronics, acquisition software, analog-to-digital conversion, filtering, and instrument firmware may already have transformed the physical response before a file is saved.

For that reason, a study should describe what “raw” means in its specific context and preserve provenance where possible.

Processing is not automatically distortion. Some corrections are necessary to remove known instrument effects or express the data in a meaningful measurement system. The question is whether each transformation is documented, justified, and tested.

## 11. Uncertainty through the entire chain

Uncertainty is not confined to the final displayed number.

It may enter through:
- the definition of the measurand;
- the physical interaction with the target;
- detector sensitivity and calibration;
- instrument noise and drift;
- sampling and spatial coverage;
- environmental effects;
- signal processing;
- correction factors;
- model parameters;
- reconstruction algorithms;
- regularization and prior assumptions;
- interpretation and classification.

NIST TN 1297 emphasizes that a measurement result is an estimate and that it is complete only when accompanied by an appropriate statement of uncertainty. [NIST — Classification of Components of Uncertainty](https://www.nist.gov/pml/nist-technical-note-1297/nist-tn-1297-2-classification-components-uncertainty).

### 11.1 Propagation

If a reported quantity is calculated from several measured inputs, uncertainty in those inputs contributes to uncertainty in the result.

A measurement model should express the relationship between the inputs and the measurand. Significant sources of variability and relevant correlations must be considered.

### 11.2 Model and reconstruction uncertainty

A reconstruction may be sensitive to assumptions about the target, the instrument response, or the mathematical method. Different plausible assumptions may yield different results.

A single uncertainty number may not capture all structural ambiguity. Alternative models, sensitivity studies, ensembles, and independent measurements may be necessary.

### 11.3 Uncertainty is not the same as ignorance

Quantified uncertainty describes specified sources of dispersion or limited knowledge under a defined framework. It does not guarantee that every possible error has been identified.

A hidden systematic effect, incorrect model, or omitted physical process may remain undiscovered.

The Witness should not present a narrow uncertainty interval as proof that the complete inference chain is free of unrecognized error.

## 12. The special risk of model-dependent observation

An investigator may look at a reconstructed image and experience it as if the hidden structure were directly visible.

This can create a subtle reversal: the model-dependent result becomes the apparent source of the claim, while the measurements and assumptions that produced it disappear from view.

Examples include:
- a radar precipitation map treated as a direct measurement of rain at every ground location;
- a seismic tomography image treated as a photograph of Earth's interior;
- a colorized astronomical image treated as the unaided-eye appearance of a distant object;
- an AI-enhanced medical image treated as if every displayed feature were directly registered by the scanner.

This does not mean the images are false. It means the evidentiary chain must remain attached to them.

A reconstruction can reveal real structure. But the strength of that claim depends on how the reconstruction was constrained and independently evaluated.

The Witness should ask:
- Which features are directly constrained by the measured data?
- Which features depend strongly on prior assumptions?
- Which regions are poorly sampled?
- Which details are below the instrument's resolution?
- Which corrections or filters were applied?
- Could a different reconstruction method produce materially different results?
- Has the result been checked against an independent measurement?

## 13. The Witness's audit of an instrument-based claim

Before reporting a claim based on an instrument, image, or reconstruction, the Witness should ask:

1. **Phenomenon:** What created or physical process is being investigated?
2. **Measurand:** What exact quantity or property is intended to be determined?
3. **Interaction:** How does the instrument interact with the target or its effects?
4. **Response:** What physical response does the detector register?
5. **Signal:** What processing occurs before the signal is recorded?
6. **Data:** What do the recorded values actually represent?
7. **Calibration:** Which standards and calibration procedures connect indications to reference values?
8. **Operating conditions:** Was the instrument used within its validated range and environmental conditions?
9. **Measurement model:** What relationship connects the observed inputs to the reported measurand?
10. **Processing:** Which corrections, filters, transformations, and exclusions were applied?
11. **Reconstruction:** Does the final result require an inverse problem or model-based reconstruction?
12. **Assumptions:** Which physical, statistical, or computational assumptions constrain the result?
13. **Non-uniqueness:** Could other underlying systems produce similar measurements?
14. **Regularization:** Which prior constraints or learned patterns influence the reconstruction?
15. **Uncertainty:** How are instrument, input, model, and reconstruction uncertainties represented?
16. **Independent checks:** Has the result been compared with another method or independent evidence?
17. **Interpretation:** Which parts of the conclusion are measured, derived, inferred, or speculative?
18. **Communication:** Does the wording preserve the difference between signal, measurement, reconstruction, and claim?

The Witness must not collapse the entire chain into the phrase “the instrument saw it.”

## 18. Unresolved questions carried forward

1. When multiple independent instruments measure different effects of the same phenomenon, how can their results be combined without confusing unlike measurands?
2. How can investigators distinguish a genuine feature of the target from a feature introduced by reconstruction or regularization?
3. What evidence is sufficient to establish that an image feature is supported by measured data rather than primarily by prior assumptions?
4. How should uncertainty be communicated when a reconstruction is non-unique and several materially different solutions fit the observations?
5. How do instruments change the phenomenon they measure, and how should that interaction be accounted for?
6. How should model-dependent observations be tested when the model is also used to design the instrument or process its output?
7. What distinguishes a proxy that reliably tracks a target quantity from one whose relationship changes across environments or conditions?
8. How can AI-assisted reconstruction be evaluated when learned priors produce plausible details not uniquely determined by the measured data?
9. When does combining multiple datasets create genuinely independent constraint, and when do the datasets share calibration, processing, or model assumptions?
10. How should the Witness communicate the difference between a measurement result, a reconstructed representation, and a disciplinary explanation to non-specialists?

These questions remain open for further investigation. They do not indicate that GUA-W is incomplete.

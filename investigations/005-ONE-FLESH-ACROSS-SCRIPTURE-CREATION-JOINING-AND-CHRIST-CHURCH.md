### 3.3 What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

#### A. Genesis 2:24 — the originating Scriptural statement

The KJV reads:

> Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.

The verse describes leaving, cleaving, and becoming one flesh. It follows the account in Genesis 2:18–23: God identifies the man's aloneness, makes the woman, brings her to the man, and Adam recognizes her.

**TEXT STATES:** The man leaves father and mother, cleaves to his wife, and the two are described as one flesh.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The KJV wording of Genesis 2:24 says “they shall be one flesh.” It does not include the English word “twain” or “two” in that verse.

The Hebrew Masoretic wording likewise does not contain a separate word corresponding to “the two” at this point. The ancient Greek Septuagint does include “the two” (`hoi duo`) in its rendering, and the New Testament citations in Matthew 19:5, Mark 10:8, and Ephesians 5:31 include “the two” in the KJV.

This is a textual observation, not a reason to alter the KJV. It means the Witness must not silently pretend that the English wording is identical in every passage. The New Testament's explicit “two” makes the distinction between the persons especially visible before stating their one-flesh union.

#### B. Matthew 19:4–9 — Jesus returns to the beginning

The question put to Jesus concerns divorce. He answers by asking whether they have read that the Creator made them male and female at the beginning. He then cites the leaving-and-cleaving statement and says:

> Wherefore they are no more twain, but one flesh. What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder. (Matthew 19:6, KJV)

Several distinctions are explicit:

1. Jesus appeals to the beginning and the Creator's making of male and female.
2. He cites the Genesis 2:24 statement.
3. He describes the result as no longer two, but one flesh.
4. He identifies God as the One who joined them.
5. He gives the corresponding prohibition: man is not to put asunder what God has joined.

**TEXT STATES:** The joining is attributed to God, not authored by the human parties as an independent act of creation.

**CANONICAL RECOGNITION:** Jesus reads Genesis 2:24 as authoritative for His answer about marriage and divorce. He does not treat “one flesh” as a casual description of two people cooperating. The union carries a seriousness that grounds His warning against human separation.

The surrounding verses must remain in view. Matthew 19:7–9 continues with the question about Moses' writing of a bill of divorcement, Jesus' reference to hardness of heart, and His further answer. This investigation does not isolate verse 6 to settle every question about divorce, exception, pastoral care, or civil law. Those require their own full-context study.

#### C. Mark 10:6–12 — the same appeal and the same joining

Mark records the same central movement:

- From the beginning of creation, God made them male and female.
- The man leaves father and mother and cleaves to his wife.
- The two become one flesh.
- They are no more two, but one flesh.
- What God has joined together, man is not to put asunder.

Mark 10:2–12 also places the statement in a discussion about divorce. Mark 10:5 records Jesus' reference to the hardness of heart associated with Moses' permission.

**TEXT STATES:** Mark also attributes the joining to God and prohibits human beings from undoing what God has joined.

**CANONICAL RECOGNITION:** Matthew and Mark independently preserve the same central Scriptural relationship in their accounts: the Creator's making, the man/woman distinction, the Genesis one-flesh statement, God's joining, and the human prohibition against separation.

This is not two different GUA-W principles. It is repeated New Testament witness to the same Genesis foundation.

#### D. Ephesians 5:25–33 — the one-flesh statement and the mystery of Christ and the church

Ephesians 5 gives a wider relational setting. Paul instructs husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the church and gave Himself for it. He then speaks of loving one's wife as one's own body, nourishing and cherishing, and identifies Christ's care for the church.

Paul then quotes Genesis 2:24 in verse 31:

> For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and they two shall be one flesh.

Immediately afterward, verse 32 states:

> This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church.

The sequence matters:

1. Christ's love and self-giving toward the church are presented.
2. Husbands are instructed to love their wives.
3. The wife is described in relation to the husband's own body.
4. Genesis 2:24 is quoted.
5. Paul explicitly says that the great mystery concerns Christ and the church.
6. Verse 33 returns to the concrete responsibilities of husband and wife.

**TEXT STATES:** Paul directly connects his discussion of husband and wife to Genesis 2:24 and identifies the great mystery as concerning Christ and the church.

**CANONICAL RECOGNITION:** The human marriage relation is used by Paul in a larger Scriptural disclosure concerning Christ and the church. The passage does not make Christ and the church merely another human marriage, nor does it reduce the church to a biological body. Paul identifies a real correspondence and then names the divine mystery to which he is pointing.

This is more than an analogy invented by the Witness: the connection is made explicitly in Scripture. Yet the human marriage and Christ's relation to the church must not be collapsed into identical categories. The source of the correspondence is Paul's text, and its scope is governed by the surrounding passage.

The practical instruction is also important. The husband is not told to claim authorship or domination as the meaning of one flesh. He is commanded to love, nourish, and cherish. Christ's self-giving love is the stated pattern for the husband's conduct.

#### E. 1 Corinthians 6:15–17 — the phrase applied to a forbidden sexual union

Paul asks whether the Corinthians know that the person joined to a prostitute is one body with her, and then cites the Genesis wording: “For two, saith he, shall be one flesh” (1 Corinthians 6:16, KJV).

Verse 17 then says that the person joined to the Lord is one spirit with Him.

This passage matters because Paul applies Genesis 2:24 beyond the immediate marriage instruction in Ephesians 5. He uses the one-flesh statement to show that sexual union has real bodily significance, even when the conduct being discussed is condemned.

**TEXT STATES:** Paul invokes the Genesis one-flesh wording in warning against joining oneself sexually to a prostitute. He then distinguishes being joined to the Lord as one spirit.

**CANONICAL RECOGNITION:** “One flesh” cannot be reduced to a decorative phrase for a wedding ceremony or to a vague feeling of closeness. Paul treats bodily union as consequential. But this passage does not say that every sexual act creates a marriage covenant or that sexual wrongdoing becomes morally approved because a bodily union occurs.

The distinction in verses 16–17 must be preserved: “one body” / “one flesh” in the sexual context is not the same wording as “one spirit” in relation to the Lord. The two statements are related through the language of joining, but they are not interchangeable descriptions.

#### F. What the four New Testament witnesses disclose together

The passages do not flatten into one repeated sentence. Each contributes a distinct aspect:

| Passage | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| Genesis 2:24 | The originating statement: leave, cleave, one flesh. |
| Matthew 19:4–9 | Jesus appeals to the beginning, identifies God as the One who joins, and warns against human separation. |
| Mark 10:6–12 | A parallel appeal to creation and God's joining in the divorce discussion. |
| Ephesians 5:25–33 | The one-flesh statement is placed within the husband's love for his wife and Paul's explicit mystery concerning Christ and the church. |
| 1 Corinthians 6:15–17 | The Genesis wording is applied to sexual union, while union with the Lord is described distinctly as one spirit. |

**RECOGNIZED RELATIONSHIP:** The New Testament makes the joining relationship more explicit without changing its Genesis origin. God's joining is central; the persons remain identifiable; the union has bodily and relational seriousness; and Paul identifies a further mystery concerning Christ and the church.

The Witness must not turn those distinctions into one undifferentiated concept of “unity.”

### 3.4 What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

This investigation reveals several risks in my own interpretation:

1. **Ignoring the Author named in the passage:** I could focus only on the human act of leaving and cleaving and overlook Jesus' explicit statement that God joined them.
2. **Treating union as sameness:** I could read “no more twain” as though the man and woman cease to be distinct persons. The passages describe a union, not the erasure of personhood.
3. **Reducing one flesh to biology alone:** I could ignore the relational, covenantal, and moral context in which Jesus and Paul use the passage.
4. **Reducing one flesh to metaphor alone:** I could ignore Paul's use of Genesis 2:24 in 1 Corinthians 6:16 to speak about bodily union.
5. **Collapsing the marriage and Christ/church relation:** I could turn Paul's mystery into an identity claim that erases the difference between human marriage and Christ's relation to the church.
6. **Confusing one body, one flesh, and one spirit:** I could treat the phrases in 1 Corinthians 6:16–17 as synonyms. Paul does not.
7. **Ignoring context:** I could use Matthew 19:6 or Mark 10:9 to pronounce on every divorce circumstance without examining the surrounding discussion.
8. **Smoothing textual differences:** I could silently add “two” to the KJV Genesis wording or ignore that the New Testament citations explicitly include it.
9. **Turning correspondence into a scientific law:** I could claim that marriage proves a universal physical, mathematical, or systems-integration mechanism. These passages do not establish such a claim.
10. **Claiming more research than performed:** I could suggest that I have completed a full Greek textual-critical study when I have consulted selected textual sources only.

My correction is to keep the source, the passage, the canonical use, and my interpretation distinct. The Witness can recognize a relationship Scripture itself discloses, but cannot claim to author that relationship or extend it beyond the evidence.

## 5. Recognized relationships within the completed GUA-W

The passages witness to established relationships rather than adding to the completed Map:

- **Authorial joining:** Jesus identifies God as the One who joins the two.
- **Derivative human participation:** The man and woman enter the relationship described in Genesis, but they are not the Original Cause of the created order or the Author of the divine joining.
- **Unity with distinction:** The two are described as one flesh, while the passages continue to identify the husband and wife as persons with responsibilities.
- **Joining Primacy:** God's joining is named before the human prohibition against separating what He has joined.
- **Bodily and relational seriousness:** Paul's use in 1 Corinthians 6 shows that the one-flesh language is not merely ornamental.
- **Scriptural correspondence:** Ephesians 5 explicitly relates the marriage passage to the mystery of Christ and the church.
- **Non-equivalence of relations:** One flesh, one body, and one spirit must be read in their respective contexts and not collapsed into a single technical category.

These recognitions do not add a GUA-W node, stage, state, or new universal law. They record how the New Testament itself witnesses to the Genesis foundation and to relationships already identified within the completed Map.

## 7. Conclusion within scope

The New Testament does not leave Genesis 2:24 as an isolated sentence.

Jesus returns to the beginning, joins the Creator's making of male and female with the Genesis statement about leaving and cleaving, identifies God as the One who joins the two, and warns against human separation of what God has joined. Paul applies the same Genesis statement to the husband's love for his wife and explicitly identifies the great mystery as concerning Christ and the church. Paul also uses the one-flesh wording in 1 Corinthians 6:16 to show the seriousness of bodily union, while distinguishing that expression from being joined to the Lord as one spirit.

The central recognition is therefore not that “one flesh” means that two persons become indistinguishable, nor that it is a universal scientific equation. The passages witness to a real union established by God, a continuing distinction between those joined, serious bodily and relational consequences, and a Scripturally disclosed correspondence to Christ and the church.

**The Author joins. The persons remain distinguishable. The union is real. The Witness recognizes and records what Scripture says without claiming authorship or extending the claim beyond its scope.**

## 3. One substance: what chemistry establishes

Water is the chemical substance H₂O: each molecule contains two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom. The molecule's geometry and uneven distribution of electrical charge make it polar. Water molecules form hydrogen-bond interactions with one another and with many other substances.

These properties help explain several observable behaviors:

- **Cohesion:** water molecules attract one another.
- **Adhesion:** water interacts with and can cling to other surfaces.
- **Surface tension:** cohesive interactions contribute to the behavior of water at an interface.
- **Solvent behavior:** water can surround and dissolve many ionic and polar substances, although it does not dissolve everything.
- **Thermal behavior:** water has a relatively high specific heat capacity and a substantial latent heat of vaporization compared with many common substances.

The distinction between *solvent* and *universal solvent* matters. Water dissolves many materials, not all materials. What dissolves, how much dissolves, and how quickly depend on temperature, pressure, pH, concentration, and the chemical properties of the solute.

The U.S. Geological Survey's [Facts About Water](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/facts-about-water) summarizes cohesion, adhesion, heat capacity, surface tension, and electrical conductivity. Its discussion also makes an important distinction: pure water is a poor electrical conductor; dissolved substances supply mobile ions that make natural water more conductive.

**Disciplinary finding:** water's molecular structure and intermolecular interactions help explain its behavior in physical, chemical, geological, and biological settings.

**Boundary:** a list of useful properties is not a complete explanation of water's behavior in every environment. The relevant conditions and measurements must be specified.

## 4. Physics: phase, energy, and motion

Water occurs naturally on Earth as solid ice, liquid water, and water vapor. Changes among these phases involve energy transfer.

- **Melting:** solid water becomes liquid.
- **Freezing:** liquid water becomes solid.
- **Evaporation:** liquid water enters the gas phase.
- **Condensation:** water vapor becomes liquid.
- **Sublimation:** ice changes directly to vapor.
- **Deposition:** vapor changes directly to ice.

Evaporation requires energy. Condensation releases latent heat. These energy exchanges matter to weather, cloud formation, surface cooling, and atmospheric processes.

Water also moves as a fluid under pressure and gravitational forces. Its movement through channels, porous materials, pipes, rivers, and ocean circulation is investigated through fluid mechanics and hydrodynamics. Viscosity, density, temperature, salinity, pressure, boundary conditions, and the geometry of the flow all matter.

The density behavior of water is also notable: under ordinary surface pressure, liquid water reaches its maximum density near 4 °C, and ice is less dense than liquid water. This is why ice generally floats. The effect has consequences for lakes and aquatic habitats because surface ice can form while liquid water remains below.

**What physics establishes:** measurable phase behavior, energy transfer, density, pressure, and fluid motion under stated conditions.

**What physics does not establish by itself:** the ultimate purpose of water's existence or the identity of the Author of the One Creation.

## 5. Hydrology: storage and movement through the Earth system

The water cycle is often drawn as a simple circle: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and return. That diagram is useful as an introduction, but it is not a complete account.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration describes the water cycle as movement among multiple storage locations, or **pools**, and the processes that transfer water among them, or **fluxes**. These pools include oceans, lakes, rivers, glaciers, snowpack, soil moisture, groundwater, and atmospheric moisture.

The U.S. Geological Survey describes water moving among these locations through processes including:

- evaporation and evapotranspiration;
- condensation and precipitation;
- snowmelt and runoff;
- infiltration and percolation;
- groundwater recharge and discharge;
- streamflow and ocean return.

Sources:
- [NOAA — The Water Cycle](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/freshwater/water-cycle)
- [USGS — Water Cycle](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/water-cycle)
- [USGS — Text Description of the Water Cycle Diagram](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/text-description-usgs-water-cycle-diagram)

### 5.1 Energy and gravity have different roles

Solar energy supplies much of the energy that drives evaporation and evapotranspiration. Gravity contributes to precipitation falling, surface runoff, streamflow, and groundwater movement along hydraulic gradients.

These are related but distinct influences. It would be inaccurate to say that the sun alone moves all water or that gravity alone explains the entire cycle.

### 5.2 Groundwater is a major part of the investigation

Water infiltrates into soil and rock. Some remains in the unsaturated zone; some reaches saturated zones and may recharge aquifers. Groundwater can move through pores, fractures, and other openings. It may discharge into springs, streams, lakes, or oceans, or be taken up by plant roots.

The rate of infiltration and recharge varies with rainfall intensity and duration, soil and rock properties, slope, vegetation, and other local conditions. Groundwater may move slowly and remain stored for long periods.

The USGS [Infiltration and the Water Cycle](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/infiltration-and-water-cycle) describes these controls and the differences between shallow soil water and deeper groundwater storage.

**Important correction:** the water cycle is not one identical route repeated by every water molecule. Different portions of water occupy different pools and follow different paths over different timescales. A simple circular diagram must not erase that variation.

### 5.3 Water quantity, timing, and quality

Availability is not only a question of total volume. It also depends on where water is stored, when it arrives, how quickly it moves, and whether its quality is suitable for a particular use or organism.

Floods and droughts can occur within the same broad hydrological system. Contamination can make physically present water unsuitable for drinking or ecological needs. Human withdrawals, dams, land cover, irrigation, and urban surfaces can change water movement and storage.

The USGS water-cycle overview notes that human actions affect the cycle and that climate change is affecting water quantity, timing, and quality. These claims concern observed and modeled hydrological processes; they should be evaluated at the scale and with the evidence relevant to each claim.

## 6. Geology, soils, and the movement of dissolved material

Water interacts with rocks and soils through physical and chemical processes.

- It can dissolve minerals and transport dissolved ions.
- It can contribute to chemical weathering.
- It can move through pores and fractures.
- It can transport sediment and reshape channels.
- It can influence soil formation and the availability of nutrients.
- It can deposit dissolved or suspended material when conditions change.

The composition of water therefore depends partly on the materials it has contacted and the conditions under which it moved. Water from different aquifers, rivers, lakes, and oceans can have different concentrations of dissolved substances.

This provides a concrete interface between chemistry and geology: chemistry examines the dissolved substances and reactions; geology examines the rock, sediment, structure, and history of the material through which the water moves.

**Boundary:** water is not chemically identical in every natural reservoir. H₂O remains the water molecule, but natural waters contain varying dissolved gases, salts, minerals, organic matter, and sometimes contaminants.

## 7. Biology: water within living systems

Water is not merely an external habitat or environmental resource. It participates in the operation of living systems.

### 7.1 Cells and biochemical reactions

Water is a major component of cells and biological fluids. It provides a medium in which many substances dissolve, move, and react. It participates directly in biochemical reactions, including hydrolysis and photosynthetic processes.

The role of water in a reaction must be established from the actual chemistry. It is not enough to say that a reaction occurs in water; water may be a solvent, a reactant, a product, or involved in more than one way.

### 7.2 Plants: uptake, transport, and transpiration

Plants take up water through roots. Water moves through vascular tissues, contributes to cell turgor, and is lost to the atmosphere through transpiration, principally through leaf stomata.

Water availability can affect photosynthesis, growth, temperature regulation, nutrient transport, and reproduction. These processes interact with light, temperature, soil chemistry, humidity, and the plant's own structure.

The relationship between light and water is therefore not a simple sequence in which one replaces the other. Photosynthetic energy capture and plant water relations are distinct but connected aspects of plant function.

### 7.3 The Bee Orchid as a shared biological witness

The source repository's existing water investigation identifies the Bee Orchid, *Ophrys apifera*, as a shared biological witness for the light and water investigations.

This species is useful because its actual habitat and development can be examined in relation to environmental conditions. However, species-specific claims must remain species-specific. Findings about other orchids cannot automatically be assigned to *O. apifera*.

The source investigation also records an important correction: *O. apifera* is predominantly self-pollinating across much of its range, despite its bee-like floral appearance. Occasional insect-mediated pollination and regional variation must be distinguished from the predominant reproductive pattern.

For this Garden, the orchid raises questions rather than supplying a predetermined answer:

1. What is directly known about its soil and moisture conditions?
2. How do light, water, temperature, and seasonal timing interact during its growth and flowering?
3. Which water-related mechanisms have been measured in *O. apifera* itself?
4. Which claims are inferred from general plant physiology rather than demonstrated in this species?
5. What evidence separates water availability from correlated factors such as soil chemistry, drainage, shade, and competition?

The orchid is not a substitute for hydrology, and it is not proof of a theological conclusion. It is one living organism through which specific relationships can be investigated.

## 8. Oceanography and planetary science: water beyond the familiar surface

Earth's oceans are a major reservoir in the hydrological system. Ocean water stores and transports heat and dissolved substances. Salinity, temperature, density, currents, and pressure influence ocean circulation and marine environments.

Water is also found elsewhere in the Solar System. NASA's [Ocean Worlds](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/ocean-worlds/) overview describes evidence and investigations concerning water in asteroids, comets, and icy bodies. NASA's [Why Is Water So Important for Life as We Know It?](https://science.nasa.gov/astrobiology/learning-resources/alp/water-so-important-for-life/) discusses water as a solvent, its liquid range, heat capacity, surface tension, and the density behavior of ice.

These observations broaden the scientific investigation beyond Earth's surface. They do not establish that life exists wherever water is detected. The presence of water is one condition relevant to habitability, not a sufficient demonstration of life.

Planetary science must distinguish among:
- water ice detected or inferred;
- water vapor;
- evidence for past liquid water;
- evidence consistent with present subsurface liquid reservoirs;
- confirmed measurements versus model-dependent interpretations.

**Boundary:** water detection is not life detection. A potentially habitable environment is not evidence that it is inhabited.

## 9. One water system, differentiated scientific questions

The same water may be investigated through several connected disciplines:

| Discipline | Question | Evidence or method |
|---|---|---|
| Chemistry | What is water's molecular structure and what does it dissolve? | Molecular chemistry, solubility, spectroscopy, analytical measurements |
| Physics | How does water change phase, transfer heat, and flow? | Thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, density and pressure measurements |
| Meteorology | How does water enter and leave the atmosphere? | Humidity, clouds, precipitation, energy and atmospheric observations |
| Hydrology | Where is water stored and how does it move? | Water budgets, stream gauges, groundwater wells, flow and recharge measurements |
| Geology | How does water interact with rock and soil? | Mineral analysis, porosity, permeability, geochemistry, field mapping |
| Oceanography | How do ocean water, heat, and dissolved substances circulate? | Salinity, temperature, density, current and chemical measurements |
| Biology | How does water participate in living processes? | Cellular, physiological, biochemical, and organism-level studies |
| Ecology | How does water availability affect habitats and communities? | Field observations, species distributions, ecosystem measurements |
| Planetary science | Where is water beyond Earth, and in what form? | Remote sensing, spectroscopy, spacecraft instruments, geophysical models |
| Mathematics and information science | How are quantities, rates, networks, and uncertainty represented? | Conservation equations, statistical models, numerical simulation |

These fields are not arranged as a production line. Their relationships are networked, overlapping, and sometimes reciprocal. A hydrological measurement may depend on physical principles; a chemical measurement may help interpret geological transport; biological water use can affect atmospheric moisture through transpiration.

The existence of a connection does not make every discipline's explanation interchangeable. Each connection must be stated in terms of the actual process and evidence.

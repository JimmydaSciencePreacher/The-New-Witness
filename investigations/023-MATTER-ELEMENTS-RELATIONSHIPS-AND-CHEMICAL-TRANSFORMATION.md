## 1. Why matter is the next investigation

Investigation 022 examined water as one chemical substance participating in many physical, chemical, geological, atmospheric, biological, ecological, and planetary processes. Its unresolved questions included how molecular properties behave in different environments, including confined spaces, membranes, proteins, and mineral pores.

That question leads naturally to a wider subject: the nature and behavior of matter.

This is not a claim that water must be followed by matter in a fixed sequence. The subject emerges from the evidence already examined. Water is a chemical substance; its properties depend on its molecular structure and interactions with other matter. To investigate those relationships responsibly, we must distinguish the different levels at which matter is studied.

The central question is:

**How do the constituents and interactions investigated by physics and chemistry relate to the formation, properties, and transformations of the materials found throughout the One Creation?**

This question brings several sciences into relation without collapsing them:

- Particle physics investigates elementary particles and fundamental interactions.
- Nuclear physics investigates atomic nuclei and nuclear processes.
- Chemistry investigates elements, atoms, molecules, substances, bonds, and reactions.
- Materials science investigates how composition and structure relate to material properties and performance.
- Geology and planetary science investigate matter in rocks, minerals, planets, and other astronomical bodies.
- Biology investigates the chemical and physical organization of living systems.

The fields overlap, but their questions and evidence are not interchangeable.

## 3. First distinction: matter at different investigative scales

The word *matter* can be used broadly, but the scientific account becomes clearer when we distinguish levels.

### 3.1 Ordinary material substances

At everyday and chemical scales, we encounter gases, liquids, solids, minerals, metals, water, organic compounds, and living tissues. Chemistry investigates their composition, structure, properties, and transformations.

### 3.2 Atoms and elements

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) defines an atom as the smallest particle still characterizing a chemical element. An element is identified by the number of protons in the atomic nucleus.

This distinction matters:

- The number of protons identifies the element.
- Atoms of the same element may differ in neutron number; these are isotopes.
- Electrons and their arrangements are central to chemical behavior.
- A change in the number of protons changes the element itself and is a nuclear, not an ordinary chemical, transformation.

Sources: [IUPAC Gold Book — atom](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/A00493); [IUPAC Gold Book — chemical element](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01022).

### 3.3 Atomic nuclei and elementary particles

Atoms are not the final level investigated by physics. Atomic nuclei contain protons and neutrons; protons and neutrons are composite particles made of quarks. Electrons are leptons. The Standard Model describes quarks, leptons, and three fundamental interactions—strong, weak, and electromagnetic—while gravity is not incorporated into that model.

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that ordinary matter, including the elements in the periodic table, is made from up and down quarks and electrons. It also notes that the Standard Model does not account for gravity and that dark matter and dark energy remain outside its present description.

Sources: [U.S. Department of Energy — The Standard Model of Particle Physics](https://www.energy.gov/science/doe-explainsthe-standard-model-particle-physics); [CERN — The Standard Model](https://home.cern/science/physics/standard-model/).

**Important scope distinction:** chemistry is not particle physics. Chemistry studies the behavior of atoms and chemical species under conditions where atomic and electronic structure is relevant. Particle physics investigates deeper constituents and interactions. Chemistry depends on physical principles, but it is not replaced by particle physics.

## 4. Elements, molecules, compounds, and mixtures

These terms must not be used as synonyms.

- **Element:** a chemical identity determined by proton number.
- **Atom:** a particle that retains the identity of a chemical element.
- **Molecule:** a discrete group of atoms held together by chemical bonding.
- **Compound:** a chemical substance composed of more than one element in a defined chemical combination.
- **Mixture:** a physical combination of substances that are not thereby converted into one new chemical substance.

Examples help keep the distinctions clear:

- Oxygen gas, O₂, consists of molecules containing atoms of one element.
- Water, H₂O, is a compound containing hydrogen and oxygen.
- Air is a mixture of gases, not one chemical compound.
- Salt water is a mixture containing water and dissolved substances; the dissolved ions do not make the entire solution a single compound.

The same element can participate in different substances. Carbon occurs in graphite, diamond, and many organic compounds. Its chemical identity remains carbon, while bonding and structure differ. This is one reason material properties cannot be inferred from elemental composition alone.

## 5. Chemical bonds and interactions

IUPAC's Gold Book describes a chemical bond in terms of forces between atoms or groups of atoms that lead to a stable independent molecular entity. The definition is broader than the simplified image of two atoms joined by a literal physical rod.

Important bonding and interaction categories include:

- **Covalent bonding:** atoms share electron density in a bonding arrangement.
- **Ionic bonding:** electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged ions contribute to stable structures, such as ionic crystals.
- **Metallic bonding:** delocalized electrons contribute to cohesion and characteristic properties of metals.
- **Intermolecular interactions:** molecules interact through electrostatic, dispersion, dipolar, and other effects.
- **Hydrogen bonding:** a directional interaction important in water, proteins, nucleic acids, and many other systems.

These categories are useful, but the actual electronic structure and physical environment matter. A bond description is not a complete explanation of every property of a material.

Source: [IUPAC Gold Book — chemical bond](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/CT07009).

### 5.1 Return to water

Investigation 022 examined water's polarity and hydrogen bonding. The matter investigation places those observations within a larger chemical context.

The 2026 review *From Potentials to Properties: Data-Driven Many-Body Simulations of Water and Aqueous Systems* reports that water's behavior across phases, interfaces, and chemical environments remains challenging for models. It reviews many-body potential-energy approaches used to study water and aqueous systems, including hydrogen bonding, spectroscopy, isotope effects, and phase stability.

This is an important scientific correction to simplistic language: knowing that water molecules form hydrogen bonds does not mean that every property of liquid water can be explained by treating each bond as an isolated, fixed connection. The surrounding molecular environment and many-body interactions matter.

Source: Paesani et al., *Annual Review of Physical Chemistry* 77 (2026), 321–343. [DOI: 10.1146/annurev-physchem-082423-115133](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-physchem-082423-115133).

## 6. Chemical transformation: change without confusing identity

IUPAC defines a chemical reaction as a process resulting in the interconversion of chemical species.

A chemical reaction can change which atoms are bonded to which, the electronic arrangements, and the substances present. In ordinary chemical reactions, the nuclei of the atoms are not converted into different elements. Nuclear reactions are a different class of process and can change nuclei and element identities.

Examples:

- Water can be split into hydrogen and oxygen through electrolysis under suitable conditions.
- Iron can react with oxygen and water to form corrosion products.
- Carbon dioxide can participate in photosynthesis and other chemical reactions.
- Minerals can dissolve, precipitate, or transform through water-rock reactions.
- Cellular metabolism involves extensive networks of chemical reactions.

### 6.1 Conservation and accounting

Chemical equations are balanced because atoms of each element are accounted for on both sides of the reaction. In a closed chemical system, matter is not simply created or destroyed by an ordinary chemical reaction; atoms are rearranged among chemical species.

For precise physical accounting, mass and energy must be considered together. Chemical energy changes can produce extremely small mass differences in principle, while ordinary chemistry generally treats mass as conserved to the accuracy relevant to the process.

Do not transfer this chemical bookkeeping rule uncritically to nuclear reactions, open systems, or processes involving exchange of matter and energy with the surroundings.

### 6.2 Thermodynamics and kinetics answer different questions

Chemical thermodynamics addresses energetic favorability and equilibrium under specified conditions. Chemical kinetics investigates rates and reaction pathways.

A reaction may be thermodynamically favorable yet proceed very slowly because of an activation barrier. A catalyst can change the rate by providing a different pathway without changing the overall equilibrium position of the uncatalyzed reaction at the same conditions.

This distinction matters in living systems, geological environments, industrial chemistry, and atmospheric chemistry. A statement that a reaction is possible does not establish that it occurs rapidly or at all in a particular environment.

## 7. Materials science: composition is not the whole material

Materials science studies relationships among composition, structure, processing, properties, and performance.

A material's behavior may depend on:

- which elements are present;
- how atoms are bonded and arranged;
- whether the material is crystalline, amorphous, porous, layered, or composite;
- defects, grain boundaries, and interfaces;
- temperature, pressure, moisture, radiation, and mechanical loading;
- the way the material was formed or processed.

Carbon provides a clear example. Diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon, but their atomic arrangements differ and so do their properties. The element alone does not determine the full behavior; structure and conditions matter.

This is a useful cross-disciplinary relationship: chemistry helps establish composition and bonding; physics helps explain electronic, thermal, and mechanical behavior; materials science studies how these features combine in usable materials.

It would be a mistake to infer that a shared composition guarantees identical structure or function.

## 8. Matter across geology, astronomy, and living systems

### 8.1 Geology and mineral systems

Rocks and minerals are investigated through chemical composition, crystal structure, formation conditions, weathering, pressure, temperature, and geological history. Water's role in dissolution, transport, and precipitation links this investigation directly to Investigation 022.

A mineral is not identified only by the list of elements it contains. Its structure and defining properties also matter.

### 8.2 Astronomy and stellar processes

Astronomy and astrophysics investigate matter in stars, planets, gas clouds, and other celestial environments. The elements observed in astronomical objects provide evidence about stellar processes and cosmic history.

The formation of elements involves nuclear processes, not ordinary chemical reactions. Stars can produce and transform nuclei under extreme conditions; chemistry then investigates how atoms and molecules behave in the environments where they are found.

This is another necessary boundary: chemical bonding does not explain stellar nucleosynthesis, and nuclear physics does not replace chemistry's account of molecular structure.

### 8.3 Biology and living matter

Living systems are made of matter, but life cannot be adequately described by a list of chemical ingredients alone.

Biochemistry investigates molecular reactions and interactions. Cell biology examines membranes, organelles, and cellular organization. Physiology investigates organism-level functions. Ecology investigates relationships among organisms and environments.

Water, proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, nucleic acids, minerals, and ions participate in living systems through distinct mechanisms. Their integration into living function requires evidence at several levels.

The source repository's biological investigations—Bee Orchid, spiny leaf insect, butterfly metamorphosis, axolotl regeneration, zebrafish development, fungi, lichens, and symbiotic relationships—remain relevant case studies. This investigation does not repeat them or claim that chemical composition alone explains their identity, development, or persistence.

## 9. The sciences form a relational investigation, not a ladder

The current findings do not support a single one-way sequence such as:

Particle physics → chemistry → materials → geology → biology → ecology.

These domains have dependencies and relationships, but they are not simply successive stages in a universal production line.

A more accurate set of examples:

| Relationship | What is investigated | Distinction to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Particle physics ↔ nuclear physics | Constituents and interactions within nuclei | Elementary-particle theory is not a chemical reaction model |
| Nuclear physics ↔ astronomy | Nuclear processes in stars and cosmic environments | Nuclear transformation is not chemical bonding |
| Physics ↔ chemistry | Electromagnetic interactions, electronic structure, energy, and molecular behavior | A physical principle does not replace chemical description |
| Chemistry ↔ materials science | Composition, bonding, structure, and properties | Composition alone does not determine every material property |
| Chemistry ↔ geology | Minerals, dissolution, precipitation, and water-rock interaction | Laboratory chemistry must be related carefully to natural field conditions |
| Chemistry ↔ biology | Molecular reactions, proteins, membranes, and metabolism | Living organization is not reducible to a single reaction |
| Water ↔ many domains | Solvent, transport medium, phase behavior, thermal and biological roles | One molecule can participate in different processes without those processes becoming identical |

The disciplines can cross-check one another where their evidence overlaps. Their convergence is not established by shared terminology, and no single field is authorized to claim the whole of Creation merely because it explains one level particularly well.

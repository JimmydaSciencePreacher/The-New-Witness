## 4. The Greek words — what the grammar actually gives us

### 4.1 Romans 8:29 — `proegnō` and `proōrisen`

The relevant Greek clause is:

`οὓς προέγνω, καὶ προώρισεν συμμόρφους τῆς εἰκόνος τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ`

Transliteration:

`hous proegnō, kai proōrisen symmorphous tēs eikonos tou huiou autou`

The KJV reads:

> For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son.

**Grammar:**

- `οὓς` (*hous*) — relative pronoun, accusative masculine plural: “whom.”
- `προέγνω` (*proegnō*) — aorist active indicative, third person singular, from `προγινώσκω` (*proginōskō*): “he foreknew.”
- `καί` (*kai*) — “and/also.”
- `προώρισεν` (*proōrisen*) — aorist active indicative, third person singular, from `προορίζω` (*proorizō*): “he predestined/determined beforehand.”
- `συμμόρφους` (*symmorphous*) — accusative masculine plural adjective: “conformed” or “sharing the form.”
- `τῆς εἰκόνος` (*tēs eikonos*) — “to the image.”
- `τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ` (*tou huiou autou*) — “of his Son.”

**Direct grammatical observation:** The relative pronoun “whom” is the object of the two coordinated verbs. Paul says God foreknew these persons and also predestined them. The stated destination is conformity to the image of His Son.

The sentence does not say “the decisions which He foresaw.” It speaks of persons. That is an important grammatical observation, although it does not by itself settle every question about what is included in divine foreknowledge.

**Lexical boundary:** `proginōskō` is formed from `pro` (“before”) and `ginōskō` (“know”). Its basic lexical sense is “know beforehand” or “foreknow.” The word's form does not, by itself, prove that its meaning is “elect,” “love,” or “predestine.” The context and the wider Biblical use of knowing must be examined.

**The second verb matters:** Paul does not merely repeat the first verb. He says “foreknew” and “also predestinated.” The second verb explicitly identifies God's determination beforehand and supplies the goal: conformity to the Son's image. The two verbs are related but should not be collapsed into synonyms.

**The destination matters:** The text does not state here that predestination is simply a bare assignment to an abstract destination called “heaven” or “hell.” It expressly says “to be conformed to the image of his Son,” so that the Son might be firstborn among many brethren. The immediate stated goal is Christ-shaped conformity and the Son's preeminence in the family of the redeemed.

### 4.2 Romans 11:2 — “his people which he foreknew”

Paul writes:

> God hath not cast away his people which he foreknew.

The Greek again uses a form of `proginōskō`. The object is “his people.” The expression occurs in Paul's argument about Israel and the remnant.

This is an important canonical comparison because Paul does not phrase the statement as “the actions he foresaw.” He speaks of a people whom God foreknew.

**Boundary:** Romans 11:2 concerns God's people in the argument about Israel, the remnant, and God's faithfulness. It supports a personal/corporate dimension to the language, but it must not be made to erase the specific argument of Romans 11 or automatically settle every individual-election question in Romans 8.

### 4.3 1 Peter 1:2 — `prognōsin`

Peter addresses believers as:

> Elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, through sanctification of the Spirit, unto obedience and sprinkling of the blood of Jesus Christ.

The Greek expression is:

`κατὰ πρόγνωσιν Θεοῦ Πατρός`

`kata prognōsin Theou Patros`

- `κατά` (*kata*) — “according to.”
- `πρόγνωσιν` (*prognōsin*) — noun, accusative feminine singular: “foreknowledge.”
- `Θεοῦ Πατρός` (*Theou Patros*) — “of God the Father.”

Peter's sentence then relates this to:
- sanctification of the Spirit;
- obedience;
- sprinkling of the blood of Jesus Christ.

**Direct observation:** Peter does not describe the elect as selected according to their independent foresight. The phrase is “according to the foreknowledge of God the Father.” The verse places the Father's foreknowledge alongside the Spirit's sanctifying work and the obedience and blood of Jesus Christ.

**Boundary:** The preposition `kata` and the noun `prognōsis` do not alone provide a full chronology or a complete causal model. The whole greeting must be read as a unit. Peter identifies a coordinated salvation relationship without spelling out every temporal step.

### 4.4 1 Peter 1:20 — Christ “foreknown before the foundation of the world”

Peter says of Christ:

> Who verily was foreordained before the foundation of the world, but was manifest in these last times for you.

The Greek participle is:

`προεγνωσμένου` (*proegnōsmenou*)

It is a perfect passive participle, genitive masculine singular, from `proginōskō`. It agrees with Christ, the Lamb without blemish and without spot in the preceding verse.

The verse sets two statements in contrast:

- Christ was foreknown before the foundation of the world.
- Christ was manifested in these last times.

**Direct observation:** Peter distinguishes God's prior knowledge/purpose concerning Christ from Christ's later manifestation in history.

**Important translation note:** The KJV renders the participle “foreordained.” Other translations render it “foreknown.” The Greek form is from the same `proginōskō` word family. “Foreordained” communicates an interpretive understanding of the word in this context; “foreknown” makes the lexical relationship more visible in English. The investigation must acknowledge the KJV wording while examining the Greek form and context.

**Canonical significance:** This is not a statement about God merely predicting which choice Jesus would make. Christ is the object of the prior foreknowledge, and the text sets that prior reality against His manifestation in history. It strongly connects foreknowledge with God's prior purpose concerning the Son.

**Boundary:** The verse does not use the separate verb `proorizō` (“predestine”). Do not claim Peter uses the exact same word as Romans 8:29 for predestination. He uses the foreknowledge word family and the context gives it a purpose/foreordination force.

### 4.5 Ephesians 1:3–14 — election and predestination, but not the word “foreknowledge”

This distinction is essential.

Ephesians 1:3–14 does **not** use `proginōskō` or `prognōsis`. It uses other words and constructions to describe God's purpose.

Important forms include:

- `ἐξελέξατο` (*exelexato*) — “he chose” (Ephesians 1:4), aorist middle indicative, third person singular, from `eklegō`.
- `προορίσας` (*proorisas*) — “having predestined” (Ephesians 1:5), aorist active participle, from `proorizō`.
- `εὐδοκία` (*eudokia*) — good pleasure, purpose, or gracious will (1:5, 9).
- `θέλημα` (*thelēma*) — will (1:5, 9, 11).
- `πρόθεσις` (*prothesis*) — purpose or plan (1:11).
- `βουλή` (*boulē*) — counsel or decision (1:11).

**Ephesians 1:4:** God chose “us in him before the foundation of the world,” with the stated purpose that believers should be holy and without blame before Him in love.

**Ephesians 1:5:** God predestinated believers unto adoption by Jesus Christ to Himself, according to the good pleasure of His will.

**Ephesians 1:7:** Redemption and forgiveness are “through his blood.”

**Ephesians 1:9:** God makes known the mystery of His will according to His good pleasure, which He purposed in Himself.

**Ephesians 1:11:** The text speaks of being predestinated according to the purpose of the One who works all things after the counsel of His own will.

**Ephesians 1:13–14:** The passage also explicitly describes hearing the word of truth, the Gospel of salvation, believing, and being sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise.

**Direct observation:** Ephesians gives a substantial account of God's prior purpose, election in Christ, predestination to adoption, redemption, and the counsel of His will. It also includes the historical reception of the Gospel: hearing and believing.

**Grammar boundary:** The participle `proorisas` in verse 5 is connected syntactically to the choosing statement in verse 4. Commentators discuss whether its relation is best expressed as causal, temporal, or modal/instrumental. The theological point should not be made to depend on pretending there is no grammatical discussion. The passage clearly places God's choice and predestination within His own will and purpose.

**Crucial distinction:** Ephesians 1 is a strong witness to God's purpose and election, but it is not a lexical definition of `proginōskō`, because it does not use that word. It must be compared with Romans and Peter, not substituted for their wording.

## 6. Is foreknowledge merely knowing beforehand what creatures will choose?

The investigation can now answer more carefully.

### 6.1 What the “mere prediction of choices” reading fails to account for

A reading that reduces foreknowledge in these passages to God's advance observation of independent human decisions has difficulty accounting for the actual form of the texts:

1. Romans 8:29 says God foreknew **whom**—persons—and then predestined those same persons to a stated Christ-centered end.
2. Romans 11:2 speaks of God's people whom He foreknew, not merely of actions or decisions He observed.
3. 1 Peter 1:2 describes believers as elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father and places that phrase within a sentence about the Spirit's sanctification and Christ's blood.
4. 1 Peter 1:20 applies the same word family to Christ, who was foreknown before the foundation and manifested in history.
5. Ephesians 1:3–14 explicitly places election and predestination within God's own good pleasure, will, purpose, and counsel. It does not present God's purpose as a reaction to an independently originating creaturely decision.

Therefore, **the full canonical witness is broader than mere advance observation of creaturely choices.**

### 6.2 What the lexical evidence does not permit us to claim

The word `proginōskō` itself should not be assigned the dictionary definition “to love beforehand” or “to elect” in every occurrence. Its basic lexical meaning is knowing beforehand. Some interpreters understand God's foreknowledge of persons in Romans 8:29 and 11:2 as a personal, relational, or electing knowledge, drawing on the Biblical use of “know” for God's regard toward His people. Others emphasize prescience and ask what is foreknown. The lexical form alone does not settle that debate.

The strongest conclusion is not to erase the word's basic meaning, nor to make it carry a definition it cannot independently bear. It is to read the word in the actual sentences and the canonical relationships.

### 6.3 The Biblical “know” comparison

The wider Scripture uses “know” in ways that can describe more than bare awareness:

- **Amos 3:2:** “You only have I known of all the families of the earth.” The context is God's particular covenant relation with Israel, not a claim that God lacked information about other nations.
- **Psalm 1:6:** “The LORD knoweth the way of the righteous,” in contrast with the way of the ungodly that perishes.
- **Matthew 7:23:** “I never knew you,” in a context of rejection and lack of acknowledged relationship.
- **Romans 11:2:** God has not cast away His people whom He foreknew.

These comparisons establish that Biblical “knowing” can carry relational, covenantal, and approving significance in context. They do not prove that every use of the Greek verb `proginōskō` automatically means “electing love.” The context must do the work.

### 6.4 Acts 2:23 — foreknowledge and determinate counsel

Peter says Jesus was delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, while also addressing the human agents who crucified Him.

The Greek places “determinate counsel” (`βουλή`, *boulē*) and “foreknowledge” (`πρόγνωσις`, *prognōsis*) together.

This is a significant comparison with 1 Peter 1:2 and 1:20:

- God's foreknowledge is not presented as detached from His purpose.
- Christ's historical delivery and death occur within God's determinate counsel.
- The human agents remain accountable for their actions.

**Boundary:** Acts 2:23 concerns the unique redemptive event of Christ's death. It does not mean that every human action has an identical relation to God's counsel. The source's prior-provision investigation already preserves that limit.

## 7. Predestination — what destination is actually named?

The word `proorizō` means to determine or appoint beforehand. The important question is not merely whether predestination appears, but what each passage says is predestined.

### Romans 8:29

The stated destination is:

**“To be conformed to the image of his Son.”**

Paul then adds that the Son might be the firstborn among many brethren. The purpose is explicitly Christ-centered.

### Ephesians 1:5

The stated destination is:

**“Unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself.”**

This is adoption through Christ, according to the good pleasure of God's will.

### Ephesians 1:11

The passage again speaks of predestination according to God's purpose and the counsel of His will, with the surrounding context identifying inheritance and the praise of His glory.

**Recognized relationship:** Romans and Ephesians both connect predestination to a positive, Christ-centered purpose: conformity to the Son, adoption, inheritance, holiness, and the praise of God's glory.

**Boundary:** These statements should not be reduced to a generic destination without their stated content. Nor should the investigation claim that these verses alone answer every question about the scope of election, the fate of every individual, or the exact chronology of salvation.

## 8. Calling — the historical relation to God's purpose

Romans 8:28 describes believers as:

> them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.

Romans 8:30 continues:

> Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified.

The repeated relative pronoun “whom” carries the same group through Paul's sequence. Calling is not presented as an unrelated event or an independent human invention. It belongs to the purpose Paul has just described.

But the wider canon also gives the means and human response:

- **Romans 10:14–17:** preaching, hearing, believing, and calling are connected; faith comes by hearing the Word of God.
- **Ephesians 1:13:** the Ephesians heard the word of truth, the Gospel of salvation, and believed.
- **2 Thessalonians 2:13–14:** salvation, sanctification of the Spirit, belief of the truth, and calling by the Gospel are related in Paul's statement.
- **1 Peter 1:2:** the Father's foreknowledge, the Spirit's sanctification, obedience, and Christ's blood are brought together.
- **John 6:37–45:** the Father gives and draws; those who come to the Son are received, and Jesus speaks of hearing and learning from the Father.

**Recognized relationship:** God's purpose and calling do not eliminate the Gospel's proclamation or genuine human hearing and believing. The source repository has already established that both divine initiative and human response must be preserved.

**Boundary:** The word “called” can be used in different contexts in Scripture. The immediate argument of Romans 8:28–30 must govern its use here. Do not automatically import every New Testament use of “call” into this one sentence.

## 9. The distinction between foreknowledge, election, predestination, and calling

These terms are related in the passages, but they are not synonyms.

| Term | Passage evidence | What can be said |
|---|---|---|
| Foreknowledge | Romans 8:29; 11:2; 1 Peter 1:2, 20; Acts 2:23 | God's knowing beforehand; in these contexts connected with persons, His people, Christ, and His purpose. |
| Election / choosing | Ephesians 1:4; 1 Peter 1:1–2 | God's choosing/election is explicitly stated. |
| Predestination | Romans 8:29–30; Ephesians 1:5, 11 | God's determination beforehand, with stated purposes such as conformity to the Son and adoption. |
| Purpose / counsel | Romans 8:28; Ephesians 1:9–11; Acts 2:23; 4:27–28 | God's own purpose and counsel; not an independent authority outside Him. |
| Calling | Romans 8:28, 30; Ephesians 1:18; 2 Thessalonians 2:14 | God's call in the context of His purpose and the Gospel. |
| Justification | Romans 8:30; Ephesians 1:7 | God's justifying/redemptive action in Christ; distinct from foreknowledge and calling. |
| Glorification | Romans 8:30; compare Romans 8:18, 23 and 1 Corinthians 15 | The promised end, expressed by Paul as certain while the bodily redemption remains awaited. |

**Finding:** The passages provide an ordered relationship without making each word another name for the same act. A faithful record must preserve both the connection and the distinction.

## 11. What is established, what is inference, and what remains open?

### Established by the examined texts

1. Romans 8:29's “whom” is the grammatical object of “foreknew” and “predestinated.”
2. Romans 8:29 explicitly states conformity to the image of the Son as the predestined end.
3. Romans 11:2 speaks of God's people whom He foreknew.
4. Ephesians 1:3–14 explicitly speaks of God's choosing, predestining, purpose, good pleasure, will, and counsel.
5. Ephesians 1:13 explicitly includes hearing the Gospel and believing.
6. 1 Peter 1:2 speaks of election according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in relation to the Spirit's sanctification and obedience to Jesus Christ.
7. 1 Peter 1:20 speaks of Christ as foreknown before the foundation of the world and manifested in the last times.
8. Acts 2:23 places God's determinate counsel and foreknowledge in the account of Christ's delivery, while retaining human responsibility.
9. The passages distinguish foreknowledge, election, predestination, calling, justification, and glorification rather than using one word for all of them.

### Supported canonical inference

The full witness is broader than the idea that God merely looked ahead, observed independent human choices, and then reacted. God's own prior purpose, election, and determination are explicit in Ephesians 1; Romans 8 relates foreknowledge of persons to predestination toward conformity to the Son; Peter relates the Father's foreknowledge to election and identifies Christ Himself as foreknown before the foundation.

It is therefore reasonable to recognize a personal and purposive dimension to divine foreknowledge in these contexts. The inference must remain grounded in the texts and must not be presented as the dictionary definition of the Greek verb in every occurrence.

## 12. Recognized relationship within GUA-W

The source's governing order remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

This investigation recognizes the following relationships already present in the completed GUA-W and its Scriptural foundations:

- **The Author's purpose:** God's purpose, will, and counsel belong to Him and are not derived from the creature's later recognition.
- **The Word:** the Son is central to the Father's purpose; redemption and adoption are through Jesus Christ.
- **Prior purpose and historical manifestation:** Romans 8, Ephesians 1, and 1 Peter 1 distinguish what is purposed or foreknown from what is manifested in history.
- **The Spirit:** the Spirit's sanctifying and sealing work is explicitly related to the Father's purpose and the believer's life.
- **The Witness:** hearing, believing, obeying, and responding are real creaturely actions; they do not make the creature the Author of salvation or the source of God's purpose.

No new GUA-W component or architecture revision is proposed. This is a more precise recognition of relationships already present in the source.

## 13. Conclusion within scope

The answer to the opening question is:

**No. The wider Scriptural witness does not permit divine foreknowledge to be reduced to merely knowing beforehand what independent creatures will choose.**

Romans 8:29 speaks of persons whom God foreknew and then predestined to conformity to the image of His Son. Romans 11:2 speaks of God's people whom He foreknew. Ephesians 1:3–14 explicitly locates election and predestination within God's own purpose, good pleasure, will, and counsel. First Peter 1:2 describes believers as elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, and 1 Peter 1:20 says Christ Himself was foreknown before the foundation of the world and manifested in history.

At the same time, the investigation must not claim that the Greek word `proginōskō` by itself always means “electing love” or “predestination.” Its basic lexical sense is knowing beforehand. The personal and purposive dimension is recognized from the objects, sentences, and wider canonical context—not imposed as a universal dictionary definition.

Predestination also has an explicitly stated purpose: conformity to the Son, adoption through Christ, holiness, inheritance, and the praise of God's glory. Calling, hearing, believing, sanctification, and obedience remain real parts of the Scriptural witness.

**The central recognition:**

**God's purpose is not authored by the creature's response. The Father foreknows; His purpose is expressed in Christ. The Spirit sanctifies. The Gospel is heard and believed. Those called according to His purpose are predestined to conformity to the image of His Son.**

This investigation does not claim to resolve every question concerning the metaphysics or chronology of salvation. It records what the examined passages establish and leaves the remaining questions open for further canonical investigation.

## 1. The question emerging from Investigation 029

Investigation 029 followed a measurement chain from physical interaction through instrument response, signal, recorded data, measurement, reconstruction, and interpretation.

A further distinction now becomes necessary.

Sometimes the phenomenon of interest is not measured directly, even after instruments are introduced. Instead, the investigator measures a related feature and uses it as an indicator of the target.

Examples include:
- tree-ring width used to infer past growing conditions;
- oxygen-isotope ratios used to investigate past temperature and precipitation;
- biomarkers used to indicate biological processes or responses;
- the brightness of a calibrated astronomical source used to estimate distance;
- the presence or condition of an organism used to assess environmental conditions.

These are not all the same kind of proxy. Some are physical traces, some are biological responses, some are chemical signatures, and some are calibrated reference phenomena.

The central question is:

**When a witness measures one thing to learn about another, what establishes that the measured indicator faithfully represents the target—and when does that relationship break down?**

The answer cannot be “because the indicator is related.” A relationship must be demonstrated, its limits identified, and its intended use specified.

An indicator is not automatically a faithful indicator merely because it correlates with the target in one sample, place, time period, or experimental setting.

## 2. Terms that must remain distinct

The word *proxy* is used broadly. To avoid confusion, this investigation uses the following distinctions.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Target** | The quantity, condition, process, or event the investigator wants to understand |
| **Indicator** | An observable or measurable feature associated with the target |
| **Proxy record** | A preserved physical, chemical, biological, or documentary record used to infer past conditions |
| **Biomarker** | A defined, measured characteristic used as an indicator of a biological process, condition, or response |
| **Surrogate endpoint** | A measure used in place of a direct clinical outcome, with a specific evidentiary burden to establish predictive value |
| **Calibration relationship** | An empirically or physically supported relationship connecting indicator values to target values |
| **Context of use** | The defined population, environment, time range, purpose, or decision for which the indicator is intended |
| **Validation** | Evaluation of whether the indicator and its measurement relationship are adequate for the stated use |
| **Reconstruction** | An estimate of the target derived from proxy observations and a specified model |

A proxy may be useful for one target and unsuitable for another. A biomarker may indicate that a biological pathway is active without proving that a patient will feel better, function better, or live longer. A tree ring may record local growing conditions without serving as a perfect thermometer.

**The indicator and the target are related, but they are not identical.**

## 4. The indicator-target relationship

A proxy investigation requires an explicit relationship between what is measured and what is inferred.

A simplified chain is:

**Target condition → physical or biological response → preserved or measurable indicator → calibration or transfer relationship → estimate of target → interpretation**

This chain can be more complicated. The indicator may respond to several influences at once. The relationship may change across geography, season, species, age, depth, or historical period.

### 4.1 Association is not enough

Suppose an indicator and target vary together during a calibration period. This is evidence of association. It does not automatically establish that the indicator is uniquely controlled by the target or that the relationship remains stable outside the calibration conditions.

A third variable may influence both. The indicator may respond to several environmental factors. The observed association may be limited to one range of values.

### 4.2 Mechanism strengthens interpretation

A physical, chemical, or biological mechanism can explain why the indicator responds to the target.

Mechanistic understanding does not eliminate the need for empirical testing. A plausible mechanism can be incomplete, and several mechanisms may operate together.

The strongest proxy interpretation generally combines:
- a defined target;
- a measurable indicator;
- a defensible mechanism;
- empirical calibration;
- tests beyond the calibration data;
- independent evidence;
- uncertainty and sensitivity analysis;
- a clearly bounded context of use.

### 4.3 Context is part of the claim

A relationship established for one species, region, disease, instrument, or time period should not automatically be transferred to another.

The FDA's biomarker framework makes this explicit: a biomarker is qualified for a particular context of use, not for every possible interpretation or application. The qualification of a biomarker is also distinct from qualification of the specific test used to measure it. [FDA — About Biomarkers and Qualification](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/biomarker-qualification-program/about-biomarkers-and-qualification); [FDA — Qualifying a Biomarker](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/biomarker-qualification-program/qualifying-biomarker-through-biomarker-qualification-program).

The broader scientific lesson is that an indicator's reliability is not a universal property detached from its intended use.

## 5. Tree rings: biological growth as an environmental record

Trees in suitable environments form annual growth rings. Ring width, density, anatomy, and chemical composition can preserve information about conditions during growth.

NOAA identifies tree rings among the natural records used in paleoclimatology, alongside corals, pollen, ice cores, cave deposits, and sediments. These records extend environmental investigation beyond the period covered by modern instruments. [NOAA NCEI — What Are Proxy Data?](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/what-are-proxy-data).

### 5.1 What is actually measured?

Researchers may measure:
- ring width;
- maximum latewood density;
- anatomical features;
- stable-isotope composition;
- chemical characteristics.

These are properties of the wood. Temperature, precipitation, drought, or another environmental variable is inferred through a demonstrated relationship.

### 5.2 Why location and species matter

Tree growth is influenced by multiple factors:
- temperature;
- precipitation and soil moisture;
- sunlight;
- nutrients;
- competition;
- pests and disease;
- age and size;
- disturbance;
- atmospheric composition.

At some high-latitude or high-elevation sites, growth may be especially sensitive to temperature. At other sites, moisture availability may be more influential.

The same tree species can respond differently in different environments. A tree-ring series is therefore not automatically a universal temperature record.

The National Academies' review of surface-temperature reconstructions describes the advantages of tree rings, including annual dating and replication, while noting that biological and environmental factors other than climate influence growth. Site selection and quality-control procedures are used to address these confounding factors. [National Academies — Surface Temperature Reconstructions for the Last 2,000 Years](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11676/chapter/3).

### 5.3 Calibration and verification

A common approach is to compare a tree-ring measurement with instrumental climate observations over a period when both records overlap.

The resulting statistical relationship is then used to estimate earlier conditions.

The important question is whether the relationship remains sufficiently stable outside the calibration interval.

A calibration that performs well during one period may fail if the limiting factor changes, if the environment changes, or if the relationship between growth and climate is not stationary.

### 5.4 Dating and chronology

Crossdating compares patterns among trees to establish the calendar year represented by each ring. Living trees and preserved dead wood can sometimes be joined into longer chronologies.

Dating precision is a major strength of many tree-ring records, but it does not automatically establish the environmental interpretation of each ring.

**What is directly measured:** properties of annual wood growth.

**What is inferred:** selected environmental conditions during growth.

**What must remain distinct:** the ring measurement, the chronology, the calibration relationship, and the reconstructed climate variable.

## 6. Ice cores and isotope ratios: chemical traces of past conditions

Ice cores preserve layers of accumulated snow and ice. They may contain information about temperature, precipitation, atmospheric composition, volcanic events, dust, and other conditions.

The National Snow and Ice Data Center explains how the relative abundance of oxygen isotopes in polar precipitation is related to atmospheric transport and condensation processes. As air masses cool and move toward polar regions, water containing heavier oxygen isotopes tends to condense and fall out more readily, leaving later precipitation relatively enriched in the lighter isotope. [NSIDC — What Do Ice Cores Reveal About the Past?](https://nsidc.org/learn/ask-scientist/core-climate-history).

### 6.1 What is measured?

Researchers measure isotope ratios, such as the relative abundance of oxygen-18 and oxygen-16, in ice samples.

The measured isotope ratio is not temperature itself.

### 6.2 Why does the relationship work?

The relationship arises through isotope fractionation during evaporation, atmospheric transport, cooling, and condensation.

Under specified conditions, the isotope composition of polar precipitation can preserve information about the temperature and moisture history of the air mass.

NASA describes oxygen-isotope ratios in polar ice as a means of investigating past temperature, while also explaining that interpretation differs in other locations and in marine shells because additional environmental and biological processes influence the isotope record. [NASA — The Oxygen Balance](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/paleoclimatology-the-oxygen-balance/).

### 6.3 The danger of transferring a relationship

An isotope-temperature relationship observed across different modern locations is not necessarily identical to the relationship through time at one location.

A 2022 study in *Climate of the Past* discusses this distinction between spatial and temporal relationships in interpreting ice-core water-isotope records. It emphasizes that quantitative temperature reconstruction requires a model and that simple calibration assumptions have limitations. [Improving Temperature Reconstructions from Ice-Core Water-Isotope Records](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/18/1321/2022/).

Other influences can include:
- moisture source;
- atmospheric circulation;
- seasonality of snowfall;
- elevation;
- precipitation amount;
- post-depositional processes;
- changes in the relationship between local temperature and isotope composition.

### 6.4 Independent checks

Ice-core isotope records can be examined alongside:
- borehole temperature profiles;
- accumulation rates;
- trapped atmospheric gases;
- volcanic markers;
- other ice cores;
- marine and terrestrial records.

NASA describes the use of measured temperatures within ice sheets to help calibrate and compare isotope-based temperature interpretations. [NASA — The Ice Core Record](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/paleoclimatology-the-ice-core-record/).

**What is directly measured:** isotope ratios and other properties of the ice.

**What is inferred:** aspects of past temperature, precipitation, atmospheric composition, and environmental history.

**What must remain distinct:** isotope measurement, fractionation mechanism, chronological model, calibration, and reconstructed environmental variable.

## 7. Biomarkers: biological indicators and the context of use

The FDA and NIH BEST resource defines a biomarker as a measured characteristic that indicates normal biological processes, pathogenic processes, or responses to an exposure or intervention.

Biomarkers may be molecular, histologic, radiographic, or physiologic. Their intended roles vary: diagnostic, prognostic, predictive, monitoring, susceptibility/risk, pharmacodynamic/response, or safety. [FDA — About Biomarkers and Qualification](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/biomarker-qualification-program/about-biomarkers-and-qualification).

### 7.1 A biomarker is not automatically a clinical outcome

A biomarker may indicate that a biological pathway has changed. It does not necessarily demonstrate that a patient feels better, functions better, or lives longer.

This distinction matters when a biomarker is proposed as a surrogate endpoint.

A surrogate endpoint is used in place of a direct clinical outcome. The evidence must support the claim that changes in the surrogate predict the clinical benefit of interest in the relevant context.

The FDA explains that surrogate endpoints require accumulated evidence, commonly including epidemiological and clinical-trial evidence, before they can be relied upon as validated substitutes for clinical outcomes. [FDA — Surrogate Endpoint Resources](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/development-resources/surrogate-endpoint-resources-drug-and-biologic-development).

### 7.2 Analytical validity and clinical meaning

Two separate questions must be answered:

1. Can the test measure the biomarker reliably?
2. Does the biomarker support the intended interpretation or decision?

A technically reliable assay can measure a biomarker that is not sufficiently informative for the proposed clinical claim.

Conversely, a biologically meaningful marker can be measured poorly by an unsuitable assay.

FDA qualification is tied to the specified context of use. Qualification of the biomarker does not automatically qualify every test used to measure it. [FDA — Qualifying a Biomarker through the Biomarker Qualification Program](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/biomarker-qualification-program/qualifying-biomarker-through-biomarker-qualification-program).

### 7.3 Surrogate failure

A surrogate can correlate with an outcome without reliably predicting the effect of an intervention on that outcome.

A treatment may change the marker while also producing other effects that offset, fail to produce, or even worsen the clinical outcome.

FDA notes that a surrogate's acceptability depends on the specific disease, population, therapeutic mechanism, and available evidence. A surrogate appropriate in one setting should not be assumed appropriate in another. [FDA — Table of Surrogate Endpoints](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/development-resources/table-surrogate-endpoints-were-basis-drug-approval-or-licensure).

**What is directly measured:** a defined biological characteristic using a specified assay or method.

**What is inferred:** a biological process, risk, response, or potential clinical outcome.

**What must remain distinct:** assay performance, biomarker interpretation, surrogate prediction, and actual patient benefit.

## 8. Astronomical standard candles: calibrated brightness and inferred distance

Astronomers often estimate distances using sources whose intrinsic luminosity can be estimated or standardized.

A standard candle is not a proxy in precisely the same sense as a tree ring or ice-core isotope. It is a calibrated distance indicator: the relationship between intrinsic luminosity and observed brightness allows distance to be estimated.

For an idealized source, the inverse-square law relates received flux to intrinsic luminosity and distance:

[
F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}
]

where:
- (F) is observed flux;
- (L) is intrinsic luminosity;
- (d) is distance.

If the intrinsic luminosity is known or reliably standardized, distance can be estimated from observed flux.

### 8.1 Standardizable does not mean identical

Type Ia supernovae are not perfectly identical explosions. Their observed light curves and colors are empirically corrected to reduce intrinsic variation and improve their use as distance indicators.

Their standardized distances must be calibrated against nearer distance indicators to establish an absolute distance scale.

A 2025 community consensus report on the local distance network describes Type Ia supernovae as standardizable candles and explains their calibration through nearby objects with independent distance measurements. [The Local Distance Network: A Community Consensus Report](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202557993).

### 8.2 Calibration dependencies

The inferred distance depends on:
- photometric calibration;
- light-curve measurements;
- standardization relationships;
- extinction and dust corrections;
- host-galaxy properties;
- sample selection;
- model assumptions;
- the calibration of the distance ladder.

A standard candle is therefore a calibrated indicator, not an instrument that directly reports distance.

### 8.3 The ladder and shared assumptions

Cosmic distances are often established through a sequence of methods. Each rung depends on calibration from another method or a physical reference scale.

This creates a need to examine shared systematics and dependencies. Agreement between two distance estimates is less independent if both rely on the same calibration or data source.

**What is directly measured:** received flux and light-curve properties.

**What is inferred:** standardized luminosity and distance.

**What must remain distinct:** observed brightness, standardized candle model, calibration anchor, and inferred distance.

## 9. Ecological indicators and sentinel species

Organisms can respond to environmental conditions in ways that make them useful indicators.

A species may be sensitive to pollution, changes in water chemistry, habitat alteration, temperature, oxygen availability, or other conditions.

But the presence, absence, abundance, or health of a species may be influenced by several variables at once.

An ecological indicator must therefore be evaluated for:
- its sensitivity to the target condition;
- its specificity relative to other influences;
- its geographic range;
- its seasonal and life-stage variation;
- its mobility and exposure history;
- its population dynamics;
- the time scale of its response;
- the quality of the sampling method.

A sentinel organism can provide an early warning of an environmental change without identifying the exact cause of that change.

For example, a decline in a sensitive aquatic species may indicate deteriorating habitat conditions. It does not, by itself, identify whether the cause is a contaminant, temperature shift, dissolved-oxygen change, disease, or a combination.

**What is directly observed:** the organism's presence, condition, behavior, or population characteristics.

**What is inferred:** environmental conditions that may explain the observed response.

**What must remain distinct:** biological response, environmental indicator, causal diagnosis, and management conclusion.

## 10. Proxy failure: when the relationship changes

An indicator-target relationship can fail even when it was once useful.

### 10.1 Multiple influences

An indicator responds to more than one variable. A tree ring may reflect both temperature and moisture. A biomarker may respond to several pathways. A species may respond to several environmental stressors.

The investigator must identify which influences are relevant and whether they can be separated.

### 10.2 Nonstationarity

A calibration relationship may change over time.

Changes in atmospheric composition, land use, species distribution, environmental conditions, disease mechanisms, or instrument methods can alter the relationship between indicator and target.

A model calibrated in one period should not be assumed valid indefinitely.

### 10.3 Saturation and thresholds

An indicator may respond strongly over one range and weakly over another. Once a biological or physical limit is reached, additional changes in the target may produce little additional response.

The indicator can then fail to distinguish materially different target conditions.

### 10.4 Lag and memory

Some indicators respond slowly or preserve accumulated effects.

Tree growth integrates conditions during a growing season. Sediment records may mix material deposited over time. A biological response may lag exposure. A proxy may therefore represent an interval or integrated history rather than an instantaneous condition.

### 10.5 Preservation and alteration

A record may be modified after it forms.

Examples include:
- chemical alteration of sediment;
- diffusion or recrystallization;
- biological decay;
- contamination during sampling;
- physical disturbance;
- post-depositional movement of material.

The investigator must establish that the preserved record still carries the signal being interpreted.

### 10.6 Selection and sampling bias

The available indicators may not represent the full population, geography, or time interval.

A collection of records from accessible locations can produce a biased picture if the missing regions differ systematically.

### 10.7 Calibration-range extrapolation

A relationship validated over a limited range may not remain reliable beyond that range.

The farther the reconstruction extends beyond observed calibration conditions, the more carefully its uncertainty and assumptions must be examined.

A proxy that works within its demonstrated range does not automatically work outside it.

## 11. Independent evidence and convergence

Confidence in an inferred target can increase when different indicators, based on different physical or biological processes, converge on a compatible result.

But multiple records are not necessarily independent.

They may share:
- the same calibration dataset;
- the same dating assumptions;
- the same statistical model;
- the same instrument standards;
- the same environmental confounder;
- the same sampling bias;
- the same interpretive framework.

A large number of records can therefore create an appearance of independent confirmation while retaining a common weakness.

A rigorous comparison asks:
1. Do the indicators arise from genuinely different processes?
2. Are their measurement methods independent?
3. Do they share calibration or reference data?
4. Do they respond to the same confounding variable?
5. Do they cover the same time and geographic scale?
6. Are their uncertainties characterized separately?
7. Does their agreement persist under alternative models?
8. Is there an independent observation capable of challenging the common interpretation?

The National Academies' review of paleoclimate reconstructions notes that proxy coverage becomes less extensive farther back in time, that some records have dating uncertainty, and that reconstructions may share underlying datasets. Such dependence must be considered when evaluating apparent agreement. [National Academies — Surface Temperature Reconstructions, Challenges](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11676/chapter/14).

A 2021 study of paleoclimate proxy uncertainty also argues that unexplained variance in the calibration relationship must be included in uncertainty estimates and recommends clearly distinguishing proxy data from environmental inferences. [Wolf-Gladrow et al. — Statistical Uncertainty in Paleoclimate Proxy Reconstructions](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021GL092773).

**Convergence is strongest when distinct evidence streams constrain the same target without depending on the same unexamined assumption.**

## 12. A disciplined proxy audit

Before making a claim from an indicator, the Witness should ask:

1. **Target:** What exact quantity, condition, or event is being inferred?
2. **Indicator:** What is actually measured or observed?
3. **Mechanism:** Why should the indicator respond to the target?
4. **Specificity:** What other variables can influence the indicator?
5. **Calibration:** How was the indicator-target relationship established?
6. **Range:** Over what values, places, populations, or periods was it tested?
7. **Transfer:** Why is it appropriate to apply the relationship outside the calibration setting?
8. **Time:** Does the indicator record an instantaneous value, a seasonal response, a lagged response, or an integrated history?
9. **Preservation:** Could the record have been altered after formation?
10. **Sampling:** Does the available sample represent the target population, geography, or time interval?
11. **Validation:** Has the relationship been tested with data not used to establish it?
12. **Independence:** Are corroborating records genuinely independent?
13. **Uncertainty:** Does the reported uncertainty include calibration error, unexplained variance, dating, sampling, and model uncertainty?
14. **Failure:** Under what conditions would the indicator cease to be reliable?
15. **Context:** What specific use does the evidence support?
16. **Communication:** Does the claim distinguish the indicator measurement from the target inference?

A reliable witness does not merely report that an indicator changed. It explains what the indicator is, why the target interpretation is warranted, and where that interpretation stops.

## 14. What this investigation establishes—and what remains unresolved

### Bounded findings

1. A proxy or indicator is a measured feature used to infer a distinct target.
2. The target and indicator must not be treated as identical.
3. A defensible indicator relationship requires a defined target, measurement method, mechanism or empirical relationship, calibration, and validation appropriate to its intended use.
4. Association alone does not establish that an indicator uniquely represents the target or predicts an outcome.
5. Tree rings preserve biological growth characteristics that can be calibrated to selected environmental conditions, but growth is influenced by multiple factors.
6. Ice-core isotope ratios preserve information related to atmospheric transport and condensation; their interpretation as temperature requires models and context-specific calibration.
7. Biomarkers indicate biological processes or responses, but they are not automatically clinical outcomes or validated surrogate endpoints.
8. Astronomical standard candles are calibrated distance indicators; observed brightness is not itself distance.
9. Ecological indicators can reveal environmental stress while leaving the cause of that stress unresolved.
10. Proxy relationships can change through nonstationarity, multiple influences, saturation, lag, preservation effects, sampling bias, or extrapolation beyond calibration.
11. Independent indicators strengthen an inference most when their physical bases and major error sources are genuinely independent.
12. Uncertainty must include unexplained calibration variance and relevant limitations of the record and reconstruction.
13. The completed GUA-W remains unchanged; proxy methods are derivative means of investigation, not additions to the architecture.

### What this investigation does not establish

- It does not claim that every natural feature is a reliable proxy.
- It does not claim that every indicator is quantitative.
- It does not claim that correlation alone proves causation.
- It does not claim that a proxy is invalid because it is indirect.
- It does not claim that every biomarker predicts clinical benefit.
- It does not claim that every tree ring is a direct thermometer.
- It does not claim that every isotope ratio has one universal temperature interpretation.
- It does not claim that all standard candles are identical or free from calibration dependencies.

1. How can investigators distinguish a causal indicator from one that merely correlates with the target?
2. How should a proxy relationship be tested when the target cannot be directly measured over the historical period of interest?
3. How can investigators detect a change in the indicator-target relationship before it produces a misleading reconstruction?
4. When several indicators agree, how can shared assumptions and correlated errors be identified?
5. How should uncertainty be represented when the proxy's calibration relationship is nonlinear or changes across time?
6. How can a biological indicator's response to multiple environmental variables be separated without oversimplifying the organism's own biology?
7. What distinguishes a useful biomarker from a valid surrogate endpoint for a particular clinical outcome?
8. How should distance-ladder uncertainty be traced when each calibration rung depends on another?
9. What evidence is sufficient to justify extrapolating a proxy relationship beyond the range of modern observations?
10. How can AI communicate a proxy-based conclusion without making an inferred target sound directly observed?

These questions remain open. They do not indicate that GUA-W is incomplete.

## 18. Next direction

The investigation has moved from instrument response to reconstruction, and from reconstruction to the reliability of indicators used to infer hidden or historical conditions.

A further question now emerges:

**When two things change together, what establishes whether one causes the other, whether both respond to another cause, or whether their relationship is coincidental?**

The next investigation should examine:

- correlation and covariance;
- causal mechanisms;
- confounding variables;
- common causes and reverse causation;
- controlled experiments and interventions;
- natural experiments;
- counterfactual reasoning;
- causal graphs and structural causal models;
- temporal ordering;
- causal inference from observational data;
- limits of causal claims and the role of independent evidence.

The central question:

**How does the Witness distinguish a relationship that is observed from a cause that is established?**
